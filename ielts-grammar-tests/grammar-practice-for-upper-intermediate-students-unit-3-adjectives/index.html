Grammar Practice for Upper Intermediate Students Unit 3 – Adjectives

Choose or type the answer to each question, then press Check to see if you are right.
  1. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: The food at my cousin's wedding ___ (wonderful / was / absolutely).
    Answer: was absolutely wonderful
    Wonderful is a predicative adjective here, so it comes after the verb was. Absolutely is an adverb that goes straight before the strong adjective it makes stronger.
  2. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: At the zoo, the kids couldn't stop staring at ___ (gray / old / an / enormous) elephant.
    Accepted answers: an enormous old gray / an enormous old grey
    Adjectives before a noun follow the order size, then age, then color: enormous old gray. An goes first and matches the vowel sound of enormous.
  3. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: After the long night shift, the nurse ___ (pale / looked / rather / and / tired).
    Accepted answers: looked rather pale and tired / looked rather tired and pale
    After a verb like look, two predicative adjectives are joined with and. Rather goes before the adjectives it describes.
  4. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: Grandpa still drives ___ (sports / old / a / gorgeous) car from the 1960s.
    Answer: a gorgeous old sports
    The order is opinion (gorgeous), then age (old), then type (sports), and the type word goes right before the noun.
  5. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: The new manager turned out to be ___ (cold / young / a / demanding) woman.
    Accepted answers: a cold, demanding young / a demanding, cold young
    Cold and demanding both say what the person is like, so they come first, in either order, with a comma between them. The age adjective young goes next to the noun.
  6. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: Since he got his new phone, Ethan has become ___ (user / a / heavy / very) of social media.
    Answer: a very heavy user
    Heavy goes before the noun here and means using something a lot. Very comes before the adjective, and the article a comes first.
  7. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: Winning the scholarship ___ (proud / her / made / parents / very).
    Answer: made her parents very proud
    The pattern is make + person + adjective: the adjective proud comes after the person it describes, with very before it.
  8. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: The museum's most famous piece is ___ (wooden / an / heavy / ornate) chest from the 1700s.
    Accepted answers: an ornate, heavy wooden / an ornate heavy wooden
    The adjective saying what the chest is like (ornate) comes first, then heavy, and the material wooden goes right before the noun. An matches the vowel sound of ornate.
  9. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: For decades, the two soda companies have been ___ (rivals / bitter / extremely).
    Answer: extremely bitter rivals
    Bitter goes before the noun rivals, and the adverb extremely goes before the adjective it makes stronger.
  10. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: Your brother ___ (quiet / seems / rather) today. Is everything OK?
    Answer: seems rather quiet
    Quiet is a predicative adjective after the verb seem, and rather goes before the adjective.
  11. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: The kids' swimming coach is ___ (patient / young / a / very) guy who never raises his voice.
    Answer: a very patient young
    The opinion adjective patient, with very before it, comes before the age adjective young, which stays next to the noun.
  12. Choose the correct word order and punctuation: Our new science teacher is ___
    When three adjectives come after the verb, we put a comma between the first two and and before the last one. Predicative adjectives can't simply stand in a row without commas or and.
  13. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: In the fall, the leaves on the maple trees ___ (red / to / begin / turn).
    Answer: begin to turn red
    Turn can be followed by an adjective to show a change: turn red. Begin to comes before turn.
  14. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: The town's oldest bakery is run by ___ (and / young / cheerful / friendly / a) couple.
    Accepted answers: a cheerful and friendly young / a friendly and cheerful young
    Two adjectives that say what someone is like can be joined with and before the noun, in either order. The age adjective young comes after them, next to the noun.
  15. Put the words in brackets in the correct order: My grandmother ___ (fine / feeling / is / now) after her surgery.
    Accepted answers: is feeling fine now / is now feeling fine
    Fine meaning healthy is only used after a verb (predicative), so it follows is feeling. Now can go at the end or after is.
  16. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Yoga helps me feel ___ (relax) before a big exam.
    Answer: relaxed
    The past participle relaxed describes how a person feels. Relaxing would describe the thing that causes the feeling, such as yoga.
  17. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Critics praised the ___ (act well) series but complained that each episode was too long.
    Answer: well-acted
    Well + past participle makes a compound adjective, written with a hyphen before a noun: a well-acted series (the actors did a good job).
  18. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: My phone rang loudly in the middle of the exam, which was really ___ (embarrass).
    Answer: embarrassing
    The present participle embarrassing describes the situation that causes the feeling. Embarrassed would describe the person.
  19. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Drop the noodles into the ___ (boil) water and stir them for three minutes.
    Answer: boiling
    The present participle boiling describes water that is boiling at this moment, which is what you cook noodles in.
  20. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Mrs. Patel's grandchildren are so ___ (bring up well) that they write thank-you notes for every gift.
    Accepted answers: well brought-up / well-brought-up / well brought up
    Well + the past participle brought up makes a compound adjective meaning taught good manners by their parents.
  21. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Be careful, there's ___ (break) glass all over the kitchen floor.
    Answer: broken
    The past participle broken describes something that has been broken. It is irregular: break, broke, broken.
  22. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Before the show, the horses' coats were shiny and very ___ (brush well).
    Accepted answers: well-brushed / well brushed
    Well + the past participle brushed makes a compound adjective meaning brushed carefully. After a verb it can be written with or without a hyphen.
  23. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: The company has to keep up with constantly ___ (change) technology.
    Answer: changing
    The present participle changing describes something that is changing all the time, which fits constantly.
  24. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: In a crisis, we need a ___ (think clearly) leader who won't panic.
    Answer: clear-thinking
    The adverb becomes clear and joins the present participle thinking to make the compound adjective clear-thinking, meaning able to think clearly. Before a noun it takes a hyphen.
  25. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Traffic jams near the stadium are a ___ (continue) headache for local residents, and nobody expects them to end soon.
    Answer: continuing
    The present participle continuing describes a problem that is still going on and will go on.
  26. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Earthquake experts say that ___ (build well) homes can survive even strong shaking.
    Answer: well-built
    Well + the past participle built makes a compound adjective meaning strong and made with care. Before a noun it takes a hyphen.
  27. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: The school trip was a ___ (plan badly) event: the bus came late and nobody had tickets.
    Accepted answers: badly-planned / badly planned
    An adverb + past participle makes a compound adjective before a noun: badly planned means organized poorly.
  28. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Our town's ___ (decline fast) population worries local business owners.
    Answer: fast-declining
    Fast + the present participle declining makes a compound adjective describing something that is getting smaller quickly.
  29. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: The bride's father wore a ___ (tailor beautifully) jacket that fit him perfectly.
    Accepted answers: beautifully-tailored / beautifully tailored
    An adverb + past participle makes a compound adjective before a noun: beautifully tailored means made very well to fit the person.
  30. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Hikers often carry ___ (dry) meat because it is light and keeps for weeks.
    Answer: dried
    The past participle dried describes food that has had the water taken out of it.
  31. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Collectors will pay a lot for a ___ (keep beautifully) old guitar in its original case.
    Accepted answers: beautifully-kept / beautifully kept
    An adverb + past participle makes a compound adjective before a noun: beautifully kept means looked after very well.
  32. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Her report was clear, short and very ___ (write well).
    Accepted answers: well-written / well written
    Well + the past participle written makes a compound adjective. After a verb it can be written with or without a hyphen.
  33. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: The insurance company paid for my ___ (steal) laptop.
    Answer: stolen
    The past participle stolen describes things that someone has stolen. It is irregular: steal, stole, stolen.
  34. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: Rescue dogs need a ___ (love) home where they feel safe.
    Answer: loving
    The present participle loving describes a home where people show love. Loved would describe something that receives love, which is not the idea here.
  35. Form an adjective from the words in brackets: The cyclist was hurt when someone opened the door of a ___ (park) car.
    Answer: parked
    The past participle parked describes a car that someone has parked and left.
  36. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: Even though it rained, the boat trip was surprisingly ___ (enjoy).
    Answer: enjoyable
    The suffix -able means able to be: enjoyable means able to be enjoyed.
  37. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: My grandfather has become a little ___ (forget) lately; he often loses his glasses.
    Answer: forgetful
    The suffix -ful means having: forgetful describes someone who often forgets things.
  38. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: As the river kept rising, the residents felt ___ (power) to stop the flooding.
    Answer: powerless
    The suffix -less means without: powerless means without the power to do anything.
  39. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: Witnesses must be completely ___ (truth) when they speak in court.
    Answer: truthful
    The suffix -ful means full of or having: truthful means telling the truth.
  40. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: The volunteers worked through the night; they were ___ (tire) in their efforts to rescue people.
    Answer: tireless
    The suffix -less means without: tireless means never seeming to get tired.
  41. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: The travel guide is very ___ (describe); it makes you feel as if you're already there.
    Answer: descriptive
    The suffix -ive means having the quality of doing this: descriptive means describing things well. The spelling changes from describe to descript-.
  42. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: You can count on Hana to finish the job on time; she's extremely ___ (rely).
    Answer: reliable
    The suffix -able means able to be: reliable means able to be relied on. The y changes to i.
  43. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: In a wolf pack, the younger wolves act ___ (submit) toward the leader.
    Answer: submissive
    The suffix -ive means tending to do this: submissive describes someone who always obeys others. Submit changes to submiss-.
  44. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: I made three ___ (care) mistakes on the test because I didn't check my answers.
    Answer: careless
    The suffix -less means without: careless mistakes are made without enough care. Careful would mean the opposite.
  45. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: The swans looked so ___ (grace) as they glided across the lake.
    Answer: graceful
    The suffix -ful means having: graceful means moving in a smooth, beautiful way.
  46. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: The deer stood completely ___ (motion) when it heard our footsteps.
    Answer: motionless
    The suffix -less means without: motionless means without any movement.
  47. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: Teenagers' moods can be very ___ (change): happy one minute and angry the next.
    Answer: changeable
    The suffix -able means able to: changeable describes something that changes often. The e of change stays before -able.
  48. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: My little brother is very ___ (act); he never sits still for more than a minute.
    Answer: active
    The suffix -ive means tending to do this: active means doing a lot and moving around.
  49. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: These sneakers are so ___ (comfort) that I can walk all day in them.
    Answer: comfortable
    The suffix -able makes comfortable, which describes things that give comfort.
  50. Make an adjective from the word in brackets: The traffic this morning was ___ (dread); it took me two hours to get to work.
    Answer: dreadful
    The suffix -ful makes dreadful, meaning very bad.
  51. The passengers grew ___ as the delay stretched past two hours.
    Words beginning with p usually take im- for the negative: impatient. Unpatient is not a word.
  52. Your question is interesting, but it's ___ to today's topic.
    Words beginning with r usually take ir- for the negative: irrelevant. Unrelevant is not a word.
  53. Many buyers found the house ___ because it was next to a busy highway.
    The negative of attractive is unattractive, with un-. Inattractive is not a word.
  54. In some regions, many older adults are still ___ because they never went to school.
    Words beginning with l usually take il- for the negative: illiterate means unable to read and write.
  55. The store's return policy is completely ___: no receipt, no refund.
    The negative of flexible is inflexible, with in-. Unflexible is not a word.
  56. The verb 'go' is ___ because its past form is 'went'.
    Words beginning with r usually take ir- for the negative: irregular.
  57. It is ___ to park in front of a fire hydrant.
    Words beginning with l usually take il- for the negative: illegal.
  58. The hotel manager was rude and ___, so we left a bad review.
    The negative of agreeable is disagreeable, with dis-, meaning unpleasant and unfriendly.
  59. Throwing food in the cafeteria is a very ___ way to behave.
    Words beginning with m usually take im- for the negative: immature means behaving like a much younger person.
  60. Sorry to call so late. Is this an ___ time?
    The negative of convenient is inconvenient, with in-. Unconvenient is not a word.
  61. Many viewers found the comedian's jokes about the disaster ___.
    The negative of tasteful is distasteful, with dis-, meaning unpleasant or offensive.
  62. Many people think it is ___ to test cosmetics on animals.
    Words beginning with m usually take im- for the negative: immoral means morally wrong.
  63. These old family photos are ___; if we lose them, we can never get them back.
    Words beginning with r usually take ir- for the negative: irreplaceable means impossible to replace.
  64. Leaving a small child alone in a car is completely ___.
    Words beginning with r usually take ir- for the negative: irresponsible.
  65. The price difference between the two laptops is ___, only about five dollars.
    The negative of significant is insignificant, with in-, meaning too small to matter.
  66. It's ___ to read the sign from here because it's too far away.
    Words beginning with p usually take im- for the negative: impossible.
  67. The doctor's handwriting was so messy that the prescription was ___.
    Words beginning with l usually take il- for the negative: illegible means impossible to read.
  68. The salesman was ___ about the car's history and hid the accident damage.
    The negative of honest is dishonest, with dis-. Unhonest is not a word.
  69. Lots of people have an ___ fear of spiders, even tiny harmless ones.
    Words beginning with r usually take ir- for the negative: irrational means not based on reason.
  70. For some ___ reason, the printer only works on Tuesdays.
    The negative of explicable is inexplicable, with in-, meaning impossible to explain.
  71. The employees claimed that their firing was ___ and sued the company.
    The negative of justified is unjustified, with un-, meaning without a good reason. Injustified is not a word.
  72. In the myth, the hero drinks a magic potion and becomes ___.
    Words beginning with m usually take im- for the negative: immortal means never dying.
  73. Doctors warned that the damage to his lungs was ___.
    Words beginning with r usually take ir- for the negative: irreversible means impossible to change back.
  74. This phone case is almost ___; you can drop it from a roof and it won't crack.
    The negative of destructible is indestructible, with in-, meaning impossible to destroy.
  75. The vase is slightly ___, so the store is selling it at a discount.
    Words beginning with p usually take im- for the negative: imperfect means having a small fault.
  76. The receptionist was so ___ that we felt unwelcome.
    The negative of friendly is unfriendly, with un-.
  77. Sand is ___ in water, so it sinks to the bottom of the glass.
    The negative of soluble is insoluble, with in-, meaning it can't be dissolved.
  78. In many countries, it is ___ to point at people with your finger.
    Words beginning with p usually take im- for the negative: impolite. Unpolite is not a word.
  79. His argument was completely ___; the conclusion didn't follow from the facts.
    Words beginning with l usually take il- for the negative: illogical.
  80. The lecture was long and ___, and half the students fell asleep.
    The negative of interesting is uninteresting, with un-. Disinteresting is not a word.
  81. The two puppies are ___; they eat, sleep and play together.
    The negative of separable is inseparable, with in-, meaning always together.
  82. The election result was very ___, since most polls had predicted the opposite.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Surprising is gradable; amazing already means very surprising, so very amazing is wrong.
  83. I skipped breakfast and lunch, so I'm absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Starving means extremely hungry, so it is non-gradable; absolutely hungry is wrong.
  84. The weather was fairly ___ for our picnic, though a bit windy.
    Fairly goes only with gradable adjectives. Nice is gradable; wonderful has a very strong meaning, so fairly wonderful is wrong.
  85. After playing soccer in the mud, the kids' uniforms were absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Filthy means extremely dirty, so it is non-gradable; absolutely dirty is wrong.
  86. When the factory closed, the whole town was absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Devastated means extremely upset, so it is non-gradable; absolutely upset is wrong.
  87. The view from the top of the mountain was absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Wonderful has a very strong meaning, so it is non-gradable; absolutely nice is wrong.
  88. I'm a bit ___. Is there anything to snack on?
    A bit goes only with gradable adjectives. Hungry is gradable; starving already means extremely hungry, so a bit starving is wrong.
  89. Grandma's homemade apple pie is absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Delicious means extremely tasty, so it is non-gradable; absolutely tasty is wrong.
  90. Their new apartment is very ___, but it doesn't have a balcony.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Big is gradable; enormous already means very big, so very enormous is wrong.
  91. The soup was fairly ___, but it needed a little more salt.
    Fairly goes only with gradable adjectives. Tasty is gradable; delicious already means extremely tasty, so fairly delicious is wrong.
  92. When Leo saw the dent in his new car, he was absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Furious means extremely angry, so it is non-gradable; absolutely angry is wrong.
  93. That sweater is a little ___, but it's very warm.
    A little goes only with gradable adjectives. Ugly is gradable; hideous already means extremely ugly, so a little hideous is wrong.
  94. Finding a parking space downtown on a Saturday is absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Impossible can't be more or less, so it is non-gradable; absolutely difficult is wrong.
  95. The hotel room was rather ___, but it was clean and quiet.
    Rather goes only with gradable adjectives. Small is gradable; minute already means extremely small, so rather minute is wrong.
  96. Everyone agreed that the new parking garage was absolutely ___ and spoiled the old town.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Hideous means extremely ugly, so it is non-gradable; absolutely ugly is wrong.
  97. During the earthquake, the children were absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Terrified means extremely frightened, so it is non-gradable; absolutely frightened is wrong.
  98. Your essay is very ___, but the conclusion could be stronger.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Good is gradable; perfect can't be more or less, so very perfect is wrong.
  99. The whale we saw from the boat was absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Enormous means extremely big, so it is non-gradable; absolutely big is wrong.
  100. I'm a little ___ today because the neighbors' party kept me awake.
    A little goes only with gradable adjectives. Tired is gradable; exhausted already means extremely tired, so a little exhausted is wrong.
  101. If you're very ___, there's cold water in the fridge.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Thirsty is gradable; parched already means extremely thirsty, so very parched is wrong.
  102. The Fourth of July fireworks show was absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Amazing means extremely surprising or impressive, so it is non-gradable; absolutely surprising is wrong.
  103. Mom was a bit ___ when we came home an hour late.
    A bit goes only with gradable adjectives. Angry is gradable; furious already means extremely angry, so a bit furious is wrong.
  104. Clean drinking water is absolutely ___ for good health.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Vital means extremely important, so it is non-gradable; with important we use very instead.
  105. After hiking all day in the desert, we were absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Parched means extremely thirsty, so it is non-gradable; absolutely thirsty is wrong.
  106. The kitchen floor is a bit ___. Could you mop it?
    A bit goes only with gradable adjectives. Dirty is gradable; filthy already means extremely dirty, so a bit filthy is wrong.
  107. Kim was a little ___ when she didn't get the part in the play.
    A little goes only with gradable adjectives. Upset is gradable; devastated already means extremely upset, so a little devastated is wrong.
  108. It's very ___ to back up your files regularly.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Important is gradable; vital already means extremely important, so very vital is wrong.
  109. The service at the restaurant was absolutely ___; we waited an hour for cold food.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Terrible means extremely bad, so it is non-gradable; absolutely bad is wrong.
  110. At first, the puppy was a little ___ of the vacuum cleaner.
    A little goes only with gradable adjectives. Frightened is gradable; terrified already means extremely frightened, so a little terrified is wrong.
  111. Under the microscope we could see some absolutely ___ insects living in the soil.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Minute means extremely small, so it is non-gradable; absolutely small is wrong.
  112. By the end of the marathon, the runners were absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Exhausted means extremely tired, so it is non-gradable; absolutely tired is wrong.
  113. The weather on their wedding day was absolutely ___.
    Absolutely goes with non-gradable adjectives. Perfect can't be more or less, so it is non-gradable; absolutely good is wrong.
  114. The last math problem was very ___, but I finally solved it.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Difficult is gradable; impossible can't be more or less, so very impossible is wrong (and the problem was solved).
  115. The traffic was very ___ this morning, but I still got to work on time.
    Very goes only with gradable adjectives. Bad is gradable; terrible already means extremely bad, so very terrible is wrong.
