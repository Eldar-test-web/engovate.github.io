Understanding and Using English Grammar Chart 18-4 – Expressing Cause and Effect In Modifying Adverbial Phrases

Choose or type the answer to each question, then press Check to see if you are right.
  1. "Waiting at the crosswalk, Diego waved to a bus driver." Who was waiting at the crosswalk?
    A modifying phrase at the start of a sentence describes the subject of the main clause. The subject is Diego, so Diego was waiting.
  2. "Swimming in the hotel pool, a seagull grabbed Hana's sandwich." Who was swimming in the hotel pool?
    The phrase describes the subject of the main clause, which is a seagull, so the sentence says the seagull was swimming. To say Hana was swimming, Hana must be the subject: Swimming in the hotel pool, Hana lost her sandwich to a seagull.
  3. "Jogging along the river this morning, I spotted a heron standing in the shallow water." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    The phrase tells what I was doing when I saw the heron: While I was jogging. Jogging was not the reason for seeing it, so it doesn't mean because.
  4. "Being the only nurse on the night shift, Hector had to answer every call himself." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    Being + noun gives a reason: Because he was the only nurse, he had to answer every call. Being is a state, not an activity going on at the same time, so it doesn't mean while.
  5. "Waiting in line at the post office, I read half a chapter of my novel." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    The phrase means While I was waiting in line: the two actions happened at the same time. Waiting was not the reason for reading.
  6. "Humming quietly to himself, the mechanic changed the oil in my car." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    Humming happened at the same time as changing the oil: While he was humming. It did not cause the oil change, so it doesn't mean because.
  7. "Having skipped breakfast, I was starving by ten o'clock." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    Having + past participle shows an earlier action that is also the reason: Because I had skipped breakfast. It can't mean while, since skipping breakfast happened first.
  8. "Knowing that the store closed at nine, Ming rushed through her shopping." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    The phrase explains why Ming rushed: Because she knew the store closed at nine. Knowing is a state, not an activity going on at the same time, so it doesn't mean while.
  9. "Looking down at his phone instead of the road, the cyclist rode straight into a parked car." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    He was looking at his phone at the moment of the crash (while), and looking at his phone also caused the crash (because). So the phrase gives both meanings.
  10. "Having lost her passport in Rome, Tara had to spend a day at the embassy." Does the opening phrase mean while, because, or both?
    Having + past participle shows an earlier action that caused the result: Because she had lost her passport. The loss came before the embassy visit, so it can't mean while.
  11. Change the adverb clause to a modifying adverbial phrase: "Because Priya didn't want to wake the baby, she tiptoed past the nursery." → ___ to wake the baby, Priya tiptoed past the nursery.
    Answer: Not wanting
    A because-clause can become an -ing phrase without because or a subject. In a negative phrase, not goes before the -ing form: Not wanting.
  12. Change the adverb clause to a modifying adverbial phrase: "Because Marcus hadn't read the email, he didn't know the meeting had moved." → ___ the email, Marcus didn't know the meeting had moved.
    Accepted answers: Not having read / Having not read
    Hadn't read is an earlier action that caused the result, so use not + having + past participle: Not having read. Because is dropped in the phrase.
  13. Change the adverb clause to a modifying adverbial phrase: "Because Dr. Silva believes that sleep matters more than diet, she asks every patient about their bedtime." → ___ that sleep matters more than diet, Dr. Silva asks every patient about their bedtime.
    Answer: Believing
    Drop because and the subject, and change the verb to its -ing form: Believing. The phrase keeps the meaning of because.
  14. Change the adverb clause to a modifying adverbial phrase: "Because Jamal is the tallest person in the office, he always gets asked to reach the top shelf." → ___ the tallest person in the office, Jamal always gets asked to reach the top shelf.
    Answer: Being
    A form of be in a because-clause changes to being: Being the tallest person. Being makes the cause-and-effect meaning clear.
  15. Before ___ this cooking class, I had never made bread from scratch. Which can complete the sentence: (a) took (b) taking (c) I took?
    Before can be followed by an -ing form (Before taking) or by a full clause with a subject (Before I took). A past form with no subject (Before took) is wrong.
  16. After ___ the instructions twice, I finally put the bookshelf together correctly. Which can complete the sentence: (a) I read (b) read (c) reading?
    After can begin a full clause (After I read) or an -ing phrase (After reading). After + a verb with no subject (After read) is wrong.
  17. Since ___ to Seattle, my aunt has joined two hiking clubs. Which can complete the sentence: (a) she moved (b) moving (c) moved?
    Since can begin a full clause (Since she moved) or an -ing phrase (Since moving). Moved with no subject is wrong after since.
  18. ___ through the national park, we were amazed by how many different birds we saw. Which can complete the sentence: (a) Driving (b) While we were driving (c) While driving?
    A while-clause can be reduced to While driving, and while can also be left out (Driving) with the same meaning. The full clause While we were driving is also correct.
  19. ___ her own restaurant, the chef had worked in hotel kitchens for ten years. Which can complete the sentence: (a) Before opening (b) She had opened (c) Before she opened?
    Before opening and Before she opened both show the time relationship. She had opened is a full sentence, so joining it to the main clause with only a comma is wrong.
  20. ___ snow before, the exchange student from Brazil ran outside as soon as it started to fall. Which can complete the sentence: (a) Had never seen (b) Having never seen (c) Because he had never seen?
    The reason can be a full because-clause or a phrase with having + past participle: Having never seen. Had never seen with no subject can't begin the sentence.
  21. Before ___ the head coach, Ms. Duarte played professional soccer for six years. Which can complete the sentence: (a) became (b) becoming (c) she became?
    Before is followed by an -ing form (Before becoming) or a clause with a subject (Before she became). Before + became with no subject is wrong.
  22. ___ the attention from the crowd, the young violinist played two extra songs. Which can complete the sentence: (a) Because enjoying (b) Enjoying (c) She was enjoying?
    Only Enjoying fits: an -ing phrase with no connecting word. Because is never used in a modifying phrase, and She was enjoying makes a full sentence joined to another with just a comma.
  23. ___ to miss the opening scene, Leila left work early and took a taxi to the theater. Which can complete the sentence: (a) Not wanting (b) Because she didn't want (c) Because not wanting?
    The reason can be a full because-clause or the phrase Not wanting. Because can't begin a modifying phrase, so Because not wanting is wrong.
  24. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "I am allergic to cats. My roommate's kitten has to stay out of my bedroom." ___
    A modifying phrase needs the same subject as the main clause. The subjects here are I and my roommate's kitten, so Being allergic to cats would wrongly describe the kitten.
  25. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "I am the newest member of the band. I practice every evening to catch up with the others." ___
    Both sentences have the subject I, so the first can become a phrase. Am changes to being, and the phrase gives the reason.
  26. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "The volunteers had planted two hundred trees. They celebrated with a picnic in the park." ___
    The volunteers and they are the same subject. The past perfect had planted becomes having + past participle: Having planted. Had planted with no subject can't begin a phrase.
  27. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "The passengers were tired. The flight attendant dimmed the cabin lights." ___
    The subjects are different: the passengers and the flight attendant. Being tired would describe the flight attendant, not the passengers.
  28. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "Our neighbor works from home. She can take care of our dog when we travel." ___
    Our neighbor and she are the same person, so the first sentence becomes an -ing phrase: Working from home. It gives the reason she can take care of the dog.
  29. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "The city closed the main bridge for repairs. Traffic has been terrible all week." ___
    The subjects are the city and traffic. A phrase would describe traffic, as if traffic had closed the bridge.
  30. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "I learned that my flight was canceled. I called the airline to book another one." ___
    Both sentences have the subject I. Having learned shows that the learning came first and was the reason for the call.
  31. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "We reached the top of the hill. The whole valley spread out below us." ___
    The subjects are we and the whole valley. Reaching the top of the hill would describe the valley, not us.
  32. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "The kids were building a sandcastle. A big wave washed it away." ___
    The subjects are the kids and a big wave. The phrase would say the wave was building the sandcastle.
  33. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "My coworker didn't want to sound rude. She thanked the manager for the feedback." ___
    My coworker and she are the same subject. Didn't want becomes not + -ing: Not wanting. Not want is the wrong form.
  34. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "I understood the question, but I had forgotten the formula. I left the last problem blank." ___
    Both verbs in the first sentence have the subject I, so both change: understood becomes understanding and had forgotten becomes having forgotten. Had forgotten can't stay in a phrase with no subject.
  35. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "My teacher praised my essay in front of the class. I turned bright red." ___
    The subjects are my teacher and I. Praising my essay would describe me, but it was the teacher who praised it.
  36. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "The museum was closed for repairs. We visited the botanical garden instead." ___
    The subjects are the museum and we. Being closed for repairs would describe us, not the museum.
  37. Combine the sentences by making a modifying phrase out of the first one, if possible: "I was convinced that the plan would work. I invested all my savings in it." ___
    Both sentences have the subject I. Was changes to being: Being convinced. The base form be can't begin the phrase.
  38. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "They sleep up to 20 hours a day." + "Koalas have little time for anything else." → ___ up to 20 hours a day, koalas have little time for anything else.
    Answer: Sleeping
    They and koalas are the same subject, so the first sentence becomes an -ing phrase: Sleeping. It gives the reason for the main clause.
  39. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "He has saved money for years." + "Tomás can finally afford a house." → ___ money for years, Tomás can finally afford a house.
    Answer: Having saved
    Has saved is an earlier action that caused the result, so use having + past participle: Having saved.
  40. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "It was built in 1890." + "The old train station needs constant repairs." → ___ in 1890, the old train station needs constant repairs.
    Accepted answers: Built / Having been built
    The station was built by someone, so the phrase is passive: Built or Having been built. Being built would mean the building work is still going on.
  41. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "They had packed everything into the van." + "The movers drove off to the new house." → ___ everything into the van, the movers drove off to the new house.
    Answer: Having packed
    Had packed shows the earlier action, so use having + past participle: Having packed. It means after, and because, they had packed.
  42. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "He had never driven in snow before." + "Ravi was nervous about the trip to the mountains." → ___ in snow before, Ravi was nervous about the trip to the mountains.
    Accepted answers: Never having driven / Having never driven
    Had never driven becomes never having driven (or having never driven). The phrase gives the reason Ravi was nervous.
  43. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "She had no car." + "Ingrid rode her bike to work every day, even in winter." → ___ no car, Ingrid rode her bike to work every day, even in winter.
    Answer: Having
    Had here means owned, so the phrase uses the -ing form having: Having no car. It explains why Ingrid rode her bike.
  44. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "It is light and very strong." + "Bamboo is used to build scaffolding in many Asian cities." → ___ and very strong, bamboo is used to build scaffolding in many Asian cities.
    Accepted answers: Being light / Light
    Is changes to being: Being light and very strong. Before an adjective, being can also be left out: Light and very strong.
  45. Use the first sentence as a modifying phrase: "They are able to swim long distances." + "Polar bears can travel between pieces of floating ice." → ___ to swim long distances, polar bears can travel between pieces of floating ice.
    Accepted answers: Being able / Able
    Are able becomes being able, or just able without being. The phrase gives the reason polar bears can travel between pieces of ice.
  46. After getting off the train, ___
    The subject of the main clause must be the one who did the action in the phrase. I got off the train, not the umbrella, so the main clause must begin with I.
  47. After checking into the hotel, ___
    We checked into the hotel, and we are the subject of the main clause, so this is correct. In the other option the subject is the beach, which can't check into a hotel.
  48. While hiking up the steep trail, ___
    I was hiking, so I must be the subject of the main clause. With a sharp rock as the subject, the phrase would say the rock was hiking.
  49. Not wanting to be late for the interview, ___
    Kofi didn't want to be late, and Kofi is the subject, so this is correct. In the other option the subject is the alarm, which can't want anything.
  50. After moving into the new apartment, ___
    We moved into the apartment, so we must be the subject of the main clause. With the heating as the subject, the phrase would say the heating moved in.
  51. While cleaning out the garage, ___
    The person cleaning must be the subject of the main clause. With an old photo album as the subject, the phrase would say the album was cleaning the garage.
  52. When asked about the delay by angry passengers, ___
    When asked means when he or she was asked. The pilot was the one asked and is the subject, so this is correct; an apology can't be asked about a delay.
  53. Being a vegetarian, ___
    My sister is the vegetarian, so my sister must be the subject of the main clause. With the menu as the subject, the phrase would say the menu is a vegetarian.
  54. Seeing the storm clouds, ___
    The farmers saw the clouds, and they are the subject, so this is correct. In the other option the subject is the hay, which can't see clouds.
  55. Holding the baby in one arm, ___
    Mr. Novak is holding the baby and is the subject, so this is correct. In the other option the subject is the front door, which can't hold a baby.
  56. Change the modifying phrase to an adverb clause: "While cooking dinner, keep the kitchen window open." → While ___ dinner, keep the kitchen window open.
    Accepted answers: you are cooking / you're cooking / you cook
    The main clause is an instruction, so its subject is you. The clause needs that subject and a full verb: you are cooking or you cook.
  57. Change the modifying phrase to an adverb clause: "When choosing a laptop for college, you should think about battery life first." → When ___ a laptop for college, you should think about battery life first.
    Accepted answers: you are choosing / you're choosing / you choose
    The subject of the main clause is you, so it becomes the subject of the clause: When you choose or When you are choosing.
  58. Change the modifying phrase to an adverb clause: "Before starting a new exercise program, talk to your doctor." → Before ___ a new exercise program, talk to your doctor.
    Answer: you start
    Talk to your doctor is an instruction to you, so the clause needs you + a present verb: Before you start. A time clause uses the simple present, not will, for the future.
  59. Change the modifying phrase to an adverb clause: "Most people make more mistakes when typing on a phone." → Most people make more mistakes when ___ on a phone.
    Accepted answers: they type / they are typing / they're typing
    The clause needs a subject that refers to the subject of the main clause, most people: they type or they are typing.
  60. Change the modifying phrase to an adverb clause: "After watching the video, write down three new words you heard." → After ___ the video, write down three new words you heard.
    Accepted answers: you watch / you have watched / you've watched
    The main clause is an instruction, so the subject is you. After you watch (or have watched) shows that the watching comes first.
  61. Change the modifying phrase to an adverb clause: "When shopping online, many people spend more than they planned." → When ___ online, many people spend more than they planned.
    Accepted answers: they shop / they are shopping / they're shopping
    The subject of the main clause is many people, so the clause uses they + a present verb: When they shop or When they are shopping.
