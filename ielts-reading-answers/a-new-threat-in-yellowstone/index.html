Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14-26 which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

A New Threat in Yellowstone

It has long been known that Yellowstone National Park lies over an enormous supervolcano. The term ‘supervolcano’ implies a volcanic centre that has had an eruption of magnitude 8 on the Volcano Explosivity Index (VEI), meaning the measured deposits for that eruption are greater than 1,000 cubic kilometres. This sounds worrying and Professor George Peters details the possible results if something were to happen. "A major eruption would obliterate the surroundings within a radius of hundreds of kilometres, and cover the rest of the United States and Canada with multiple inches of ash. This would shut down agriculture and cause global climate cooling for as long as a decade.” To calm everyone down, geologist, Tony Masters, explains there is little to fear today. "All VEI 8 eruptions, including the last at Yellowstone, occurred tens of thousands to millions of years ago. Another eruption could occur, but it is very unlikely to happen in the next million years or so.”

Yellowstone is no stranger to controversy. There was a previous media accusation that US Geological Survey (USGS) geologists had not done their work properly and that the identification of Yellowstone as a supervolcano was not done until scientists looked at photographs of Yellowstone from space. The Yellowstone scientists denied this. Spokesman Alice Wheeler clarifies their position. "The scientist who first identified the three Yellowstone calderas was from the USGS and he told the world about the great eruptions that formed them. He traced out the caldera boundaries through old fashioned field work, walking around with a hammer and hand lens and looking carefully at the rocks and their distributions.” The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) also agreed. Stan Forsyth, their spokesman, explains. "Several authors have written that these large calderas in Yellowstone were discovered from space, but we suspect that the rumour probably got started because initial field work that identified them was partly funded by NASA.”

A new problem in Yellowstone is that the supervolcano has now been discovered to be larger than originally thought and this has made people feel more nervous. Seismologists at the University of Utah have worked with several other institutions to create an image of the Yellowstone magma reservoir using a technique called seismic tomography. Masters student, Julia Grey, explains the results. "By looking closely at data from thousands of earthquakes, we have discovered that there are two magma reservoirs, one shallow and one deep, and that they are much larger than originally believed. The shallow one was previously known about to us, but the deeper one is a new finding.”

To create an image of this second magma reservoir beneath Yellowstone, the research teams reviewed data from thousands of earthquakes. Seismic waves travel slower through hot, partially molten rock and faster in cold, solid rock. The researchers made a map of the locations where seismic waves travel more slowly, which provided a sub-surface image of the hot or partially molten bodies in the crust beneath Yellowstone. The deeper magma storage region extends from 20 to 50 kilometres depth, contains about 2 per cent melt, and is about 4.5 times larger than the shallow magma body. The shallower magma storage region is about 90 kilometres long, extends from 5 to 17 kilometres depth, and is 2.5 times larger than a prior, less accurate, study indicated. This magma reservoir contains between about 5 to 15 per cent molten rock. Although this is the crustal magma storage region that has fuelled Yellowstone’s past volcanic activity, magma typically does not erupt unless it has greater than 50 per cent melt.

The US and world media were quick to dramatise the finding and exaggerate the threat that these findings represent. Yellowstone park scientist, Amy Brent, has calming words. "These findings do not increase the assessment of volcanic hazard for Yellowstone. The inferred magma storage region is no larger than we already knew. The research simply makes a better image of the magmatic system. Simply, we have more key information about how the Yellowstone volcano works.”

Many independent reports back up Brent’s comments and have shown that the Yellowstone area has been on a long cycle of periodic eruptions. Eruptions are extremely infrequent in supervolcanos, and eventually the cycle ends in their deaths. US government geologist, Andrea Haller, explains the state of the Yellowstone supervolcano. "By investigating the patterns of behaviour in two previously completed caldera cycles, we can suggest that the current activity of Yellowstone is on the dying cycle.” This is based on comparisons with other supervolcanos. Scientists know the behaviour of the past and they know at what comparative stage Yellowstone is right now. It is believed that Yellowstone is currently on a third and dying cycle. This can be concluded by the fact that dying volcanos produce less fresh molten material from the Earth’s crust. Haller continues. "We’ve observed a lot of material in the magma chambers that represent recycled volcanic rocks, which were once buried inside of calderas and are now getting reused. Yellowstone has erupted enough of this material already to suggest that the future melting potential of the crust is getting exhausted.”

Whatever the truth about Yellowstone, it seems that during the lives of most people, the geological status of Yellowstone can still prove hazardous. The park has often been closed due to volcanic activity in the past and this is likely to happen again before the volcano becomes harmless.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q14 AH US government geologist, Andrea Haller, explains the state of the Yellowstone supervolcano. "By investigating the patterns of behaviour in two previously completed caldera cycles, we can suggest that the current activity of Yellowstone is on the dying cycle.” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that a geologist named Andrea Haller looked at the past cycles of the volcano and concluded that Yellowstone is now in a cycle where its volcanic activity is slowly ending or dying out.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "AH" represents Andrea Haller, the scientist who states that the Yellowstone volcano is currently in its dying cycle.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is AH because Andrea Haller directly explains that the current activity of the Yellowstone supervolcano is coming to an end. In the text, Haller notes that by studying past cycles, scientists can see that the volcano's current activity is on a "dying cycle." Key phrases to notice include "Andrea Haller" and "on the dying cycle."
Q15 AW Spokesman Alice Wheeler clarifies their position. "The scientist who first identified the three Yellowstone calderas was from the USGS and he told the world about the great eruptions that formed them. He traced out the caldera boundaries through old fashioned field work, walking around with a hammer and hand lens and looking carefully at the rocks and their distributions." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Alice Wheeler explaining that the first scientist to discover the volcanic areas at Yellowstone did not use space photos. Instead, he did normal, hands-on field science by walking around the park with simple tools and checking the rocks closely.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "AW" stands for Alice Wheeler, the person who explained that the Yellowstone volcano areas were first discovered by a scientist doing normal, hands-on geological work outdoors.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is AW because Alice Wheeler describes how the Yellowstone calderas were originally discovered. In the second paragraph, she states that the scientist found them through "old fashioned field work" by walking around with tools like a hammer and a hand lens. In the question, the phrase "traditional geology work" matches Wheeler's words "old fashioned field work", and "first identified" directly matches her statement "The scientist who first identified the three Yellowstone calderas".
Q16 GP Professor George Peters details the possible results if something were to happen. "A major eruption would obliterate the surroundings within a radius of hundreds of kilometres, and cover the rest of the United States and Canada with multiple inches of ash. This would shut down agriculture and cause global climate cooling for as long as a decade." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Professor George Peters said a big explosion from the volcano would cover Canada and the United States in deep ash, which would completely stop all farming.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "GP" refers to Professor George Peters.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is GP because in the first paragraph, Professor George Peters discusses what would happen if a massive eruption occurred. He explains that ash would cover the United States and Canada, which would "shut down agriculture." In the question, "farming to cease" directly matches the idea of "shut down agriculture."
Q17 JG Masters student, Julia Grey, explains the results. "By looking closely at data from thousands of earthquakes, we have discovered that there are two magma reservoirs, one shallow and one deep, and that they are much larger than originally believed. The shallow one was previously known about to us, but the deeper one is a new finding." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says Julia Grey explains the new results. She says there are two magma reservoirs, and both are much bigger than people first believed.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says Julia Grey is the person who said the magma chambers are bigger than people thought before.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is JG because the statement matches her words. 'Magma chambers' means 'magma reservoirs', and 'larger than previously thought' means 'much larger than originally believed'. Amy Brent says the opposite: the region is 'no larger than we already knew'.
Q18 TM To calm everyone down, geologist, Tony Masters, explains there is little to fear today. "All VEI 8 eruptions, including the last at Yellowstone, occurred tens of thousands to millions of years ago Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows geologist Tony Masters saying that very big volcanic explosions, including the most recent one at Yellowstone, happened tens of thousands to millions of years ago, meaning people do not need to worry right now.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TM" stands for Tony Masters, who said that the previous big explosion at Yellowstone happened tens of thousands to millions of years in the past.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is Tony Masters (TM) because the first paragraph links him directly to the statement. Tony Masters explains that huge "VEI 8 eruptions" (major eruptions), including the most recent one at Yellowstone, "occurred tens of thousands to millions of years ago." Therefore, he is the person who stated that a major Yellowstone eruption last happened thousands of years ago.
Q19 AB Yellowstone park scientist, Amy Brent, has calming words. "These findings do not increase the assessment of volcanic hazard for Yellowstone. The inferred magma storage region is no larger than we already knew. The research simply makes a better image of the magmatic system. Simply, we have more key information about how the Yellowstone volcano works.” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains through park scientist Amy Brent that the new research does not mean there is more danger; instead, it means scientists now possess more important details to understand how the volcano works.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "AB" refers to the initials of Amy Brent.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is AB because Amy Brent states that scientists now have clearer knowledge about the Yellowstone volcano. In paragraph 5, she explains, "Simply, we have more key information about how the Yellowstone volcano works." The phrase "know better about the functioning" matches "have more key information about how [it] works".
Q20 SF Stan Forsyth, their spokesman, explains. "Several authors have written that these large calderas in Yellowstone were discovered from space, but we suspect that the rumour probably got started because initial field work that identified them was partly funded by NASA." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that Stan Forsyth noted that NASA partly paid for the early field studies that discovered the volcanic calderas in Yellowstone.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "SF" refers to Stan Forsyth.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is SF because Stan Forsyth, speaking for NASA, explains that early scientific work identifying the Yellowstone calderas received financial support from NASA. In the text, the phrase "partly funded by NASA" matches the statement that NASA "provided money in the past to help research."
Q21 50 The deeper magma storage region extends from 20 to 50 kilometres depth, contains about 2 per cent melt, and is about 4.5 times larger than the shallow magma body Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the deeper pool of magma reaches from 20 kilometres down to 50 kilometres underground.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "50" refers to the deepest point, in kilometres beneath the Earth's surface, reached by the deeper underground magma reservoir.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 50 because paragraph 4 describes the dimensions and depth of the deeper magma chamber. Specifically, it notes that this deeper region "extends from 20 to 50 kilometres depth". Therefore, the number marking the bottom limit of this area is 50.
Q22 2% / 2 per cent The deeper magma storage region extends from 20 to 50 kilometres depth, contains about 2 per cent melt, and is about 4.5 times larger than the shallow magma body Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says the deeper magma area is 20 to 50 kilometres underground. It has about 2 per cent melted rock. It is about 4.5 times bigger than the shallow magma area.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the deeper magma region has 2 per cent melt, so this number fills the diagram label.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is '2 per cent' because the passage says the deeper region 'contains about 2 per cent melt'. Look for the key words 'deeper', 'contains' and 'melt'. The shallow region has a different number (5 to 15 per cent), so do not choose that one.
Q23 50% / 50 per cent magma typically does not erupt unless it has greater than 50 per cent melt Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says magma usually does not erupt unless more than half of it is melted (more than 50 per cent melt).
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the amount of melt needed for an eruption is 50 per cent.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 50% because the passage says magma 'does not erupt unless it has greater than 50 per cent melt'. Look for the keywords 'erupt' and 'melt' with a number. The diagram label asks for the melt level needed to erupt, so the number is 50 per cent.
Q24 A Seismic waves travel slower through hot, partially molten rock and faster in cold, solid rock. The researchers made a map of the locations where seismic waves travel more slowly, which provided a sub-surface image of the hot or partially molten bodies in the crust beneath Yellowstone Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that earthquake waves go more slowly through hot, partly melted rock and more quickly through cold, hard rock. By tracking where the waves slow down, scientists were able to create an underground picture of the magma chambers.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that scientists can create pictures of magma chambers because earthquake waves move at different speeds depending on whether the rock is hot and melted or cold and solid.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the text explains that seismic waves move at varying speeds through different types and temperatures of rock—moving slower in hot, partly melted rock and faster in cold, solid rock. By mapping where the waves move slower versus faster, scientists are able to produce an image of the magma reservoirs beneath the ground. Keywords to notice include "travel slower" and "faster" (which mean "different speeds"), as well as "hot, partially molten rock" and "cold, solid rock" (which refer to "different states of rock").
Q25 C It is believed that Yellowstone is currently on a third and dying cycle. This can be concluded by the fact that dying volcanos produce less fresh molten material from the Earth’s crust Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that scientists think Yellowstone is coming to the end of its life (dying cycle) because dying volcanoes generate less new liquid rock from the crust.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that scientists can likely tell that the Yellowstone supervolcano is dying because it is making less new melted rock.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the passage explains that experts believe Yellowstone is in its "dying cycle." They can tell this because dying volcanoes make less "fresh molten material" (melted rock) from the Earth's crust, and instead recycle old rocks. Thus, this dying stage can be predicted by observing the decrease or lack of new melted rock.
Q26 A The park has often been closed due to volcanic activity in the past and this is likely to happen again before the volcano becomes harmless Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Yellowstone has been shut down many times before because of volcanic danger, and it will probably be closed again in the future.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Yellowstone National Park will likely need to shut down and turn visitors away during dangerous conditions.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the last paragraph states that the park was frequently shut down due to volcanic activity before, and this is expected to happen again. In the text, the phrase 'likely to happen again' matches 'will probably', and volcanic activity being 'hazardous' matches 'times of danger'.
