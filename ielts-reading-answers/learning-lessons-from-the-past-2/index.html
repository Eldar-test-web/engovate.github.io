From IELTS Past Papers Academic Reading Test 07 · Part 3 · Questions 27–40

Reading Passage

Learning lessons from the past

A Many past societies collapsed or vanished, leaving behind monumental ruins such as those that the poet Shelley imagined in his sonnet, Ozymandias. By collapse, I mean a drastic decrease in human population size and/or political/economic/social complexity, over a considerable area, for an extended time. By those standards, most people would consider the following past societies to have been famous victims of full-fledged collapses rather than of just minor declines: the Anasazi and Cahokia within the boundaries of the modem US, the Maya cities in Central America, Moche and Tiwanaku societies in South America, Norse Greenland, Mycenean Greece and Minoan Crete in Europe, Great Zimbabwe in Africa, Angkor Wat and the Harappan Indus Valley cities in Asia, and Easter Island in the Pacific Ocean.

B The monumental ruins left behind by those past societies hold a fascination for all of us. We marvel at them when as children we first learn of them through pictures. When we grow up, many of us plan vacations in order to experience them at first hand. We feel drawn to their often spectacular and haunting beauty, and also to the mysteries that they pose. The scales of the ruins testify to the former wealth and power of their builders. Yet these builders vanished, abandoning the great structures that they had created at such effort. How could a society that was once so mighty end up collapsing?

C It has long been suspected that many of those mysterious abandonments were at least partly triggered by ecological problems: people inadvertently destroying the environmental resources on which their societies depended. This suspicion of unintended ecological suicide (ecocide) has been confirmed by discoveries made in recent decades by archaeologists, climatologists, historians, paleontologists, and palynologists (pollen scientists). The processes through which past societies have undermined themselves by damaging their environments fall into eight categories, whose relative importance differs from case to case: deforestation and habitat destruction, soil problems, water management problems, overhunting, overfishing, effects of introduced species on native species, human population growth, and increased impact of people.

D Those past collapses tended to follow somewhat similar courses constituting variations on a theme. Writers find it tempting to draw analogies between the course of human societies and the course of individual human lives - to talk of a society’s birth, growth, peak, old age and eventual death. But that metaphor proves erroneous for many past societies: they declined rapidly after reaching peak numbers and power, and those rapid declines must have come as a surprise and shock to their citizens. Obviously, too, this trajectory is not one that all past societies followed unvaryingly to completion: different societies collapsed to different degrees and in somewhat different ways, while many societies did not collapse at all.

E Today many people feel that environmental problems overshadow all the other threats to global civilisation. These environmental problems include the same eight that undermined past societies, plus four new ones: human-caused climate change, build up of toxic chemicals in the environment, energy shortages, and full human utilisation of the Earth’s photosynthetic capacity. But the seriousness of these current environmental problems is vigorously debated. Are the risks greatly exaggerated, or conversely are they underestimated? Will modem technology solve our problems, or is it creating new problems faster than it solves old ones? When we deplete one resource (eg wood, oil, or ocean fish), can we count on being able to substitute some new resource (eg plastics, wind and solar energy, or farmed fish)? Isn’t the rate of human population growth declining, such that we're already on course for the world’s population to level off at some manageable number of people?

F Questions like this illustrate why those famous collapses of past civilisations have taken on more meaning than just that of a romantic mystery. Perhaps there are some practical lessons that we could learn from all those past collapses. But there are also differences between the modem world and its problems, and those past societies and their problems. We shouldn’t be so naive as to think that study of the past will yield simple solutions, directly transferable to our societies today. We differ from past societies in some respects that put us at lower risk than them; some of those respects often mentioned include our powerful technology (ie its beneficial effects), globalisation, modem medicine, and greater knowledge of past societies and of distant modem societies. We also differ from past societies in some respects that put us at greater risk than them: again, our potent technology (ie its unintended destructive effects), globalisation (such that now a problem in one part of the world affects all the rest), the dependence of millions of us on modem medicine for our survival, and our much larger human population. Perhaps we can still learn from the past, but only if we think carefully about its lessons.

Questions

Questions 27–29 One Choice

Choose the correct letter, A, B, C or D.

27 When the writer describes the impact of monumental ruins today, he emphasises
  1. the income they generate from tourism.
  2. the area of land they occupy.
  3. their archaeological value.
  4. their romantic appeal.
28 Recent findings concerning vanished civilisations
  1. have overturned long-held beliefs.
  2. caused controversy amongst scientists.
  3. come from a variety of disciplines.
  4. identified one main cause of environmental damage.
29 What does the writer say about ways in which former societies collapsed?
  1. The pace of decline was usually similar.
  2. The likelihood of collapse would have been foreseeable.
  3. Deterioration invariably led to total collapse.
  4. Individual citizens could sometimes influence the course of events.

NO if the statement contradicts the claims of the writer

NOT GIVEN if it is impossible to say what the writer thinks about this

35 Evidence of the greatness of some former civilisations
36 The parallel between an individual’s life and the life of a society
37 The number of environmental problems that societies face

Questions 40–40 One Choice

Choose the correct letter, A, B, C or D.

40 What is the main argument of Reading Passage 3?
  1. There are differences as well as similarities between past and present societies.
  2. More should be done to preserve the physical remains of earlier civilisations.
  3. Some historical accounts of great civilisations are inaccurate.
  4. Modern societies are dependent on each other for their continuing survival.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q27 D We feel drawn to their often spectacular and haunting beauty, and also to the mysteries that they pose Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that people are deeply attracted to the amazing beauty and secret mysteries of ancient ruins.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the author highlights how people today are attracted to the special, beautiful, and mysterious nature of ancient ruins.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D. In Paragraph B, the author describes how monumental ruins affect people today, noting that we feel pulled toward their 'spectacular and haunting beauty' and the 'mysteries that they pose.' This focus on mystery, beauty, and emotional fascination represents their romantic appeal. The passage does not highlight the money made from tourism, the physical area of land, or their value to archaeologists in this context.
Q28 C This suspicion of unintended ecological suicide (ecocide) has been confirmed by discoveries made in recent decades by archaeologists, climatologists, historians, paleontologists, and palynologists (pollen scientists) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that recent discoveries were made by many different kinds of experts, including people who study ancient remains, weather, history, fossils, and pollen.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that new information discovered about societies that disappeared comes from many different areas of science and study.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text explains that new discoveries about why old societies disappeared were made by experts from many different fields, such as archaeologists, climatologists, historians, paleontologists, and pollen scientists. In the text, 'discoveries made in recent decades' means 'recent findings', and the list of different types of scientists represents 'a variety of disciplines' (different areas of knowledge).
Q29 A Those past collapses tended to follow somewhat similar courses constituting variations on a theme. Writers find it tempting to draw analogies between the course of human societies and the course of individual human lives - to talk of a society’s birth, growth, peak, old age and eventual death. But that metaphor proves erroneous for many past societies: they declined rapidly after reaching peak numbers and power, and those rapid declines must have come as a surprise and shock to their citizens Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that when old civilizations fell, they usually followed a similar path, dropping quickly in power and population soon after reaching their strongest point.
Answer Explanation:
The answer choice A means that the speed at which old societies fell apart was generally almost the same.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the text states that past collapses usually followed a similar pattern or course, and that many societies "declined rapidly" (went down quickly) after reaching their highest point of power. Keywords like "similar courses" match "usually similar", and "declined rapidly" refers to the fast "pace of decline". Choices B, C, and D are incorrect because the text states the collapse was a surprise (not foreseeable), did not happen to all societies (not invariable), and does not mention individual citizens changing the outcome.
Q30 YES Today many people feel that environmental problems overshadow all the other threats to global civilisation Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that nowadays, a lot of people think that nature and environment problems are more serious than any other danger facing the world.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that most people agree that environmental issues are the biggest threat to our world today.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because paragraph E states that many people feel environmental problems are bigger than any other danger to world civilization. The phrase 'many people feel' matches 'it is widely believed', and 'overshadow all the other threats' means these problems represent the main danger.
Q31 YES These environmental problems include the same eight that undermined past societies, plus four new ones: human-caused climate change, build up of toxic chemicals in the environment, energy shortages, and full human utilisation of the Earth’s photosynthetic capacity Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that today's world has the same eight environmental problems as past human groups, along with four brand new problems, including the collection of poisonous chemicals in nature.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is YES, meaning the writer agrees with the statement.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because the passage explains that the increase of harmful materials in nature is a new problem. The author lists eight main environmental issues from ancient times and mentions that today we face those same eight issues plus four new ones. One of these four new issues is the "build up of toxic chemicals in the environment." Here, "accumulation" matches "build up," "poisonous substances" matches "toxic chemicals," and being one of the "new ones" shows it is a relatively modern problem.
Q32 NO But the seriousness of these current environmental problems is vigorously debated Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that people strongly argue and disagree about how bad or dangerous today's environmental issues really are.
Answer Explanation:
The answer choice "NO" means that the statement in the question directly contradicts or disagrees with the information given by the writer in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NO because the statement says people generally agree that environmental threats are very serious, but the passage states that the seriousness of these problems is "vigorously debated." Since people are arguing and debating about how serious the risks are—asking if they are exaggerated or underestimated—there is no general agreement.
Q33 NOT GIVEN But there are also differences between the modem world and its problems, and those past societies and their problems. Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage discusses the differences between today's world and past societies altogether, but it never mentions if certain past societies were more like present-day societies than other past societies were.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the text does not give enough information to know if the writer agrees or disagrees with the statement.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because the passage only discusses how past societies in general differ from our modern world. It does not compare different past societies with each other to say whether some of them were more similar to present-day society than others. Since this comparison is missing, the writer's opinion cannot be known.
Q34 YES Perhaps we can still learn from the past, but only if we think carefully about its lessons Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that we can still learn from history, but only if we pay close attention and think deeply about the lessons it gives us.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the writer agrees we must be cautious when comparing history with our modern world.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because the writer states that we must think carefully about history's lessons rather than expecting simple, direct answers. The writer highlights that there are key differences between past societies and our society today, so we need to be cautious. The phrase "think carefully about its lessons" directly supports the idea that we should be careful when comparing the past and present.
Q35 C The scales of the ruins testify to the former wealth and power of their builders Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the large size of the old buildings proves how rich and powerful the people who built them used to be.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that proof showing how grand or powerful past civilisations were can still be seen today as real, physical buildings or ruins.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the passage explains that large broken buildings left behind by old societies show how rich and powerful they once were. The word "ruins" refers to "physical structures", and "testify to the former wealth and power" means providing evidence of their past greatness.
Q36 A Writers find it tempting to draw analogies between the course of human societies and the course of individual human lives - to talk of a society’s birth, growth, peak, old age and eventual death. But that metaphor proves erroneous for many past societies: they declined rapidly after reaching peak numbers and power, and those rapid declines must have come as a surprise and shock to their citizens Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that people often compare a society's life to a human's life (birth, growth, old age, death). However, this comparison is wrong ('erroneous') for many past societies because they fell quickly after reaching their strongest point.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that comparing a person's life to the history of a society is not always true or correct.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph D mentions that writers like to compare the life of a person to the life of a society, but this idea is wrong ('erroneous') for many societies in history. In this text, 'analogies' means comparisons or parallels, and 'erroneous' means incorrect or not valid.
Q37 F These environmental problems include the same eight that undermined past societies, plus four new ones: human-caused climate change, build up of toxic chemicals in the environment, energy shortages, and full human utilisation of the Earth’s photosynthetic capacity Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that present-day environmental problems include the eight issues from the past plus four extra new ones.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "F" means that there are more nature-related problems today than there were in the past.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because the text states that today's environmental issues consist of the same eight problems that affected past human groups, along with four new ones. Since there are four extra problems now, the total number of environmental issues facing societies today is higher than in the past. Keywords like "eight" and "four new ones" show this increase.
Q38 D We differ from past societies in some respects that put us at lower risk than them; some of those respects often mentioned include our powerful technology (ie its beneficial effects), globalisation, modem medicine, and greater knowledge of past societies and of distant modem societies. We also differ from past societies in some respects that put us at greater risk than them: again, our potent technology (ie its unintended destructive effects), Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that our technology can protect us because it brings good benefits, but at the same time, it can put us in danger because of its unwanted harmful effects.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that technology can have good effects as well as bad effects.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Paragraph F mentions that our powerful technology has 'beneficial effects' (positive aspects) that lower our risk, but it also has 'unintended destructive effects' (negative aspects) that put us at greater risk. Therefore, technology can be both helpful and harmful.
Q39 E We shouldn’t be so naive as to think that study of the past will yield simple solutions, directly transferable to our societies today Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that we should not be foolish enough to expect that learning about history will give us simple answers that we can use directly for modern society.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that looking at history and past human developments will not give us easy or immediate answers to solve today's issues.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because paragraph F cautions us against believing that looking at history ("study of the past") will easily give us fixes ("yield simple solutions") that can be directly applied to our current world ("transferable to our societies today"). Therefore, studying historical events does not offer direct answers for present-day problems.
Q40 A Perhaps there are some practical lessons that we could learn from all those past collapses. But there are also differences between the modem world and its problems, and those past societies and their problems. Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that while we can learn from past societies because of their similarities to us, there are also important differences between the modern world and past societies.
Answer Explanation:
The answer choice A means that societies long ago and societies today share some things in common, but they also have key differences.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the whole text compares past societies with our world today. The author explains that we share similar environmental problems with the past, but also points out that modern society has unique differences that can make us either safer or at greater risk. This balance of similarities and differences forms the main idea of the passage.
