Reading Passage

MENTAL GYMNASTICS

A THE working day has just started at the head office of Barclays Bank in London. Seventeen staff are helping themselves to a buffet breakfast as young psychologist Sebastian Bailey enters the room to begin the morning's training session. But this is no ordinary training session. He's not here to sharpen their finance or management skills. He's here to exercise their brains.

B Today’s workout, organised by a company called the Mind Gym in London, is entitled "having presence". What follows is an intense 90-minute session in which this rather abstract concept is gradually broken down into a concrete set of feelings, mental tricks and behaviours. At one point the bankers are instructed to shut their eyes and visualise themselves filling the room and then the building. They finish up by walking around the room acting out various levels of presence, from low-key to over the top.

C It's easy to poke fun. Yet similar mental workouts are happening in corporate seminar rooms around the globe. The Mind Gym alone offers some 70 different sessions, including ones on mental stamina, creativity for logical thinkers and "zoom learning". Other outfits draw more directly on the exercise analogy, offering "neurobics" courses with names like "brain sets" and "cerebral fitness". Then there are books with titles like Pumping Ions, full of brainteasers that claim to "flex your mind", and software packages offering memory and spatial awareness games.

D But whatever the style, the companies' sales pitch is invariably the same—follow our routines to shape and sculpt your brain or mind, just as you might tone and train your body. And, of course, they nearly all claim that their mental workouts draw on serious scientific research and thinking into how the brain works.

E One outfit, Brainergy of Cambridge, Massachusetts (motto: "Because your grey matter matters") puts it like this: "Studies have shown that mental exercise can cause changes in brain anatomy and brain chemistry which promote increased mental efficiency and clarity. The neuroscience is cutting-edge." And on its website, Mind Gym trades on a quote from Susan Greenfield, one of Britain's best known neuroscientists: "It's a bit like going to the gym, if you exercise your brain it will grow."

F Indeed, the Mind Gym originally planned to hold its sessions in a local health club, until its founders realised where the real money was to be made. Modern companies need flexible, bright thinkers and will seize on anything that claims to create them, especially if it looks like a quick fix backed by science. But are neurobic workouts really backed by science? And do we need them?

G Nor is there anything remotely high-tech about what Lawrence Katz, co-author of Keep Your Brain Alive, recommends. Katz, a neurobiologist at Duke University Medical School in North Carolina, argues that just as many of us fail to get enough physical exercise, so we also lack sufficient mental stimulation to keep our brain in trim. Sure we are busy with jobs, family and housework. But most of this activity is repetitive routine. And any leisure time is spent slumped in front of the TV.

H So, read a book upside down. Write or brush your teeth with your wrong hand. Feel your way around the room with your eyes shut. Sniff vanilla essence while listening intently to orchestral music. Anything, says Katz, to break your normal mental routine. It will help invigorate your brain, encouraging its cells to make new connections and pump out neurotrophins, substances that feed and sustain brain circuits.

I Well, up to a point it will. "What I'm really talking about is brain maintenance rather than bulking up your IQ," Katz adds. Neurobics, in other words, is about letting your brain fulfill its potential. It cannot create super-brains. Can it achieve even that much, though? Certainly the brain is an organ that can adapt to the demands placed on it. Tests on animal brain tissue, for example, have repeatedly shown that electrically stimulating the synapses that connect nerve cells thought to be crucial to learning and reasoning, makes them stronger and more responsive. Brain scans suggest we use a lot more of our grey matter when carrying out new or strange tasks than when we're doing well-rehearsed ones. Rats raised in bright cages with toys sprout more neural connections than rats raised in bare cages—suggesting perhaps that novelty and variety could be crucial to a developing brain, says Katz. And neurologists have proved time and again that people who lose brain cells suddenly during a stroke often sprout new connections to compensate for the loss—especially if they undergo extensive therapy to overcome any paralysis.

J Guy Claxton, an educational psychologist at the University of Bristol, dismisses most of the neurological approaches as "neuro-babble". Nevertheless, there are specific mental skills we can learn, he contends. Desirable attributes such as creativity, mental flexibility, and even motivation, are not the fixed faculties that most of us think. They are thought habits that can be learned. The problem, says Claxton, is that most of us never get proper training in these skills. We develop our own private set of mental strategies for tackling tasks and never learn anything explicitly. Worse still, because any learned skill—even driving a car or brushing our teeth-quickly sinks out of consciousness, we can no longer see the very thought habits we're relying upon. Our mental tools become invisible to us.

K Claxton is the academic adviser to the Mind Gym. So not surprisingly, the company espouses his solution-that we must return our thought patterns to a conscious level, becoming aware of the details of how we usually think. Only then can we start to practise better thought patterns, until eventually these become our new habits. Switching metaphors, picture not gym classes, but tennis or football coaching.

L In practice, the training can seem quite mundane. For example, in one of the eight different creativity workouts offered by the Mind Gym—entitled "creativity for logical thinkers" - one of the mental strategies taught is to make a sensible suggestion, then immediately pose its opposite. So, asked to spend five minutes inventing a new pizza, a group soon comes up with no topping, sweet topping, cold topping, price based on time of day, flat-rate prices and so on.

M Bailey agrees that the trick is simple. But it is surprising how few such tricks people have to call upon when they are suddenly asked to be creative: "They tend to just label themselves as uncreative, not realising that there are techniques that every creative person employs." Bailey says the aim is to introduce people to half a dozen or so such strategies in a session so that what at first seems like a dauntingly abstract mental task becomes a set of concrete, learnable behaviours. He admits this is not a short cut to genius. Neurologically, some people do start with quicker circuits or greater handling capacity. However, with the right kind of training he thinks we can dramatically increase how efficiently we use it.

N It is hard to prove that the training itself is effective. How do you measure a change in an employee's creativity levels, or memory skills? But staff certainly report feeling that such classes have opened their eyes. So, neurological boosting or psychological training? At the moment you can pay your money and take your choice. Claxton for one believes there is no reason why schools and universities shouldn't spend more time teaching basic thinking skills, rather than trying to stuff heads with facts and hoping that effective thought habits are somehow absorbed by osmosis.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q1 NO At one point the bankers are instructed to shut their eyes and visualise themselves filling the room and then the building Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that during the exercise, the workers were asked to close their eyes and picture their presence expanding to fill the whole room and the building, not to pretend that they were the building.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NO" means that the statement directly contradicts or goes against what the text says.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NO because the coach did not tell the employees to imagine that they were the building itself. Instead, the passage states that the bankers were told to "visualise themselves filling the room and then the building." Because picturing oneself filling a space is different from imagining that one is the structure itself, the statement is false.
Q2 YES But whatever the style, the companies' sales pitch is invariably the same—follow our routines to shape and sculpt your brain or mind, just as you might tone and train your body Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that all these brain-training companies, including the Mind Gym, share the exact same marketing message: practice their exercises to train your brain in the same way you exercise your physical body.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "YES" means that the statement correctly matches the facts given in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because Paragraph D explains that no matter the style, the companies offering mental workouts all use essentially the same sales pitch (marketing method): encouraging people to train their minds just like training a physical body. The Mind Gym is one of these companies, so it uses the same general marketing idea as the others. Keywords like "sales pitch" correspond to "marketing theory", and "invariably the same" matches "similar [...] that is used all round".
Q3 NO And on its website, Mind Gym trades on a quote from Susan Greenfield, one of Britain's best known neuroscientists: "It's a bit like going to the gym, if you exercise your brain it will grow." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that Mind Gym uses a quote on its website from Susan Greenfield, identifying her as a famous British brain scientist, not the creator or founder of Mind Gym.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NO" means that the statement is false according to the passage.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NO because the passage explains that Susan Greenfield is "one of Britain's best known neuroscientists" whom Mind Gym quotes on its website, not the founder of the company. Therefore, stating that she is the founder contradicts the information in the text.
Q4 NO Yet similar mental workouts are happening in corporate seminar rooms around the globe. The Mind Gym alone offers some 70 different sessions, including ones on mental stamina, creativity for logical thinkers and "zoom learning". Other outfits draw more directly on the exercise analogy, offering "neurobics" courses with names like "brain sets" and "cerebral fitness" Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that mental workouts take place in company seminar rooms worldwide, but many different providers ("other outfits") offer these services, not just Mind Gym, and it does not say that all companies use them.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NO" means the statement is false according to the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "NO" because the statement claims that "all" businesses and industries globally use Mind Gym's sessions. However, the text only states that "similar mental workouts are happening in corporate seminar rooms around the globe" and mentions that "other outfits" also provide different brain training courses. Saying that every single business and industry uses Mind Gym contradicts the passage.
Q5 YES And on its website, Mind Gym trades on a quote from Susan Greenfield, one of Britain's best known neuroscientists: "It's a bit like going to the gym, if you exercise your brain it will grow." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says Mind Gym's website uses a quote from a famous brain scientist, Susan Greenfield. She says exercising your brain makes it grow.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the statement is true: Mind Gym says science strongly supports its training sessions.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES. Paragraph D says nearly all these companies, Mind Gym included, claim their workouts draw on 'serious scientific research.' Paragraph E shows Mind Gym using a neuroscientist's quote to support this. The key words are 'claim', 'scientific research' and 'neuroscientist'. The statement says 'According to Mind Gym', so it only needs what Mind Gym claims, not whether the claim is true.
Q6 D Katz, a neurobiologist at Duke University Medical School in North Carolina, argues that just as many of us fail to get enough physical exercise, so we also lack sufficient mental stimulation to keep our brain in trim Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that, according to Katz, just as we often do not get enough body exercise, we also do not get enough mental challenge or stimulation to keep our minds fit.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" refers to Lawrence Katz, who believes that people do not receive enough mental stimulation or inspiration to keep their brains healthy and active.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because in Paragraph G, Lawrence Katz argues that many people do not get enough mental stimulation to keep their brains in good shape. In the text, the phrase "lack sufficient mental stimulation to keep our brain in trim" directly matches the idea in the statement "do not have enough inspiration to keep our brain fit".
Q7 C And on its website, Mind Gym trades on a quote from Susan Greenfield, one of Britain's best known neuroscientists: "It's a bit like going to the gym, if you exercise your brain it will grow." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that neuroscientist Susan Greenfield said that working out your brain is like going to a fitness gym, and exercising it causes it to grow.
Answer Explanation:
The answer C means Susan Greenfield is the person who compared brain exercise to going to the gym and said that exercising the brain helps it grow.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because paragraph E directly attributes this idea to Susan Greenfield. The text quotes her saying: "It's a bit like going to the gym, if you exercise your brain it will grow." This matches the statement in the question exactly.
Q8 D "What I'm really talking about is brain maintenance rather than bulking up your IQ," Katz adds Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Katz views brain workouts as a way to maintain and take care of the brain, not to make a person's general intelligence or IQ score bigger.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is D (Lawrence Katz).
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because in paragraph I, Lawrence Katz explains that brain exercise is about "brain maintenance" (keeping your brain healthy) rather than "bulking up your IQ" (making your IQ score higher).
Q9 A Claxton for one believes there is no reason why schools and universities shouldn't spend more time teaching basic thinking skills, rather than trying to stuff heads with facts and hoping that effective thought habits are somehow absorbed by osmosis Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Guy Claxton thinks schools and colleges should dedicate more time to teaching how to think, instead of just filling students' heads with facts.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "A" refers to Guy Claxton.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Guy Claxton thinks schools and universities should teach thinking skills directly instead of simply filling students' minds with facts. In the passage, "basic thinking skills" matches the idea of learning how to think and create, while "trying to stuff heads with facts" matches teaching basic known knowledge.
Q10 D Katz, And neurologists have proved time and again that people who lose brain cells suddenly during a stroke often sprout new connections to compensate for the loss—especially if they undergo extensive therapy to overcome any paralysis Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Katz and brain doctors have shown that stroke patients who lose brain cells can grow new brain connections to make up for the damage, especially when they receive a lot of therapy.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" means that the person who showed or stated this idea is Lawrence Katz.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because in Paragraph I, the passage mentions that Katz and neurologists showed that when individuals lose brain cells due to a stroke, they can grow new neural pathways, particularly if they receive medical treatment like therapy. In this context, "lose brain cells" matches "lose old connections", "sprout new connections" matches "develop new neuron connections", and "undergo extensive therapy" matches "via certain treatment". Therefore, this statement matches Lawrence Katz.
Q11 B "They tend to just label themselves as uncreative, not realising that there are techniques that every creative person employs." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Sebastian Bailey explaining that individuals usually consider themselves uncreative without understanding that creative people simply use specific methods and tricks.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means that Sebastian Bailey is the person who stated that people often call themselves uncreative because they do not realize that there are methods anyone can use to be creative.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because in paragraph M, psychologist Sebastian Bailey explains that when people are required to be creative, they frequently "label themselves as uncreative" because they do not know that "techniques" (approaches or methods) exist for being creative. Matching keywords include: "mark themselves as not creative" matching "label themselves as uncreative", and "approaches" matching "techniques".
Q12 B young psychologist Sebastian Bailey enters the room to begin the morning's training session. But this is no ordinary training session. He's not here to sharpen their finance or management skills. He's here to exercise their brains
Today’s workout, organised by a company called the Mind Gym in London		 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Sebastian Bailey ran a training session to help employees exercise their brains, and this mental workout was organised by the organization called the Mind Gym.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B refers to Sebastian Bailey.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because the passage describes Sebastian Bailey leading a training session to exercise the brains of bank staff (employees). The session is a workout arranged by the company named the Mind Gym. Keywords in the text include "psychologist Sebastian Bailey", "exercise their brains", and "workout, organised by a company called the Mind Gym".
Q13 A The problem, says Claxton, is that most of us never get proper training in these skills Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that, according to Claxton, the issue is that the majority of people are never properly taught or trained in how to use these mental skills.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "A" means that Guy Claxton is the person who stated that most people do not receive proper training in thinking skills.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because in Paragraph J, Guy Claxton discusses how mental abilities like creativity and mental flexibility are skills and habits that can be taught. He points out that the problem is that most people never receive "proper training in these skills." Here, "most of US" matches "majority of people," and "proper training in these skills" matches "appropriate skills-training for brain."
