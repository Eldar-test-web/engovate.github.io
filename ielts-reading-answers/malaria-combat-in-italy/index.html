Malaria Combat In Italy - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

From IELTS Mock Tests Academic Reading Test 29 · Part 2 · Questions 14–27

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14-27 which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

Malaria Combat in Italy

A

Mal-aria. Bad air. Even the word is Italian, and this horrible disease marked the life of those in the peninsula for thousands of years. Giuseppe Garibaldi’s wife died of the disease, as did the country’s first prime minister, Cavour, in 1861. Yet by 1962, Italy was officially declared malaria-free, and it has remained so ever since. Frank Snowden’s study of this success story is a remarkable piece of historical work. Original, crystal-clear, analytical and passionate, Snowden (who has previously written about cholera) takes us to areas historians have rarely visited before.

B

Everybody now knows that malaria is carried by mosquitoes. Malaria has always been the subject of research for medical practitioners from time immemorial. However, many ancient texts, especially medical literature, mention various aspects of malaria and even of its possible link with mosquitoes and insects. Early man, confronting the manifestations of malaria, attributed the fevers to supernatural influences: evil spirits, angered deities, or the black magic of sorcerers. But in the 19th century, most experts believed that the disease was not produced by unclean air (“miasma” or “poisoning of the air”). Two Americans, Josiah Clark Nott and Lewis Daniel Beauperthy, echoed Crawford’s ideas. Nott in his essay “Yellow Fever Contrasted with Bilious Fever,” published in 1850, dismissed the miasma theory as worthless, arguing that microscopic insects somehow transmitted by mosquitoes caused both malaria and yellow fever. Others made a link between swamps, water and malaria, but did not make the further leap towards insects. The consequences of these theories were that little was done to combat the disease before the end of the century. Things became so bad that 11m Italians (from a total population of 25m) were “permanently at risk”. In malarial zones, the life expectancy of land workers was a terrifying 22.5 years. Those who escaped death were weakened or suffered from splenomegaly – a “painful enlargement of the spleen” and “a lifeless stare”. The economic impact of the disease was immense. Epidemics were blamed on southern Italians, given the widespread belief that malaria was hereditary. In the 1880s, such theories began to collapse as the dreaded mosquito was identified as the real culprit.

C

Italian scientists, drawing on the pioneering work of French doctor Alphonse Laveran, were able to predict the cycles of fever but it was in Rome that further key discoveries were made. Giovanni Battista Grassi, a naturalist, found that a particular type of mosquito was the carrier of malaria. By experimenting on healthy volunteers (mosquitoes were released into rooms where they drank the blood of the human guinea pigs), Grassi was able to make the direct link between the insects (all females of a certain kind) and the disease. Soon, doctors and scientists made another startling discovery: the mosquitoes themselves were also infected and not mere carriers. Every year, during the mosquito season, malarial blood was moved around the population by the insects. Definitive proof of these new theories was obtained after an extraordinary series of experiments in Italy, where healthy people were introduced into malarial zones but kept free of mosquito bites – and remained well. The new Italian state had the necessary information to tackle the disease.

D

A complicated approach was adopted, which made use of quinine – a drug obtained from tree bark which had long been used to combat fever but was now seen as a crucial part of the war on malaria. Italy introduced a quinine law and a quinine tax in 1904, and the drug was administered to large numbers of rural workers. Despite its often terrible side-effects (the headaches produced were known as the “quinine-buzz”), the drug was successful in limiting the spread of the disease, and in breaking cycles of infection. In addition, Italy set up rural health centres and invested heavily in education programmes. Malaria, as Snowden shows, was not just a medical problem, but a social and regional issue, and could only be defeated through multi-layered strategies. Politics was itself transformed by the anti-malarial campaigns.

E

It was originally decided to give quinine to all those in certain regions – even healthy people; peasants were often suspicious of the medicine being forced upon them. Doctors were sometimes met with hostility and refusal, and many were dubbed “poisoners”. Despite these problems, the strategy was hugely successful. Deaths from malaria fell by some 80% in the first decade of the 20th century and some areas escaped altogether from the scourge of the disease.

F

Shamefully, the Italian malaria expert Alberto Missiroli had a role to play in the disaster: he did not distribute quinine, despite being well aware of the epidemic to come. Snowden claims that Missiroli was already preparing a new strategy – with the support of the US Rockefeller Foundation – using a new pesticide, DDT. Missiroli allowed the epidemic to spread, in order to create the ideal conditions for a massive, and lucrative, human experiment. Fifty-five thousand cases of malaria were recorded in the province of Littoria alone in 1944. It is estimated that more than a third of those in the affected area contracted the disease. Thousands, nobody knows how many, died.

G

With the war over, the US government and the Rockefeller Foundation were free to experiment. DDT was sprayed from the air and 3m Italians had their bodies covered with the chemical. The effects were dramatic, and nobody really cared about the toxic effects of the chemical. By 1962, malaria was more or less gone from the whole peninsula. The last cases were noted in a poor region of Sicily. One of the final victims to die of the disease in Italy was the popular cyclist, Fausto Coppi. He had contracted malaria in Africa in 1960, and the failure of doctors in the north of Italy to spot the disease was a sign of the times. A few decades earlier, they would have immediately noticed the tell-tale signs; it was later claimed that a small dose of quinine would have saved his life.

H

As there are still more than 1m deaths every year from malaria worldwide, Snowden’s book also has contemporary relevance. This is a disease that affects every level of the societies where it is rampant. As Snowden writes: “In Italy, malaria undermined agricultural productivity, decimated the army, destroyed communities and left families impoverished.” The economic miracle of the 50s and 60s which made Italy into a modern industrial nation would not have been possible without the eradication of malaria. Moreover, this book convincingly argues that the disease was “an integral part of the big picture of modern Italian history”. This magnificent study, beautifully written and impeccably documented, deserves an audience beyond specialists in history, or in Italy. It also provides us with “a message of hope for a world struggling with the great present-day medical emergency”.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q14 insects However, many ancient texts, especially medical literature, mention various aspects of malaria and even of its possible link with mosquitoes and insects Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that old medical books and writings talked about malaria and suggested that mosquitoes and other insects could be connected to the disease.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "insects" refers to small creepy-crawly creatures or bugs, which ancient medical writings suggested might be connected to spreading malaria.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph B of the text. The summary states that it was "recorded in words" that certain creatures, "including mosquito," might be the cause. In the text, the phrase "recorded in words" corresponds to "ancient texts, especially medical literature, mention", and the text explicitly talks about the disease's "possible link with mosquitoes and insects." Therefore, the missing word is "insects."
Q15 unclean air But in the 19th century, most experts believed that the disease was not produced by unclean air (“miasma” or “poisoning of the air”) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in the 1800s, experts started to believe that malaria was not caused by dirty air, which was also called "miasma".
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to dirty or polluted air.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "unclean air" because Paragraph B explains that the "miasma" theory referred to "unclean air" (or "poisoning of the air"). The summary mentions that the miasma theory connected malaria to this factor, which matches the passage's explanation defining "miasma" as "unclean air".
Q16 hereditary Epidemics were blamed on southern Italians, given the widespread belief that malaria was hereditary Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that people held southern Italians responsible for outbreaks of the disease because of a common belief that malaria ran in their families.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "hereditary" means passed from parents to their children through family genes or birth.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "hereditary" because the passage explains that people blamed malaria epidemics on southern Italians because they believed the sickness was passed down in families (hereditary). In the summary, the sentence says southern Italians were blamed and describes what people thought malaria was to them ("to whom malaria was hereditary"). The summary words directly match the passage keywords "blamed on southern Italians" and "widespread belief".
Q17 life expectancy In malarial zones, the life expectancy of land workers was a terrifying 22.5 years Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in places where malaria was common, farm laborers lived for an average of only 22.5 years, which was shockingly low.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "life expectancy" means the average number of years a person is expected to live.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by paragraph B, which discusses the severe impact of malaria in affected regions. The summary states that in malarial areas, something belonging to rural workers became surprisingly shorter. In the text, the phrase "In malarial zones" matches "In malarial areas", "land workers" matches "rural workers", and "a terrifying 22.5 years" explains that their lifespan was shockingly short. Therefore, the exact term that fills the blank is "life expectancy".
Q18 NOT GIVEN By experimenting on healthy volunteers (mosquitoes were released into rooms where they drank the blood of the human guinea pigs), Grassi was able to make the direct link between the insects (all females of a certain kind) and the disease Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Grassi tested mosquitoes on healthy people who agreed to help with the study, but it never mentions where these people lived or came from.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the text does not contain enough information to say if the statement is true or false.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because Paragraph C mentions that Giovanni Battista Grassi tested his ideas using "healthy volunteers", but the text does not mention where these volunteers came from. Because there is no information about whether they came from all parts of Italy or just one place, we cannot confirm or deny the statement.
Q19 YES Definitive proof of these new theories was obtained after an extraordinary series of experiments in Italy, where healthy people were introduced into malarial zones but kept free of mosquito bites – and remained well Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that tests proved the theory because healthy people went into areas with malaria, were protected from mosquito bites, and stayed completely healthy.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "YES" means that the text confirms healthy individuals could stay healthy and avoid getting sick in areas with malaria as long as mosquitoes did not bite them.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because Paragraph C describes experiments showing that when healthy volunteers entered areas where malaria was common ("malarial zones") and were protected from mosquito bites ("kept free of mosquito bites"), they did not fall sick ("remained well"). This directly matches the statement that healthy people could remain safe if they avoided mosquito bites.
Q20 NO A complicated approach was adopted, which made use of quinine – a drug obtained from tree bark which had long been used to combat fever but was now seen as a crucial part of the war on malaria Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that quinine had a long history of being used to treat high body temperature (fever), but using it specifically to fight malaria was a new development at that time.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is "NO" because the statement is false according to the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NO because the statement says quinine had long been used to fight malaria, but the text says it had long been used to fight general "fever" and was only recently considered an important weapon against malaria. In paragraph D, the author explains that quinine was "a drug obtained from tree bark which had long been used to combat fever but was now seen as a crucial part of the war on malaria." Since it was historically used for fever, not malaria itself, the statement directly contradicts the passage.
Q21 YES Malaria, as Snowden shows, was not just a medical problem, but a social and regional issue, and could only be defeated through multi-layered strategies. Politics was itself transformed by the anti-malarial campaigns Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that fighting malaria was more than just a health or medical task; it was also closely connected to society and greatly influenced politics.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "YES" means that the text confirms getting rid of malaria was important for both health reasons and political reasons.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because Paragraph D states that malaria was not merely a "medical problem" and that "politics was itself transformed" by the campaigns to fight the disease. This matches the idea in the statement that defeating malaria had both "medical and political significance".
Q22 C it was in Rome that further key discoveries were made. Giovanni Battista Grassi, a naturalist, found that a particular type of mosquito was the carrier of malaria Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that important new findings were made in Rome, where a scientist named Giovanni Battista Grassi discovered that a specific kind of mosquito carries malaria.
Answer Explanation:
The answer C means that Paragraph C is the section that talks about the important discovery that mosquitoes carry malaria.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Paragraph C describes the major discovery (breakthrough) made in Rome by Giovanni Battista Grassi. The paragraph explicitly states that Grassi discovered that a specific mosquito was the carrier of the disease, directly matching the phrase in the question.
Q23 F Missiroli allowed the epidemic to spread, in order to create the ideal conditions for a massive, and lucrative, human experiment Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage clearly states that Missiroli purposely let the illness spread to set up a large scientific test on humans.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Paragraph F tells the story of an expert who purposefully let the disease outbreak expand instead of stopping it.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because it describes the Italian malaria expert Alberto Missiroli. The passage explains that Missiroli "allowed the epidemic to spread" on purpose ("in order to create the ideal conditions for a massive, and lucrative, human experiment") rather than limiting it. In this context, "scientist" matches the "malaria expert", and "intentionally failed to restrict" matches "allowed the epidemic to spread, in order to".
Q24 H This magnificent study, beautifully written and impeccably documented, deserves an audience beyond specialists in history, or in Italy. It also provides us with “a message of hope for a world struggling with the great present-day medical emergency” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that this great book should be read by people all over the world, not just Italian history experts. It gives hope to everyone in the world who is facing major health problems today.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Paragraph H is the section that explains how the story of defeating malaria in Italy is still important and meaningful for people reading it around the world today.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is H because the author discusses the modern value of the book about Italy's success against malaria. The text notes that this study has "contemporary relevance" (it is still meaningful today) because malaria still causes many deaths "worldwide". At the end of Paragraph H, the text explains that the book "deserves an audience" (should be read by readers) not just in Italy, but by people everywhere, bringing hope to a "world struggling with the great present-day medical emergency". Therefore, Paragraph H contains the idea that this success story remains relevant for readers all over the globe today.
Q25 G One of the final victims to die of the disease in Italy was the popular cyclist, Fausto Coppi Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Fausto Coppi, a famous bicycle racer, was one of the last people in Italy who died from malaria.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Paragraph G talks about an individual who was among the last people to lose their life to malaria in Italy.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is G because this paragraph details the final stages of eliminating the disease in Italy and names a specific person who was among the last to die from it. The text identifies the famous cyclist Fausto Coppi as 'one of the final victims to die of the disease in Italy'. The phrase 'final victims to die' directly corresponds to 'final cases reported dying' in the question.
Q26 D Despite its often terrible side-effects (the headaches produced were known as the “quinine-buzz”), the drug was successful in limiting the spread of the disease, and in breaking cycles of infection Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even though the medicine caused bad health problems such as bad headaches, it still worked very well to stop the disease from spreading.
Answer Explanation:
The answer identifies Paragraph D as the section of the text that talks about the bad physical reactions caused by a medicine that worked very well.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Paragraph D discusses the medicine quinine and directly mentions both its bad effects and how well it worked. It refers to "terrible side-effects" like painful headaches (called the "quinine-buzz"), which matches "the negative symptoms". At the same time, it explains that "the drug was successful in limiting the spread of the disease", which matches "the highly effective drug".
Q27 B Early man, confronting the manifestations of malaria, attributed the fevers to supernatural influences: evil spirits, angered deities, or the black magic of sorcerers Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in early human history, people guessed that the fevers of malaria came from supernatural forces, such as evil spirits, angry gods, or sorcery.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means that paragraph B gives a list of guesses and unproven beliefs about what caused malaria.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because paragraph B provides several speculative hypotheses (unproven guesses or theories) from history regarding the cause of malaria. These include supernatural causes ("evil spirits, angered deities, or the black magic of sorcerers"), unclean air ("miasma"), swamps and water, and the belief that malaria was "hereditary". These different ideas correspond directly to a cited list of speculative hypotheses.
