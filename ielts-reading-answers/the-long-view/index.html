Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 27–40, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

The long view

Climate change, moon bases, cybercrime – we all know the predictions, but how are they made? One expert explains the science of futurology

The business of predicting the future has a long history. Several millennia ago in ancient Greece, people would consult Apollo, the god of prophecy, to learn if fortune favoured their chosen course of action. The tradition has changed considerably since then. Today, futurology is a science that drives a thriving business – its three main consumers being the military, big business, and national governments. Modern futurists do not predict that a certain event will occur at a particular time. Instead, they look at what is happening now to see what this may lead to further down the line. Alternatively, they start with a desired result and work out how people and organisations can be manoeuvred into realizing that ambition.

The oil company Shell has been one of the major producers of future studies. It recently published a comprehensive study looking at how people may live in 2020 and 2050, given the type of energy which is purchased. Philip Watts from Shell says, “This is not an exercise in prophecy. Rather, it is designed to challenge our thinking so that we can make better choices in today’s business world.”

The short-term attitudes and bureaucracy of many governments can be frustrating to futurists, but this mutual wariness is slowly changing. The Scottish Parliament recently hosted a conference of 150 futurists from across the globe. The parliament is setting up a forum to look at where Scotland is heading in the next 20 years. Among the issues are demographics and economic competition within Europe and with Asia. It’s a first for the United Kingdom, although not for Europe - this strategy has already been put into practice in Finland.

Eamonn Kelly, head of the San Francisco-based Global Business Network futurist group, was one of those who attended the conference. According to Kelly, “There are many different tools a futurist can employ but essentially there are three main ingredients. First, there is the “science”, which can encompass everything from environmental studies to psychology. Then, there is the “craft”, which may include interviews and the creation of maps of cause and effect. Finally, there is the “art”, the intuitive ability to meld all this into a coherent whole.

Dr James Canton is the founder of the San Francisco-based Institute for Global Futures and has been an adviser to leading US companies. Like many futurists, he started in the private sector but is also comfortable in academia and has an urge to change things for the better. “A futurist is an alchemist who mixes the qualitative with the quantitative,” he says. He needs intuition and has to work with people from a range of academic backgrounds, gathering primary evidence about what motivates people. Futurists are constantly discussing ideas with their peers, but they do not simply surf the net picking up on the latest fad.

New Zealand futurist Robin Gunston says, “I don't believe you can train yourself to become a futurist. A certain amount of this is to do with how your brain is hard-wired, and we know this is difficult to change. Futurology requires extensive life experience and a broad education to test theories against. Much of modern teenage culture is about me, what I want, and what the world owes me – that does not make it easy to see different kinds of futures and pose the hard questions about how we will get there.”

Those best able to respond to futurists are the most agile. “Large corporations beholden to shareholders and governments burdened by bureaucracy are often simply too slow. Asian companies tend to be better prepared because they take a longer perspective. Sony, for example, is very forward-looking,” Canton says. “There will be sunset and sunrise nations and corporations, in terms of prosperity in the 21st century. The winners will be those corporations and governments which conduct future studies and take steps to become future-proofed.”

According to Alan Beckley, a former West Mercia chief inspector and past president of the Police Futurists International (PFI), getting public bodies in the UK future-proofed could be a long process. “People are concerned about the methodology and the reliability of future studies,‟ he says. They are more likely to follow their political masters than go to them and say, “We have done this scenario, and this is what we should do”. However, as the Scottish Parliament has shown, there are a few examples of government exercises. Also, in 2003, Britain’s Foreign and Commonwealth Office (FCO) published for the first time its strategic thinking for the next decade. This document was drawn up with the help of in-house and outside futurists. FCO minister Bill Rammell says,” We do occasionally look further ahead than the next ten years on issues such as demographics. But the period chosen for the strategy was deliberately a decade. Even assumptions about technology get very shaky beyond that.

American futurist Steve Brant stresses the importance of remembering that the road ahead has yet to be chosen. “People can create the future they want using various points of leverage,” he says. “We have much more influence over what the future will be like than many of us realise”. Futurists used to be like firefighters – they tried to work out what to do if things went wrong. Now, the aim of this profession is to imagine the best possible future for society and start creating it today.

Questions 38–40 Three Choices

Choose THREE letters A-G.

38 39 40 Which THREE of the following statements are true according to the passage?
  1. Present-day futurology can focus on achieving a particular outcome
  2. Futurists recognise that most governments have already made long-term plans
  3. The world's first futurology conference was hosted by Finland.
  4. Futurists need to talk to people from various areas of expertise.
  5. Futurists should carry out internet research to keep up with popular trends.
  6. Aspects of modern culture prepare people well for a career in futurology.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q27 B Those best able to respond to futurists are the most agile. “Large corporations beholden to shareholders and governments burdened by bureaucracy are often simply too slow. Asian companies tend to be better prepared because they take a longer perspective. Sony, for example, is very forward-looking,” Canton says Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that organizations able to move quickly and adapt easily do the best job using futurists' ideas, while slow groups struggle to react fast enough.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means that Dr James Canton believes organizations that can adapt quickly and easily are better at using the work of futurists.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Dr James Canton points out that organizations that can respond best to futurists are the "most agile". In this context, the word "agile" is a direct synonym for "flexible", and "respond to futurists" matches the idea of making use of futurology.
Q28 E FCO minister Bill Rammell says,” We do occasionally look further ahead than the next ten years on issues such as demographics. But the period chosen for the strategy was deliberately a decade. Even assumptions about technology get very shaky beyond that Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Bill Rammell explaining that they purposely planned for ten years (a decade) because guessing what will happen after that time becomes too uncertain and unreliable.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "E" means that Bill Rammell is the person associated with the idea in the statement.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because the passage quotes FCO minister Bill Rammell explaining why they plan for only a decade (ten years). He explains that guesses and predictions about things like technology "get very shaky" (become unreliable and cannot be trusted) when looking further than ten years into the future. Therefore, his words match the statement that predictions beyond ten years cannot be trusted.
Q29 C New Zealand futurist Robin Gunston says, “I don't believe you can train yourself to become a futurist. A certain amount of this is to do with how your brain is hard-wired, and we know this is difficult to change Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Robin Gunston explaining that you cannot just learn or practice to be a futurist, because part of it comes from how your brain is naturally wired from birth, which cannot easily be changed.
Answer Explanation:
The answer C means that Robin Gunston is the person who stated that not everyone can become a futurist, because it requires specific natural qualities and deep experience.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Robin Gunston explains that a person cannot simply train to become a futurist. He points out that part of being a futurist depends on how a person's brain is naturally built ("how your brain is hard-wired"), which is hard to alter, along with having wide life experience and education. This matches the idea that only particular or certain individuals are suited for this job.
Q30 A Philip Watts from Shell says, “This is not an exercise in prophecy. Rather, it is designed to challenge our thinking so that we can make better choices in today’s business world.” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Philip Watts believes the study of the future helps people think more clearly so they can make better business choices right now.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "A" means that Philip Watts is the person who expressed this idea.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because in the second paragraph, Philip Watts explains that their future study is created to help people think differently and make "better choices in today’s business world." In this statement, "choices" matches "decisions", "today's" matches "current", and "business" matches "commercial". Therefore, his quote directly means that future studies help guide today's business choices.
Q31 B Futurists are constantly discussing ideas with their peers, but they do not simply surf the net picking up on the latest fad Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that futurists always talk about ideas with other experts, but they do not just follow every temporary trend or short-term popular idea online.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is B because Dr James Canton is the person associated with the statement.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because the passage describes Dr James Canton's perspective on futurology. He explains that futurists regularly share thoughts with others ("constantly discussing ideas with their peers", matching "a broad exchange of information"), but they avoid blindly following short-lived trends ("do not simply surf the net picking up on the latest fad", matching "not influenced by every new idea"). Therefore, this statement matches Dr James Canton.
Q32 F “We have much more influence over what the future will be like than many of us realise” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage means that human beings actually have a lot of power to shape how tomorrow turns out, even though most people do not understand or notice this fact.
Answer Explanation:
The answer F refers to Steve Brant.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because in the final paragraph, American futurist Steve Brant explains that individuals have far greater power to shape the future than they think. The phrase 'People are not aware' matches 'than many of us realise', and 'the extent to which they can determine the future' matches having 'much more influence over what the future will be like'.
Q33 D According to Alan Beckley, a former West Mercia chief inspector and past president of the Police Futurists International (PFI), getting public bodies in the UK future-proofed could be a long process. “People are concerned about the methodology and the reliability of future studies,‟ he says Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that, according to Alan Beckley, preparing public organizations for the future is slow because people doubt how accurate and dependable future studies really are.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" refers to Alan Beckley. The statement means that because people do not fully trust predictions about the future, organizations are slow or hesitant to use them.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Alan Beckley discusses why preparing UK public groups for the future takes a long time. He explains that people worry about the "reliability" (how much they can trust it) and "methodology" (the way it is done) of future studies. In other words, a lack of confidence or trust in these studies impacts how readily or widely they are adopted.
Q34 energy It recently published a comprehensive study looking at how people may live in 2020 and 2050, given the type of energy which is purchased Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Shell released a detailed report about the way people might live in future years depending on what kind of energy they buy.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "energy" refers to power sources such as oil, gas, or electricity used by people.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "energy" because the passage mentions that the oil company Shell published a study examining how people might live in the future based on the kinds of power they buy. In the sentence, "a report" matches "a comprehensive study", "future lifestyles" corresponds to "how people may live in 2020 and 2050", and "various kinds of" paraphrases "the type of".
Q35 economic competition Among the issues are demographics and economic competition within Europe and with Asia Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that one of the topics Scotland is examining for its future is business rivalry (economic competition) involving European and Asian countries.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means business or financial rivalry between countries.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "economic competition" because the text mentions that the Scottish Parliament is creating a group to look into Scotland's future over the next 20 years. One of the main challenges they want advice on is business rivalry with other nations. In the text, this is described as "economic competition within Europe and with Asia", which directly matches the sentence about advice regarding Asian and European nations.
Q36 private sector Like many futurists, he started in the private sector Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Dr James Canton began his career working in regular commercial businesses, just like many other people who study and predict the future.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to businesses and commercial companies that are not run by the government.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "private sector" because the text directly discusses Dr James Canton's background. It mentions that "like many futurists" (similar to other futurists), "he started in the private sector" (he began his career there). Therefore, "private sector" correctly completes the sentence.
Q37 demographics FCO minister Bill Rammell says,” We do occasionally look further ahead than the next ten years on issues such as demographics Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that an official from the FCO mentions they sometimes study topics like population changes (demographics) more than 10 years into the future.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means population data and trends, such as changes in the age, size, or makeup of a population.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text where FCO minister Bill Rammell explains how far ahead the department looks. In the passage, "occasionally" matches the word "sometimes" in the question, "look further ahead than the next ten years" matches "beyond 10 years", and "issues such as" corresponds to "areas like". Therefore, the missing word identifying the topic they study beyond a 10-year period is "demographics".

Q40 A / D / G Alternatively, they start with a desired result and work out how people and organisations can be manoeuvred into realizing that ambition
He needs intuition and has to work with people from a range of academic backgrounds, gathering primary evidence about what motivates people
The winners will be those corporations and governments which conduct future studies and take steps to become future-proofed		 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that: first, futurists can begin by choosing a specific goal and planning how to reach it; second, a futurist must cooperate with people educated in many different subjects; and third, businesses and governments that do research about the future will be the successful winners.
Answer Explanation:
The answer selects statements A, D, and G because each accurately matches what the text says: futurology today can focus on reaching a desired goal (A), futurists must collaborate with experts from many different fields (D), and doing future studies can determine if a business stays successful (G).
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer consists of statements A, D, and G based on three key parts of the passage:

1. For statement A: The passage states that modern futurists can "start with a desired result and work out how people and organisations can be manoeuvred into realizing that ambition." Here, "desired result" matches "particular outcome" and "realizing that ambition" matches "achieving".

2. For statement D: Dr Canton notes that a futurist "has to work with people from a range of academic backgrounds". The phrase "a range of academic backgrounds" means "various areas of expertise".

3. For statement G: Dr Canton explains that in terms of 21st-century prosperity, "The winners will be those corporations and governments which conduct future studies and take steps to become future-proofed." Being a "winner" in prosperity means continuing to be successful.
