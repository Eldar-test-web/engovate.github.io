Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14-28, which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

THE BRAINS BUSINESS

A For those of a certain age and educational background, it is hard to think of higher education without thinking of ancient institutions. Some universities are of a venerable age – the University of Bologna was founded in 1088, the University of Oxford in 1096 – and many of them have a strong sense of tradition. The truly old ones make the most of their pedigrees, and those of a more recent vintage work hard to create an aura of antiquity. Yet these tradition-loving (or -creating) institutions are currently enduring a thunderstorm of changes so fundamental that some say the very idea of the university is being challenged. Universities are experimenting with new ways of funding (most notably through student fees), forging partnerships with private companies and engaging in mergers and acquisitions. Such changes are tugging at the ivy's roots.

B This is happening for four reasons. The first is the democratisation of higher education - "massification", in the language of the educational profession. In the rich world, massification has been going on for some time. The proportion of adults with higher educational qualifications in developed countries almost doubled between 1975 and 2000, from 22% to 41%. Most of the rich countries are still struggling to digest this huge growth in numbers. Now massification is spreading to the developing world. China doubled its student population in the late 1990s, and India is trying to follow suit.

C The second reason is the rise of the knowledge economy. The world is in the grips of a "soft revolution" in which knowledge is replacing physical resources as the main driver of economic growth. Between 1985 and 1997 the contribution of knowledge-based industries to total value added increased from 51% to 59% in Germany and from 45% to 51% in Britain. The best companies are now devoting at least a third of their investment to knowledge-intensive intangibles such as R&D, licensing and marketing. Universities are among the most important engines of the knowledge economy. Not only do they produce the brain workers who man it, they also provide much of its backbone, from laboratories to libraries to computer networks.

D The third factor is globalisation. The death of distance is transforming academia just as radically as it is transforming business. The number of people from developed countries studying abroad has doubled over the past twenty years, to 1.9 million; universities are opening campuses all around the world; and a growing number of countries are trying to turn higher education into an export industry. The fourth is competition. Traditional universities are being forced to compete for students and research grants, and private companies are trying to break into a sector which they regard as the new health care. The World Bank calculates that global spending on higher education amounts to $300 billion a year, or 1% of global economic output. There are more than 80 million students worldwide, and 3.5 million people are employed to teach them or look after them.

E All this sounds as though a golden age for universities has arrived. However, inside academia, particularly in Europe, it does not feel like it. Academics complain and administrators are locked in bad-tempered exchanges with the politicians who fund them. What has gone wrong? The biggest problem is the role of the state. If more and more governments are embracing massification, few of them are willing to draw the appropriate conclusion from their enthusiasm: that they should either provide the requisite funds (as the Scandinavian countries do) or allow universities to charge realistic fees. Many governments have tried to square the circle through tighter management, but management cannot make up for lack of resources.

F So in all too much of the academic world, the writer Kingsley Amis's famous dictum that more means worse is coming to pass. Academic salaries are declining when measured against similar jobs elsewhere, and buildings and libraries are deteriorating. In mega-institutions such as the University of Rome (180000 students), the National University of Mexico (200000-plus), and Turkey's Anadolu University (530000) individual attention to students is bound to take a back seat. The innate conservatism of the academic profession does not help. The modern university was born in a very different world from the current one, a world where only a tiny minority of the population went into higher education, yet many academics have been reluctant to make any allowances for massification. Italian universities, for instance, still insist that all students undergo a viva voce examination by a full professor, lasting an average of about five minutes.

G What, if anything, can be done? Techno-utopians believe that higher education is ripe for revolution. The university, they say, is a hopelessly antiquated institution, wedded to outdated practices such as tenure and lectures, and incapable of serving a new world of mass audiences and just-in-time information. "Thirty years from now the big university campuses will be relics," says Peter Drucker, a veteran management guru. "I consider the American research university of the past 40 years to be a failure." Fortunately, in his view, help is on the way in the form of Internet tuition and for profit universities. Cultural conservatives, on the other hand, believe that the best way forward is backward. They think it is foolish to waste higher education on people who would rather study "Seinfeld" than Socrates, and disingenuous to confuse the pursuit of truth with the pursuit of profit.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q14 B The first is the democratisation of higher education - "massification", in the language of the educational profession Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that university education is becoming widely available to the public, a change that educators call "massification".
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means Paragraph B is the section that discusses higher education opening up to large numbers of people.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because the entire paragraph focuses on the idea of university education becoming available to the general public. It introduces this trend as the "democratisation of higher education" and refers to it as "massification"—which literally means turning something into a mass phenomenon. The paragraph also provides statistics showing how the number of students has dramatically grown in both rich nations and developing countries, perfectly matching the heading "Education for the masses".
Q15 G What, if anything, can be done? Techno-utopians believe that higher education is ripe for revolution. The university, they say, is a hopelessly antiquated institution, wedded to outdated practices such as tenure and lectures, and incapable of serving a new world of mass audiences and just-in-time information. "Thirty years from now the big university campuses will be relics," says Peter Drucker, a veteran management guru. "I consider the American research university of the past 40 years to be a failure." Fortunately, in his view, help is on the way in the form of Internet tuition and for profit universities. Cultural conservatives, on the other hand, believe that the best way forward is backward Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows different views on what might happen to higher education in the coming years, such as big campuses disappearing in thirty years, online classes becoming common, or universities returning to older, traditional ways.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "G" means that paragraph G is the part of the text that discusses different ideas and options for what could happen to universities in the future.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is G because the paragraph asks what can be done about the problems facing universities and explores future possibilities. It presents ideas from techno-utopians, who look ahead thirty years to online learning and for-profit universities, as well as the opposing views of cultural conservatives on the way forward. Key phrases pointing to the future include "What, if anything, can be done?", "Thirty years from now", "help is on the way", and "the best way forward".
Q16 D The third factor is globalisation. The death of distance is transforming academia just as radically as it is transforming business. The number of people from developed countries studying abroad has doubled over the past twenty years, to 1.9 million; universities are opening campuses all around the world; and a growing number of countries are trying to turn higher education into an export industry. The fourth is competition Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the world becoming more connected (globalisation) is changing education, and it also points out that universities must now fight against each other for students and funds (competition).
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" means Paragraph D is the best match for the heading "Globalisation and competition".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Paragraph D directly discusses both topics named in the heading. The paragraph begins by explaining that the third reason for big changes in universities is "globalisation" (students studying in other countries and universities opening branches worldwide). It then immediately introduces the fourth reason as "competition" (universities competing against each other for students and money). Therefore, this paragraph perfectly fits the heading.
Q17 E If more and more governments are embracing massification, few of them are willing to draw the appropriate conclusion from their enthusiasm: that they should either provide the requisite funds (as the Scandinavian countries do) or allow universities to charge realistic fees Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that while governments want more people to go to university, very few governments are willing to give universities the money they need or let them charge enough fees to pay for it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "E" means that paragraph E is the section of the text that focuses on the trouble universities have with getting enough money.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because paragraph E directly discusses money and financial support for universities. It explains that university administrators argue with the politicians who "fund" them. Furthermore, governments want more students to attend university but fail to provide the "requisite funds" (necessary money) or permit colleges to collect proper student "fees," leading to a serious "lack of resources." Therefore, paragraph E describes a funding problem.

Q23 C Universities are experimenting with new ways of funding (most notably through student fees), forging partnerships with private companies and engaging in mergers and acquisitions Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage clearly states that universities are trying out new methods to get money, and the most important example of this is making students pay fees.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that universities are dealing with big changes by asking students to pay more money to attend classes.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph A. The text explains that universities are facing a massive wave of changes. To handle this, they are trying out new methods to get money, especially by using "student fees" (charging students to study). Therefore, charging students higher fees is one of the main ways universities are responding to these changes.
Q24 C Universities are among the most important engines of the knowledge economy. Not only do they produce the brain workers who man it, they also provide much of its backbone, from laboratories to libraries to computer networks Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that universities are like main engines that power the knowledge economy, because they train the smart workers and supply necessary tools and facilities like laboratories, libraries, and computer systems.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the knowledge economy depends very strongly on universities to work and grow.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because paragraph C explains that universities are essential to the knowledge economy. The passage calls universities 'the most important engines' of this economy, pointing out that they train the workers who run it and provide its basic support, such as labs, libraries, and computers. Keywords like 'important engines' and 'backbone' correspond directly to the idea of being 'heavily reliant on'.

Q26 A / B Academics complain and administrators are locked in bad-tempered exchanges with the politicians who fund them. What has gone wrong? The biggest problem is the role of the state. If more and more governments are embracing massification, few of them are willing to draw the appropriate conclusion from their enthusiasm: that they should either provide the requisite funds (as the Scandinavian countries do) or allow universities to charge realistic fees Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that university managers are having angry arguments with the politicians who provide their money. It also explains that governments want more students to attend university, but they fail to provide the necessary money or allow universities to collect realistic fees.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that universities, particularly in Europe, are currently facing arguments between university leaders and government officials, as well as a serious shortage of money.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph E, which explains the difficulties universities face in Europe. First, choice A is correct because the text states that university "administrators" (managers) are having angry arguments ("locked in bad-tempered exchanges") with "politicians" (governments). Second, choice B is correct because the text points out that governments do not supply the "requisite funds" (necessary money) or permit realistic student fees, leading to a major lack of financial resources. Choice C is incorrect because the passage notes that governments try "tighter management," but management itself is not the problem—it simply cannot fix the lack of money.

Q28 A / C Fortunately, in his view, help is on the way in the form of Internet tuition and for profit universities. Cultural conservatives, on the other hand, believe that the best way forward is backward. They think it is foolish to waste higher education on people who would rather study "Seinfeld" than Socrates Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that reformers see hope in online learning (Internet tuition), while traditional thinkers want to go back to the past and avoid giving university degrees to just anyone, which means teaching fewer students.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the suggested solutions include using more technology (such as learning on the internet) and educating fewer university students.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph G, which describes ideas from two different groups. First, modern reformers (techno-utopians) believe online education will help solve the problem, which matches the option 'greater use of technology' through 'Internet tuition'. Second, 'cultural conservatives' think 'the best way forward is backward' and believe universities should not be wasted on everyone, which means returning to the past and 'teaching fewer students'. Meanwhile, the passage mentions a 'management guru' (Peter Drucker) as someone offering an opinion, not as someone who should be hired to teach.
