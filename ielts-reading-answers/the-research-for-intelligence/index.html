The Research for Intelligence

A In Robert Plomin's line of work, patience is essential. Plomin, a behavioral geneticist at the Institute of Psychiatry in London, wants to understand the nature of intelligence. As part of his research, he has been watching thousands of children grow up. Plomin asks the children questions such as “What do water and milk have in common?" and "In what direction does the sun set?" At first he and his colleagues quizzed the children in person or over the telephone. Today many of those children are in their early teens, and they take their tests on the Internet. In one sense, the research has been a rousing success. The children who take the tests are all twins, and throughout the study identical twins have tended to get scores closer to each other than those of non-identical twins, who in turn have closer scores than unrelated children. These results— along with similar ones from other studies— make clear to the scientists that genes have an important influence on how children score on intelligence tests.

B But Plomin wants to know more. He wants to find the specific genes that are doing the influencing. And now he has a tool for pinpointing genes that he could not have even dreamed of when he began quizzing children. Plomin and his colleagues have been scanning the genes of his subjects with a device called a micro array, a small chip that can recognize half a million distinctive snippets of DNA. The combination of this powerful tool with a huge number of children to study meant that he could detect genes that had only a tiny effect on the variation in scores.

C Still, when Plomin and his co-workers unveiled the results of their micro-array study—the biggest dragnet for intelligence-linked genes ever undertaken—they were underwhelming. The researchers found only six genetic markers that showed any sign of having an influence on the test scores. When they ran stringent statistical tests to see if the results were flukes, only one gene passed. It accounted for 0.4 percent of variation in the scores. And to cap it all off, no one knows what the gene does in the body." It's a real drag in some ways," Plomin says.

D Plomin's experience is a typical one for scientists who study intelligence. Along with using micro-arrays, they are employing brain scans and other sophisticated technologies to document some of the intricate dance steps that genes and environment take together in the development of intelligence. They are beginning to see how differences in intelligence are reflected in the structure and function of the brain. Some scientists have even begun to build a new vision of intelligence as a reflection of the ways in which information flows through the brain. But for all these advances, intelligence remains a profound mystery. "It's amazing the extent to which we know very little," says Wendy Johnson, a psychologist at the University of Minnesota.

E In some ways, intelligence is very simple. “It's something that everybody observes in others," says Eric Turkheimer of the University of Virginia. "Everybody knows that some people are smarter than others, whatever it means technically. It's something you sense in people when you talk to them." Yet that kind of gut instinct does not translate easily into a scientific definition. In 1996 the American Psychological Association issued a report on intelligence, which stated only that "individuals differ from one another in their ability to understand complex ideas, to adapt effectively to the environment, to learn from experience, to engage in various forms of reasoning, to overcome obstacles by taking thought."

F To measure these differences, psychologists in the early 1900s invented tests of various kinds of thought, such as math, spatial reasoning and verbal skills. To compare scores on one type of test to those on another, some psychologists developed standard scales of intelligence. The most familiar of them is the intelligence quotient, which is produced by setting the average score at 100. IQ scores are not arbitrary numbers, however. Psychologists can use them to make strong predictions about other features of people's lives. It is possible to make reasonably good predictions, based on IQ scores in childhood, about how well people will fare in school and in the workplace. People with high IQs even tend to live longer than average." If you have an IQ score, does that tell you everything about a person's cognitive strengths and weaknesses? No," says Richard J. Haier of the University of California, Irvine. But even a simple number has the potential to say a lot about a person. "When you go see your doctor, what's the first thing that happens? Somebody takes your blood pressure and temperature. So you get two numbers. No one would say blood pressure and temperature summarize everything about your health, but they are key numbers."

G Then what underlies an intelligence score? “It's certainly tapping something,” says Philip Shaw, a psychiatrist at the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). The most influential theory of what the score reflects is more than a century old. In 1904 psychologist Charles Spearman observed that people who did well on one kind of test tended to do well on others. The link from one score to another was not very tight, but Spearman saw enough of a connection to declare that it was the result of something he called a g factor, short for general intelligence factor. How general intelligence arose from the brain, Spearman could not say. In recent decades, scientists have searched for an answer by finding patterns in the test scores of large groups of people. Roughly speaking, there are two possible sources for these variations. Environmental influences— anything from the way children are raised by their parents to the diseases they may suffer as they develop — are one source. Genes are another. Genes may shape the brain in ways that make individuals better or worse at answering questions on intelligence tests.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q1 v And now he has a tool for pinpointing genes that he could not have even dreamed of when he began quizzing children. Plomin and his colleagues have been scanning the genes of his subjects with a device called a micro array, a small chip that can recognize half a million distinctive snippets of DNA Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the researcher now has a new, advanced electronic tool (a micro array chip) to help him search for and locate specific genes.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "v" means heading "v. An innovative appliance to improve the probe" is the best title for Paragraph B.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading v because Paragraph B focuses on a new technology used to search for intelligence genes. In the text, words like "device called a micro array" and "powerful tool" match the idea of an "appliance" or instrument. The phrase "could not have even dreamed of when he began" shows that it is "innovative" (new and advanced). Finally, using this device for "pinpointing genes" and detecting genes that influence test scores matches improving the "probe" (scientific investigation or search).
Q2 i Still, when Plomin and his co-workers unveiled the results of their micro-array study—the biggest dragnet for intelligence-linked genes ever undertaken—they were underwhelming. The researchers found only six genetic markers that showed any sign of having an influence on the test scores. When they ran stringent statistical tests to see if the results were flukes, only one gene passed. It accounted for 0.4 percent of variation in the scores Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the results of the massive gene study were not exciting or convincing. Out of everything tested, only one gene met the strict criteria, and it was responsible for a tiny 0.4% difference in test scores.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "i" means that the study's results were disappointing and unconvincing because the identified genetic link was extremely tiny.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is i because Paragraph C focuses on how disappointing the findings of the large genetic study turned out to be. The author uses the word "underwhelming" (meaning disappointing or unpersuasive) and explains that after checking if the results were accidental "flukes," only a single gene passed the test. Moreover, this single gene explained just "0.4 percent of variation," showing an extremely tiny effect (low probability/impact) that led to disappointing and weak findings.
Q3 ii But for all these advances, intelligence remains a profound mystery. "It's amazing the extent to which we know very little," says Wendy Johnson, a psychologist at the University of Minnesota Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even with modern scientific progress, intelligence is still a big mystery, and experts are surprised by how little they actually know about it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "ii" means that our knowledge and understanding of how intelligence works is still very small and incomplete.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "ii" because Paragraph D talks about how scientists use modern tools to study the brain, but despite all their new discoveries, human intelligence is still mostly unknown. In this paragraph, the text states that "intelligence remains a profound mystery" and quotes a psychologist saying "we know very little." These words directly match the idea in heading ii that the "understanding of intelligence remains limited."
Q4 iii Yet that kind of gut instinct does not translate easily into a scientific definition Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that our natural feeling or sense about who is smart cannot be easily turned into a clear, official scientific description.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'iii' means that it is hard to give an exact scientific meaning or description to what intelligence really is.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading iii because Paragraph E discusses the challenge of creating a clear scientific definition for intelligence. The author notes that while everyday people can easily sense or observe smartness in others, this natural feeling "does not translate easily into a scientific definition." Keywords like "scientific definition" directly match the idea of "defining intelligence," and "does not translate easily" matches the word "difficulty."
Q5 vii " If you have an IQ score, does that tell you everything about a person's cognitive strengths and weaknesses? No," says Richard J. Haier of the University of California, Irvine. But even a simple number has the potential to say a lot about a person. "When you go see your doctor, what's the first thing that happens? Somebody takes your blood pressure and temperature. So you get two numbers. No one would say blood pressure and temperature summarize everything about your health, but they are key numbers." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that an IQ test score cannot show everything about how someone thinks, but it is still an important and useful number, just like temperature and blood pressure numbers are important when visiting a doctor.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "vii" means the paragraph explains how a measurement tool (like an IQ score) is not completely perfect, but it is still very helpful and useful to look at.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading vii because Paragraph F discusses IQ scores as a measurement tool or indicator. An expert points out that an IQ score is imperfect because it does not tell you everything about a person's abilities ("does that tell you everything... ? No"). However, it is still referable (valuable to use as a reference) because it can make strong predictions about school, work, and health, much like blood pressure and body temperature give essential information to a doctor.
Q6 x Roughly speaking, there are two possible sources for these variations. Environmental influences— anything from the way children are raised by their parents to the diseases they may suffer as they develop — are one source. Genes are another Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that differences in intelligence scores come from two main causes: how a person grows up (their environment) and their biology (their genes).
Answer Explanation:
The answer "x" means that heading x, "Multiple factors involved in intelligence", is the best title for Paragraph G.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is x because Paragraph G discusses what causes differences in intelligence scores. The author explains that scientists have identified more than one source behind these differences, specifically pointing out that "Environmental influences" are one factor and "Genes are another." This shows that there are multiple factors (several different causes) that contribute to a person's intelligence.
Q7 E " If you have an IQ score, does that tell you everything about a person's cognitive strengths and weaknesses? No," says Richard J. Haier of the University of California, Irvine Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Richard J. Haier explaining that an IQ score cannot tell you every single thing about how smart a person is or what their mental strengths and weaknesses are.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "E" refers to Richard J. Haier.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because Richard J. Haier states that having an IQ score does not tell you everything about a person's mental strengths and weaknesses. He compares it to a doctor taking your blood pressure and temperature: those numbers give important clues, but they cannot give a complete picture of your entire health. Therefore, he believes that a single number or test score cannot lead to a complete conclusion about someone.
Q8 A When they ran stringent statistical tests to see if the results were flukes, only one gene passed Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Plomin and his team checked whether their findings happened just by pure luck or accident, and only a single gene survived this strict test.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "A" refers to Robert Plomin, the scientist whose study faced the challenge of ruling out accidental or chance results.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph C describes Robert Plomin and his co-workers testing their gene discoveries. To make sure their findings were not random accidents, they used strict statistical tests. The passage states: "When they ran stringent statistical tests to see if the results were flukes, only one gene passed." The word "flukes" refers to chance occurrences or "occasionality," showing that almost all the discovered links could not be confirmed beyond pure chance.
Q9 F intelligence remains a profound mystery. "It's amazing the extent to which we know very little," says Wendy Johnson, a psychologist at the University of Minnesota Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that intelligence is still a big mystery and that Wendy Johnson points out how surprisingly little we actually understand about it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "F" points to the psychologist named Wendy Johnson.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because in Paragraph D, Wendy Johnson explains that scientists actually know very little about intelligence. The passage notes that despite many scientific discoveries, "intelligence remains a profound mystery," and Johnson states, "It's amazing the extent to which we know very little." This directly matches the idea that humans still have much more to learn and explore about what intelligence really is.
Q10 D How general intelligence arose from the brain, Spearman could not say Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Charles Spearman did not know how general intelligence actually developed or started inside the human brain.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" refers to the psychologist Charles Spearman.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Paragraph G states that Charles Spearman introduced the concept of general intelligence (the "g factor"), but he was unable to explain how it actually came from the brain. In other words, he could not identify the real origins or source of general intelligence.
Q11 micro-arrays / micro arrays Plomin's experience is a typical one for scientists who study intelligence. Along with using micro-arrays, they are employing brain scans and other sophisticated technologies to document some of the intricate dance steps that genes and environment take together in the development of intelligence Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says Plomin's experience is common for scientists who study intelligence. They use micro-arrays, brain scans, and other advanced tools to record how genes and environment work together as intelligence develops.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the first blank is 'micro-arrays', the tool that the researchers use.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'micro-arrays' because the summary's 'typical challenge' matches 'typical one' in the passage. 'Implemented' matches 'using' and 'employing'. The passage names 'micro-arrays' first in the list of tools, and it is only two words or fewer, so it fits the word limit.
Q12 intricate dance steps / dance steps Along with using micro-arrays, they are employing brain scans and other sophisticated technologies to document some of the intricate dance steps that genes and environment take together in the development of intelligence Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says scientists use micro-arrays, brain scans and other advanced tools to record the complex 'dance steps' that genes and environment do together as intelligence develops.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the missing words are 'dance steps', which means the complex actions of genes and environment working together.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'dance steps' because the summary says researchers 'record certain ___ performed both by genes and environment'. The passage says they 'document some of the intricate dance steps that genes and environment take together'. 'Record' matches 'document', and 'performed by genes and environment' matches 'take together'. 'Intricate dance steps' is also correct as it is only three words.
Q13 a reflection / reflection Some scientists have even begun to build a new vision of intelligence as a reflection of the ways in which information flows through the brain Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says some scientists now see intelligence as a reflection of how information moves through the brain.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is the word 'reflection'. It fills the gap about what intelligence is regarded to be.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'reflection' because the summary says intelligence is regarded to be '___ of how messages transmit in the brain'. This matches 'a reflection of the ways in which information flows through the brain'. Notice the synonyms: 'messages transmit' = 'information flows', and 'regarded' = 'new vision'.
