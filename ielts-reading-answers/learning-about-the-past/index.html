From Expert On Cambridge IELTS 07 Academic Reading Test 1 · Part 3 · Questions 29–42

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 29-42, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

Learning about the past

If the past is a foreign country, the version that used to be taught in Irish schools had a simple landscape. For 750 years after the first invasion by an English king, Ireland suffered from oppression. Then at Easter 1916, her brave sons rose against the tyrant; their leaders were shot but their cause prevailed, and Ireland (or 26 of her 32 counties) lived happily ever after. Awkward episodes, like the conflict between rival Irish nationalist groups in 1922-23, were airbrushed away. "The civil war was just an embarrassment, it was hardly mentioned," says Jimmy Joyce, who went to school in Dublin in the 1950s.

These days, Irish history lessons are more sophisticated. They deal happily with facts that have no place in a plain tale of heroes and tyrants: like the fact that hundreds of thousands of Irish people, Catholic and Protestant, fought for Britain during the two world wars. Why the change? First because in the 1980s, some people in Ireland became uneasy about the fact that a crude view of their national history was fuelling a conflict in the north of the island. Then came a fall in the influence of the Catholic church, whose authority had rested on a deft fusion between religion and patriotism. Also at work was an even broader shift: a state that was rich, confident and cosmopolitan saw less need to drum simple ideas into its youth, especially if those ideas risked encouraging violence.

As countries all over the world argue over "what to tell the children" about their collective past, many will look to Ireland rather enviously. Its seamless transition from a nationalist view of history to an open-minded one is an exception. A history curriculum is often a telling sign of how a nation and its elites see themselves: as victims of colonialism or practitioners (either repentant or defiant) of imperial power. In the modern history of Mexico, for example, a big landmark was the introduction, 15 years ago, of textbooks that were a bit less anti-American.

Many states still see history teaching and the inculcation of foundation myths, as a strategic imperative; others see it as an exercise in teaching children to think for themselves. The experience of several countries suggests that, whatever educators and politicians might want, there is a limit to how far history lessons can diverge in their tone from society as a whole.

Take Australia. John Howard, the conservative prime minister, has made history one of his favourite causes. At a "history summit" he held last August, educators were urged to "re-establish a structured narrative" about the nation's past. This was seen by liberal critics as a doomed bid to revive a romantic vision of white settlement in the 18th century. The romantic story has been fading since the 1980s, when a liberal, revisionist view came to dominate curricula: one that replaced "settlement" with "invasion" and that looked for the first time at the stories of aborigines and women. How much difference have Australia's policy battles made to what children in that cosmopolitan land are taught? Under Mr Howard's 11-year government, "multicultural" and "aboriginal reconciliation", two terms that once had currency, have faded from the policy lexicon, but not from classrooms. Australia's curricula are controlled by the states, not from Canberra. Most states have rolled Australian history into social studies courses, often rather muddled. In New South Wales, where the subject is taught in its own right, Mr Howard's bid to promote a patriotic view of history meets strong resistance.

Judy King, head of Riverside Girls High School in Sydney, has students from more than 40 ethnic groups at her school. "It's simply not possible to present one story to them, and nor do we," she says. "We canvass all the terms for white settlement: colonialism, invasion and genocide. Are all views valid? Yes. What's the problem with that? If the prime minister wants a single narrative instead, then speaking as someone who's taught history for 42 years he'll have an absolute fight on his hands." Tom Ying, head of history at Burwood Girls High School in Sydney, grew up as a Chinese child in the white Australia of the 1950s. In a school where most students are from non-English-speaking homes, he welcomes an approach that includes the dark side of European settlement. "When you have only one side of the story, immigrants, women and aborigines aren't going to have an investment in it."

Australia is a country where a relatively gentle (by world standards) effort to re-impose a sort of national ideology looks destined to fail. Russia, by contrast, is a country where the general principle of a toughly enforced ideology, and a national foundation story, still seems natural to many people, including the country's elite. In a telling sign of how he wants Russians to imagine their past, President Vladimir Putin has introduced a new national day – November 4th – to replace the old communist Revolution Day holiday on November 7th. What the new date recalls is the moment in 1612 when Russia, after a period of chaos, drove the Catholic Poles and Lithuanians out of Moscow. Despite the bonhomie of this week's 25-minute chat between Mr Putin and Pope Benedict XVI, the president is promoting a national day which signals "isolation and defensiveness" towards western Christendom, says Andrei Zorin, a Russian historian.

In South Africa, where white rule collapsed at the same time as communism did elsewhere, the authorities seem to have done a better job at forging a new national story and avoiding the trap of replacing one rigid ideology with another. "The main message of the new school curriculum is inclusion and reconciliation," says Linda Chisholm, who designed post-apartheid lessons. "We teach pupils to handle primary sources, like oral history and documents, instead of spoon-feeding them on textbooks," adds Aled Jones, a history teacher at Bridge House school in Cape Province. It helps that symbols and anniversaries have been redefined with skill. December 16th was a day to remember white settlers clashing with the Zulus in 1838; now it is the Day of Reconciliation.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q29 A Then at Easter 1916, her brave sons rose against the tyrant; their leaders were shot but their cause prevailed, and Ireland (or 26 of her 32 counties) lived happily ever after. Awkward episodes, like the conflict between rival Irish nationalist groups in 1922-23, were airbrushed away. "The civil war was just an embarrassment, it was hardly mentioned," says Jimmy Joyce, who went to school in Dublin in the 1950s Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Irish schools preferred to tell a happy story about heroes fighting a foreign ruler. An uncomfortable event where Irish people fought each other in 1922–23—the civil war—was seen as an embarrassment, so it was almost never spoken about.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that schools did not teach the Irish Civil War because it did not match the simple story of Irish heroes fighting against British control.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is choice A because the passage explains that Irish history classes used to teach a very simple story: Ireland suffered under British rule, brave Irish people fought back against the tyrant (Britain), and Ireland won. The Irish Civil War was a fight between different Irish groups rather than against Britain. Because this war was an "awkward" event that did not match the story of Irish unity against British oppressors, it was "airbrushed away" (hidden or ignored) and "hardly mentioned."
Q30 A This was seen by liberal critics as a doomed bid to revive a romantic vision of white settlement in the 18th century. The romantic story has been fading since the 1980s, when a liberal, revisionist view came to dominate curricula: one that replaced "settlement" with "invasion" and that looked for the first time at the stories of aborigines and women Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that critics viewed John Howard's effort as an attempt to bring back an old, idealized story about white people settling in Australia. It notes that this story was taught until the 1980s, after which school programs changed to teach new and different perspectives.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that John Howard wants history in Australia to be taught the way it was taught prior to the 1980s.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the text mentions that John Howard tried to bring back ("revive") an old, positive story ("a romantic vision") of European settlement. The passage states that this traditional narrative began disappearing ("fading") in the 1980s, when school lessons changed to include different viewpoints. Therefore, the style of history John Howard supports is the one taught before the 1980s.
Q31 C What the new date recalls is the moment in 1612 when Russia, after a period of chaos, drove the Catholic Poles and Lithuanians out of Moscow. Despite the bonhomie of this week's 25-minute chat between Mr Putin and Pope Benedict XVI, the president is promoting a national day which signals "isolation and defensiveness" towards western Christendom, says Andrei Zorin, a Russian historian Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the new holiday celebrates when Russia pushed Catholic forces out of Moscow. An expert notes that this special day shows Russia is separating itself and defending itself against Western Christian religions.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the new holiday shows Russia is resisting or taking a defensive stand against Western Christian traditions, such as Catholicism.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text states that the new national holiday celebrates Russia driving out Catholic invaders in 1612. A Russian historian explains that this holiday sends a clear message of "isolation and defensiveness" toward "western Christendom" (which is a synonym for Western Christianity). Therefore, creating this holiday shows that Russia is opposed to the influence of Western Christianity.
Q32 B In South Africa, where white rule collapsed at the same time as communism did elsewhere, the authorities seem to have done a better job at forging a new national story and avoiding the trap of replacing one rigid ideology with another Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in South Africa, government power held by white people came to an end, allowing leaders to create a fresh national story and teach history in a new way.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that South Africa changed the way it teaches history after white people were no longer in power and their control over the government ended.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because the text connects the creation of a new national story and school curriculum in South Africa to the end of apartheid, stating that "white rule collapsed". Although the passage mentions that communism ended at the same time in other places, it was the collapse of white rule in South Africa itself that led to the post-apartheid history lessons.
Q33 fuelling a conflict / fueling a conflict First because in the 1980s, some people in Ireland became uneasy about the fact that a crude view of their national history was fuelling a conflict in the north of the island Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that during the 1980s, people in Ireland worried that teaching an overly simple version of their history was making the violent troubles in Northern Ireland worse.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means making fighting, violence, or disagreement stronger and worse.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "fuelling a conflict" because the passage explains why Ireland changed its history lessons in the 1980s. Irish people were worried that teaching an overly simple or "crude view" of history was encouraging fighting ("fuelling a conflict") in the northern part of Ireland ("the north of the island"). Therefore, "fuelling a conflict" correctly completes the sentence.
Q34 see themselves A history curriculum is often a telling sign of how a nation and its elites see themselves: as victims of colonialism or practitioners (either repentant or defiant) of imperial power Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that what students learn in history lessons clearly shows how a country and its ruling class perceive their own identity.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "see themselves" means how a country and its leaders view, perceive, or think about their own identity and role in the world.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is found in the third paragraph. The sentence in the task states that the topics taught in history classes show how countries and people view their own identity. In the passage, "A history curriculum" matches "The things that are taught in history classes," "a telling sign of" matches "often tell us," and "a nation and its elites" matches "countries and people." Therefore, the phrase that completes the sentence is "see themselves."
Q35 classrooms Under Mr Howard's 11-year government, "multicultural" and "aboriginal reconciliation", two terms that once had currency, have faded from the policy lexicon, but not from classrooms Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even though the two terms stopped being used in political language, they are still used in schools.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "classrooms" refers to school rooms where students are taught lessons.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text, which discusses Australia and explains that the words "multicultural" and "aboriginal reconciliation" have disappeared from government policy language, but they have "not [faded] from classrooms." Therefore, these terms can still be found and used in Australian classrooms.
Q36 a single narrative If the prime minister wants a single narrative instead, then speaking as someone who's taught history for 42 years he'll have an absolute fight on his hands Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Judy King explaining that the prime minister wishes to teach only one version of history instead of sharing different viewpoints, but she strongly disagrees with this idea.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means only one official version or story of history.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is confirmed by Judy King's statements in paragraph 6. She discusses John Howard (referred to as "the prime minister") and notes that instead of teaching different viewpoints about history, he wants "a single narrative".
Q37 rigid ideology avoiding the trap of replacing one rigid ideology with another Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that South Africa successfully avoided the mistake of simply trading one fixed, inflexible set of beliefs for a different one.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "rigid ideology" means a strict, unchanging set of political beliefs or ideas that people are forced or expected to accept without question.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "rigid ideology" because the passage discusses how South African leaders managed their new national history after the end of white rule. In the text, the sentence talks about "avoiding the trap of replacing one rigid ideology with another." Here, the word "replacing" corresponds to "changing", and "avoiding" matches "has been avoided" in the question prompt.
Q38 TRUE a state that was rich, confident and cosmopolitan saw less need to drum simple ideas into its youth, especially if those ideas risked encouraging violence Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that because the country became wealthy and outward-looking, it no longer felt the need to force overly simple views and stories on young people.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means that the text confirms that when Ireland became wealthier, there was less desire or need to teach simple, black-and-white ideas.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because the second paragraph explains the reasons why Irish history lessons changed. It mentions that the country became "rich, confident and cosmopolitan" and therefore felt "less need to drum simple ideas into its youth." In this context, Ireland becoming "rich" corresponds to "became richer," and feeling "less need to drum simple ideas" matches becoming "less interested in simple ideas."
Q39 NOT GIVEN Many states still see history teaching and the inculcation of foundation myths, as a strategic imperative; others see it as an exercise in teaching children to think for themselves Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that many nations use history lessons to teach national myths, whereas other nations use them to encourage children to think independently. It does not state what the majority of countries believe.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the text does not say whether the majority of nations hold this opinion.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because the passage mentions that some countries ("others") view history as a way to help children think independently, while "many states" view it as a strategic tool to teach national myths. The text never says that "most" countries share the view that history lessons should help students think for themselves.
Q40 FALSE "We canvass all the terms for white settlement: colonialism, invasion and genocide." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the school looks at different words to describe the arrival and control of white people, showing that "invasion" is used as a term for "white settlement."
Answer Explanation:
The answer is FALSE because the passage shows that "invasion" is used as another term for "settlement" when describing the same historical event.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the passage treats "invasion" as an alternative name for "white settlement." A school leader explains that they explore different terms for white settlement, specifically listing "colonialism, invasion and genocide." Because the text presents "invasion" as one of the terms describing white settlement rather than a completely unrelated topic, the statement that they do not mean or refer to the same thing contradicts the passage.
Q41 NOT GIVEN Russia, by contrast, is a country where the general principle of a toughly enforced ideology, and a national foundation story, still seems natural to many people, including the country's elite Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that having a strong national ideology and a foundation story feels normal to many Russian citizens and leaders. However, it says nothing about Russian classrooms or history teaching.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is NOT GIVEN because the passage does not provide enough information to know if history classes in Russia focus on a foundation story.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because the text discusses Russia's general society, its political leaders, and a new national holiday, but it never mentions Russian school curricula or history lessons. While the author notes that a "national foundation story" seems normal to Russian leaders and the public, there is no mention of what takes place in Russian "history classes". Therefore, we cannot confirm whether this statement is true or false.
Q42 TRUE "The main message of the new school curriculum is inclusion and reconciliation," says Linda Chisholm, who designed post-apartheid lessons Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that the main goal of the new school plan in South Africa is to welcome everyone and help different groups get along with each other.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that South Africa's new history lessons are designed to welcome all perspectives and bring people together in peace.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because the passage directly supports this statement. In the final paragraph, the person who designed the post-apartheid lessons, Linda Chisholm, states that the central goal of the new curriculum is "inclusion and reconciliation". Here, "inclusion" matches the idea of including different standpoints, and "reconciliation" means bringing divided groups of people together in harmony.
