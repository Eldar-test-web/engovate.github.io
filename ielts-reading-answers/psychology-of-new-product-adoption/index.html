Psychology Of New Product Adoption - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

From IELTS Mock Tests Academic Reading Test 27 · Part 3 · Questions 28–40

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 28-40 which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

Psychology Of New Product Adoption

A

In today’s hypercompetitive marketplace, companies that successfully introduce new products are more likely to flourish than those that don’t. Yet businesses spend billions of dollars making better “mousetraps” only to find consumers roundly rejecting them. Studies show that new products fail at the stunning rate of between 40% and 90%, depending on the category, and the odds haven’t changed much in the past 25 years. In the U.S. packaged goods industry, for instance, companies introduce 30,000 products every year, but 70% to 90% of them don’t stay on store shelves for more than 12 months. Most innovative products – those that create new product categories or revolutionize old ones – are also unsuccessful. According to one study, 47% of first movers have failed, meaning that approximately half the companies that pioneered new product categories later pulled out of those businesses.

B

After the fact, experts and novices alike tend to dismiss unsuccessful innovations as bad ideas that were destined to fail. Why do consumers fail to buy innovative products even when they offer distinct improvements over existing ones? Why do companies invariably have more faith in new products than is warranted? Few would question the objective advantages of many innovations over existing alternatives, but that’s often not enough for them to succeed. To understand why new products fail to live up to companies’ expectations, we must delve into the psychology of behavior change.

C

New products often require consumers to change their behavior. As companies know, those behavior changes entail costs. Consumers incur costs, such as the activation fees they have to pay when they switch from one cellular service provider to another. They also bear learning costs, such as when they shift from manual to automatic automobile transmissions. People sustain obsolescence costs, too. For example, when they switch from VCRs to DVD players, their videotape collections become useless. All of these are economic switching costs that most companies routinely anticipate.

D

What businesses don’t take into account, however, are the psychological costs associated with behavior change. Many products fail because of a universal, but largely ignored, psychological bias: People irrationally overvalue benefits they currently possess relative to those they don’t. The bias leads consumers to value the advantages of products they own more than the benefits of new ones. It also leads executives to value the benefits of innovations they’ve developed over the advantages of incumbent products.

E

Companies have long assumed that people will adopt new products that deliver more value or utility than existing ones. Thus, businesses need only to develop innovations that are objectively superior to incumbent products, and consumers will have sufficient incentive to purchase them. In the 1960s, communications scholar Everett Rogers called the concept “relative advantage” and identified it as the most critical driver of new-product adoption. This argument assumes that companies make unbiased assessments of innovations and of consumers’ likelihood of adopting them. Although compelling, the theory has one major flaw: It fails to capture the psychological biases that affect decision making.

F

In 2002, psychologist Daniel Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in economics for a body of work that explores why and when individuals deviate from rational economic behavior. One of the cornerstones of that research, developed with psychologist Amos Tversky, is how individuals value prospects, or choices, in the marketplace. Kahneman and Tversky showed, and others have confirmed, that human beings’ responses to the alternatives before them have four distinct characteristics.

G

First, people evaluate the attractiveness of an alternative based not on its objective, or actual, value but on its subjective, or perceived value. Second, consumers evaluate new products or investments relative to a reference point, usually the products they already own or consume. Third, people view any improvements relative to this reference point as gains and treat all shortcomings as losses. Fourth, and most important, losses have a far greater impact on people than similarly sized gains, a phenomenon that Kahneman and Tversky called “loss aversion.” For instance, studies show that most people will not accept a bet in which there is a 50% chance of winning $100 and a 50% chance of losing $100. The gains from the wager must outweigh the losses by a factor of between two and three before most people find such a bet attractive. Similarly, a survey of 1,500 customers of Pacific Gas and Electric revealed that consumers demand three to four times more compensation to endure a power outage – and suffer a loss – than they are willing to pay to avoid the problem, a potential gain. As Kahneman and Tversky wrote, “losses loom larger than gains.”

H

Loss aversion leads people to value products that they already possess – those that are part of their endowment – more than those they don’t have. According to behavioral economist Richard Thaler, consumers value what they own, but may have to give up, much more than they value what they don’t own but could obtain. Thaler called that bias the “endowment effect.”

I

In a 1990 paper, Thaler and his colleagues describe a series of experiments they conducted to measure the magnitude of the endowment effect. In one such experiment, they gave coffee mugs to a group of people, the Sellers, and asked at what price point – from 25 cents to $9.25 – the Sellers would be willing to part with those mugs. They asked another group – the Choosers – to whom they didn’t give coffee mugs, to indicate whether they would choose the mug or the money at each price point. In objective terms, all the Sellers and Choosers were in the same situation: They were choosing between a mug and a sum of money. In one trial of this experiment, the Sellers priced the mug at $7.12, on average, but the Choosers were willing to pay only $3.12. In another trial, the Sellers and the Choosers valued the mug at $7.00 and $3.50, respectively. Overall, the Sellers always demanded at least twice as much to give up the mugs as the Choosers would pay to obtain them.

J

Kahneman and Tversky’s research also explains why people tend to stick with what they have even if a better alternative exists. In a 1989 paper, economist Jack Knetsch provided a compelling demonstration of what economists William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser called the “status quo bias.” Knetsch asked one group of students to choose between an attractive coffee mug and a large bar of Swiss chocolate. He gave a second group of students the coffee mugs but a short time later allowed each student to exchange his or her mug for a chocolate bar. Finally, Knetsch gave chocolate bars to a third group of students but much later allowed each student to exchange his or her bar for a mug. Of the students given a choice at the outset, 56% chose the mug, and 44% chose the chocolate bar, indicating a near even split in preferences between the two products. Logically, therefore, about half of the students to whom Knetsch gave the coffee mug should have traded for the chocolate bar and vice versa. That didn’t happen. Only 11% of the students who had been given the mugs and 10% of those who had been given the chocolate bars wanted to exchange their products. To approximately 90% of the students, giving up what they already had seemed like a painful loss and shrank their desire to trade.

K

Interestingly, most people seem oblivious to the existence of the behaviors implicit in the endowment effect and the status quo bias. In study after study, when researchers presented people with evidence that they had irrationally overvalued the status quo, they were shocked, skeptical, and more than a bit defensive. These behavioral tendencies are universal, but awareness of them is not.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q28 B In the 1960s, communications scholar Everett Rogers called the concept “relative advantage” and identified it as the most critical driver of new-product adoption. This argument assumes that companies make unbiased assessments of innovations and of consumers’ likelihood of adopting them. Although compelling, the theory has one major flaw: It fails to capture the psychological biases that affect decision making Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Everett Rogers introduced the idea of "relative advantage" to explain why people choose new products. However, the author notes that this theory has a major weakness or mistake because it ignores the psychological biases that affect how people make decisions.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "B" refers to Everett Rogers.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because paragraph E discusses Everett Rogers and his idea of "relative advantage" regarding how consumers adopt new products. The passage explicitly states that his theory has "one major flaw" because it does not consider the mental or psychological biases that influence how people make choices. This directly matches the idea in the question of stating a theory that contains a potential problem or fault.
Q29 C Kahneman and Tversky showed, and others have confirmed, that human beings’ responses to the alternatives before them have four distinct characteristics Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Kahneman and Tversky proved that how people react when presented with choices has four specific qualities.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the researchers Kahneman and Tversky identified four main traits or ways people act when choosing between different options.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because paragraph F directly links Kahneman and Tversky to the discovery of these traits. In the text, the phrase "four distinct characteristics" matches the question's phrase "several behavior features," and "responses to the alternatives before they" corresponds to "when they face other options."
Q30 A According to behavioral economist Richard Thaler, consumers value what they own, but may have to give up, much more than they value what they don’t own but could obtain Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that, according to Richard Thaler, buyers value the items they currently have and might lose far more than items they do not yet have but could get.
Answer Explanation:
The answer matches behavioral economist Richard Thaler with the idea that people place higher value on the things they already own and must give up than on new things they could receive.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because paragraph H specifically attributes this idea to Richard Thaler. The passage states that according to Thaler, "consumers value what they own, but many have to give up, much more than they value what they don't own but could obtain." Here, "customers" corresponds to "consumers", "possession they are going to abandon" corresponds to "what they own, but many have to give up", and "alternative they are going to swap in" matches "what they don't own but could obtain."
Q31 C Kahneman and Tversky’s research also explains why people tend to stick with what they have even if a better alternative exists Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that the work done by Kahneman and Tversky shows why individuals usually keep their current items, even when a superior replacement is available.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "C" means that Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky are the researchers who explained why individuals choose to keep the items they already own instead of getting new ones.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text directly states in Paragraph J that Kahneman and Tversky's research explains why people keep using what they have instead of switching to something newer or better. In this context, "explains why" corresponds to "answered the reason why," and "tend to stick with what they have even if a better alternative exists" means consumers "don’t replace existing products."
Q32 FALSE Few would question the objective advantages of many innovations over existing alternatives, but that’s often not enough for them to succeed Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that many new products are clearly better than older options, but this advantage alone is usually not enough to make them successful.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is FALSE because innovative products that are better than current products are not guaranteed to succeed in the market.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the statement claims that being better than existing products guarantees success. However, paragraph B clearly explains that even though many new creations have real benefits over existing options, that advantage is frequently not enough for them to succeed. Therefore, beating older alternatives does not promise or guarantee success.
Q33 FALSE Thus, businesses need only to develop innovations that are objectively superior to incumbent products, and consumers will have sufficient incentive to purchase them Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says companies think they only need to make products that are clearly better than old ones, and then people will want to buy them.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the statement is wrong. Knowing a new product has benefits does not guarantee it will succeed.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the passage says this company belief has "one major flaw" (paragraph E). Many new products still fail because of psychological biases like loss aversion and the endowment effect. Pay attention to words like "fail", "flaw", and "not enough", which show that benefits alone do not guarantee success.
Q34 NOT GIVEN Fourth, and most important, losses have a far greater impact on people than similarly sized gains, a phenomenon that Kahneman and Tversky called “loss aversion.” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that losses affect people much more strongly than gains of the same size, but it speaks about people in general and does not mention gender at all.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the text never mentions whether being male or female (gender) has any effect on how people handle gains and losses in the market.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because the passage discusses how consumers weigh gains and losses (loss aversion), but it never mentions gender, men, or women. Paragraph G explains that "losses have a far greater impact on people than similarly sized gains" and shares examples from studies and surveys, but it does not state or suggest that gender changes this outcome.
Q35 TRUE Overall, the Sellers always demanded at least twice as much to give up the mugs as the Choosers would pay to obtain them Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that across the experiment, the people selling the mugs consistently wanted double or more the amount of money that the people choosing were willing to pay.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means that the passage confirms there was a very big difference in price between what the sellers asked for and what the choosers wanted to pay in the experiment.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph I describes the experiments measuring the endowment effect. In these experiments, the Sellers asked for $7.12 and $7.00, while the Choosers only wanted to pay $3.12 and $3.50. The passage concludes that the sellers consistently asked for "at least twice as much" as the choosers were willing to pay. This shows there was indeed a huge gap between the seller's expected price (anticipation) and what the chooser was willing to pay (offer).
Q36 FALSE In study after study, when researchers presented people with evidence that they had irrationally overvalued the status quo, they were shocked, skeptical, and more than a bit defensive Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that whenever researchers showed people proof that they favored their current situation unfairly, the people were surprised, did not believe it, and became defensive rather than calm.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the statement is incorrect because people do not calmly or peacefully agree when they are shown their status quo bias.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the passage explicitly says people react with shock, doubt, and defensiveness—not peaceful acceptance—when shown proof of this bias. In paragraph K, when researchers showed participants evidence that they favored the status quo in an irrational way, the participants became "shocked, skeptical, and more than a bit defensive." This directly contradicts the claim that they accept it "peacefully."
Q37 C According to one study, 47% of first movers have failed, meaning that approximately half the companies that pioneered new product categories later pulled out of those businesses Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage clearly states that in one study, 47% of the first companies to try a new category failed, showing that around half of these creative business ventures did not succeed.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that close to 50% of the companies that try to create and launch brand-new types of products end up unsuccessful.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Paragraph A discusses the high failure rate of innovative ventures. Specifically, the passage notes that 47% of first movers fail, which directly explains that "approximately half the companies that pioneered new product categories later pulled out of those businesses." In this context, "pioneered new product categories" matches the idea of a business creative venture, and "47%" means "roughly half." None of the other options are supported: the passage mentions store shelves (not warehouses), does not state that only the US packaged goods industry is affected, and states that new products usually fail quickly rather than having a long life span.
Q38 B After the fact, experts and novices alike tend to dismiss unsuccessful innovations as bad ideas that were destined to fail Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that both experienced people (experts) and beginners (novices) assume that failed products were simply bad ideas, which implies they believe a product sells well if the idea behind it is good and innovative enough.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "being creative and innovative enough" means that both experts and beginners believe a product succeeds simply because it is novel, inventive, and a genuinely good idea.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Paragraph B explains the common view held by "experts and novices alike" (specialists and freshers/beginners). When a new product fails, they simply assume it was a "bad idea that was destined to fail." Consequently, they believe that success comes down to having a truly good, innovative idea (being creative and innovative enough), overlooking the psychological barriers consumers face when changing their behavior.
Q39 A Many products fail because of a universal, but largely ignored, psychological bias: People irrationally overvalue benefits they currently possess relative to those they don’t Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that many new products are not successful because companies forget a common human habit: people tend to value what they currently have much more than what they do not have.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that many new items fail to sell because companies overlook how much buyers value and prefer the things they already have.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph D clearly explains why many new products fail. Businesses forget to consider the psychological bias where consumers "irrationally overvalue benefits they currently possess" compared to what they do not have. This bias makes people place too much value on the products they already own rather than adopting new ones. In the question choices, this matches choice A ("they ignore the fact that people tend to overvalue the product they own").
Q40 D Only 11% of the students who had been given the mugs and 10% of those who had been given the chocolate bars wanted to exchange their products Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that almost all students wanted to keep the item they were initially given, with just 10% of the chocolate bar owners deciding to trade their chocolate for a mug.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that merely ten percent of the people who received a chocolate bar wanted to trade it for a coffee mug.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the status quo bias experiment described in Paragraph J. When students were given chocolate bars and later offered the chance to trade them for coffee mugs, only 10% of them wanted to make the trade. The word "swap" in the answer choice is a synonym for "exchange" in the passage.
