Mammoth Kill

What led to the disappearance of the giant mammals? Kate Wong examines the theories

Although it's hard to imagine in this age of urban sprawl and automobiles, North America once belonged to huge, elephant-like mammoths, camels, bear-sized beavers and other giant beasts, collectively known as 'megafauna'. Some 11,000 years ago, however, these large-bodied mammals – about 70 species in all – disappeared. Their demise coincided roughly with the arrival of humans in this region and dramatic climate change – factors that have inspired several theories about the die-off. Yet despite decades of scientific investigation, the exact cause remains a mystery. Now new findings offer support to one of these controversial hypotheses: that human hunting drove these huge 'megafauna' species to extinction.

This belief resulted in the overkill model which emerged in the 1960s, when it was put forth by Paul S Martin of the University of Arizona. Since then, critics have charged that no archaeological remains exist to support the idea that the first Americans hunted to the extent necessary to cause these extinctions. But at the annual meeting of the Society of Vertebrate Paleontology in Mexico City in October 1999, specialist John Alroy of the University of California at Santa Barbara argued that in fact, hunting-driven extinction is not only plausible, it was unavoidable. He has determined, using a computer simulation, that even a very modest amount of hunting would have wiped out these animals.

Assuming an initial human population of 100 people that grew no more than two per cent annually, Alroy determined that, if each band of, say, 50 people killed 15 to 20 large animals a year, humans could have eliminated the animal populations within 1,000 years. Large mammals in particular would have been vulnerable to the pressure because they have longer gestation periods than smaller mammals and their young require extended care.

However, not everyone agrees with Alroy's assessment. For one thing, the results depend on population size estimates for the extinct animals – estimates that are not necessarily reliable. But a more specific criticism comes from mammal expert Ross D E Macphee of the American Museum of Natural History in New York City, who points out that the relevant archaeological record contains barely a dozen examples of stone points embedded in mammoth bones (and none, it should be noted, are known from other megafaunal remains) – hardly what one might expect if hunting drove these animals to extinction. Furthermore, some of these species had a vast range, covering the whole continent – the Jefferson's Ground Sloth, for example, lived as far north as the Yukon and as far south as Mexico – which would have made hunting them in numbers sufficient to cause their extinction rather unlikely, he says.

Macphee agrees that humans most likely brought about these extinctions (as well as others around the world that coincided with human arrival), but not directly. Rather than through hunting, he suggests that people may have introduced a deadly disease, perhaps through their dogs or accompanying vermin, which then spread wildly among the native species because of their low resistance to the new introductions. Repeated outbreaks of a deadly disease could thus quickly drive them to the point of no return. So far, Macphee does not have empirical evidence for this theory, and it will not be easy to come by: such disease would kill far too quickly to leave its signature on the bones themselves. But he hopes that analyses of tissue and DNA from the most recent animal remains will eventually reveal the microbes responsible.

The third explanation for what brought on this North American extinction does not involve human beings. Instead, its proponents blame the loss on the climate. The Pleistocene epoch in question witnessed considerable climate instability, explains Russell W Graham of the Denver Museum of Nature and Science. As a result, their regular habitats disappeared, and species that had once formed communities split apart. For some animals, this brought opportunity. For much of the megafauna, however, the increasingly uniform terrain left them with shrinking geographical ranges – a death sentence for large animals, which need correspondingly large ranges. Although these creatures managed to maintain viable populations through most of the Pleistocene period, the final major climate fluctuation pushed them over the edge, Graham says.

For his part, Alroy is still convinced that human hunters were the destroyers of the giant animals. The overkill model explains everything the disease and climate scenarios explain, he asserts, and in addition makes accurate predictions about which species would eventually become extinct.

Q14 North America North America once belonged to huge, elephant-like mammoths, camels, bear-sized beavers and other giant beasts, collectively known as 'megafauna'. Some 11,000 years ago, however, these large-bodied mammals – about 70 species in all – disappeared Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that huge animals once lived in North America and that they disappeared about 11,000 years ago.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'North America' is the place where the large mammals lived.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'North America' because the passage links the 11,000-year-old disappearance of the giant animals to that region.
Q15 overkill model This belief resulted in the overkill model which emerged in the 1960s, when it was put forth by Paul S Martin of the University of Arizona Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says the overkill model came from Paul S Martin in the 1960s.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'overkill model' is the name of the first theory.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'overkill model' because the passage names Paul S Martin as its source, and 'around fifty years ago' matches 'the 1960s'.
Q16 hunting Now new findings offer support to one of these controversial hypotheses: that human hunting drove these huge 'megafauna' species to extinction Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that one idea is that human hunting made the giant animals die out.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'hunting' means chasing and killing animals.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'hunting' because the overkill model says people killed too many animals, and the passage says 'human hunting drove' them to extinction.

Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'disease' because the passage says people 'introduced a deadly disease' and the summary says humans 'introduced a ...'.

Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'evidence' because 'lacks any' in the summary matches 'does not have' in the passage.

The passage says the weather and climate changed a lot in that period.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'climate instability' means the climate was not steady and changed often.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'climate instability' because 'significant' in the summary matches 'considerable' in the passage.
Q20 communities As a result, their regular habitats disappeared, and species that had once formed communities split apart Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says animal groups that lived together split apart when their habitats disappeared.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'communities' means groups of animals living together.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'communities' because the passage says species 'had once formed communities' that 'split apart', matching 'division'.
Q21 B who points out that the relevant archaeological record contains barely a dozen examples of stone points embedded in mammoth bones (and none, it should be noted, are known from other megafaunal remains) – hardly what one might expect if hunting drove these animals to extinction Excerpt/Passage Explanation:

Answer Explanation:
The answer is B because Macphee says there is too little proof for the hunting theory.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because 'too little evidence' matches 'barely a dozen examples' of hunting traces in the archaeological record.
Q22 C For much of the megafauna, however, the increasingly uniform terrain left them with shrinking geographical ranges – a death sentence for large animals, which need correspondingly large ranges Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that large animals need large areas to live in, so smaller areas were deadly for them.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is C because Graham says large animals need a large range.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because 'the bigger the animal, the bigger the territory' matches 'large animals, which need correspondingly large ranges'.
Q23 B Macphee agrees that humans most likely brought about these extinctions (as well as others around the world that coincided with human arrival), but not directly Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says Macphee thinks humans caused extinctions around the world, but not by hunting.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is B because Macphee thinks humans caused many extinctions in an indirect way.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because 'globally' matches 'around the world' and 'indirectly' matches 'not directly'.
Q24 A For one thing, the results depend on population size estimates for the extinct animals – estimates that are not necessarily reliable Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says the results of Alroy's work depend on estimates of how many animals there were.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is A because Alroy's computer calculation uses estimates of animal populations.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the passage says 'the results depend on population size estimates', which matches 'population estimates' in the question.
Q25 B But he hopes that analyses of tissue and DNA from the most recent animal remains will eventually reveal the microbes responsible Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says Macphee hopes that tests on tissue and DNA from animal remains will find the germs that caused the deaths.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is B because Macphee hopes scientific tests on remains will support his disease theory.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because 'scientific examination of fossil remains' matches 'analyses of tissue and DNA from ... animal remains'.
Q26 C As a result, their regular habitats disappeared, and species that had once formed communities split apart Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that when habitats disappeared, animal communities broke apart.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is C because Graham says climate change split up animal groups.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because 'social groupings' matches 'communities' and 'negatively affected' matches 'split apart'.
