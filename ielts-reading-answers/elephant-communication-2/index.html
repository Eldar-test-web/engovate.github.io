Elephant Communication - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

From IELTS Past Papers Academic Reading Test 23 · Part 3 · Questions 28–40

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 28-40, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

Elephant communication

A. A postdoctoral fellow at Stanford University, O'Connell-Rodwell has come to Namibia's premiere wildlife sanctuary to explore the mysterious and complex world of elephant communication. She and her colleagues are part of a scientific revolution that began nearly two decades ago with the stunning revelation that elephants communicate over long distances using low-frequency sounds, also called infrasounds, that are too deep to be heard by most humans.

B. As might be expected, the African elephant's ability to sense seismic sound may begin in the ears. The hammer bone of the elephant's inner ear is proportionally very large for a mammal, but typical for animals that use vibrational signals. It may therefore be a sign that elephants can communicate with seismic sounds. Also, the elephant and its relative the manatee are unique among mammals in having reverted to a reptilian-like cochlear structure in the inner ear. The cochlea of reptiles facilitates a keen sensitivity to idbrations and may do the same in elephants.

C. But other aspects of elephant anatomy also support that ability. First, its enormous body, which allows it to generate low-frequency sounds almost as powerful as those of a jet takeoff, provides an ideal frame for receiving ground vibrations and conducting them to the inner ear. Second, the elephant's toe bones rest on a fatty pad that might help focus vibrations from the ground into the bone. Finally, the elephant's enormous brain lies in the cranial cavity behind the eyes in line with the auditory canal. The front of the skull is riddled with sinus cavities that may function as resonating chambers for vibrations from the ground.

D. How the elephants sense these vibrations is still unknown, but O'Connell-Rodwell who just earned a graduate degree in entomology at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, suspects the pachyderms are "listening" with their trunks and feet. The trunk may be the most versatile appendage in nature. Its uses include drinking, bathing, smelling, feeding and scratching. Both trunk and feet contain two kinds of pressure-sensitive nerve endings—one that detects infrasonic vibrations and another that responds to vibrations with slightly higher frequencies. For O'Connell-Rodwell, the future of the research is boundless and unpredictable: "Our work is really at the interface of geophysics, neurophysiology and ecology," she says. "We're asking questions that no one has really dealt with before."

E. Scientists have long known that seismic communication is common in small animals, including spiders, scorpions, insects and a number of vertebrate species such as white-lipped frogs, blind mole rats, kangaroo rats and golden moles. They also have found evidence of seismic sensitivity in elephant seals—2-ton marine mammals that are not related to elephants. But O'Connell-Rodwell was the first to suggest that a large land animal also is sending and receiving seismic messages. O'Connell-Rodwell noticed something about the freezing behavior of Etosha's six-ton bulls that reminded her of the tiny insects back in her lab. "I did my masters thesis on seismic communication in planthoppers," she says. "I'd put a male planthopper on a stem and play back a female call, and the male would do the same thing the elephants were doing: He would freeze, then press down on his legs, go forward a little bit, then freeze again. It was just so fascinating to me, and it's what got me to think, maybe there's something else going on other than acoustic communication."

F. Scientists have determined that an elephant's ability to communicate over long distances is essential for its survival, particularly in a place like Etosha, where more than 2,400 savanna elephants range over an area larger than New Jersey. The difficulty of finding a mate in this vast wilderness is compounded by ... elephant reproductive biology. Females breed only when in oestrus a period of sexual arousal that occurs every two years and lasts just a few days. "Females in estrus make these very low, long calls that bulls home in on, because it's such a rare event," O'Connell-Rodwell says. These powerful estrus calls carry more than two miles in the air and may be accompanied by long-distance seismic signals, she adds. Breeding herds also use low-frequency vocalizations to warn of predators. Adult bulls and cows have no enemies, except for humans, but young elephants are susceptible to attacks by lions and hyenas. When a predator appears, older members of the herd emit intense warning calls that prompt the rest of the herd to clump together for protection, then flee. In 1994, O'Connell-Rodwell recorded the dramatic cries of a breeding herd threatened by lions at Mushara. "The elephants got really scared, and the matriarch made these very powerful warning calls, and then the herd took off screaming and trumpeting," she recalls. "Since then, every time we've played that particular call at the water hole, we get the same response the elephants take off."

G. Reacting to a warning call played in the air is one thing, but could the elephants detect calls transmitted only through the ground? To find out, the research team in 2002 devised an experiment using electronic equipment that allowed them to send signals through the ground at Mushara. The results of our 2002 study showed us that elephants do indeed detect warning calls played through the ground," O'Connell-Rodwell observes. "We expected them to clump up into tight groups and leave the area, and that's in fact what they did. But since we only played back one type of call, we couldn't really say whether they were interpreting it correctly. Maybe they thought it was a vehicle or something strange instead of a predator warning."

H. An experiment last year was designed to solve that problem by using three different recordings—the 1994 warning call from Mushara, an anti-predator call recorded by scientist Joyce Poole in Kenya and an artificial warble tone. Although still analyzing data from this experiment, O'Connell-Rodwell is able to make a few preliminary observations: "The data I've seen so far suggest that the elephants were responding like I had expected, when the '94 warning call was played back, they tended to clump together and leave the water hole sooner. But what's really interesting is that the unfamiliar anti-predator call from Kenya also caused them to clump up, get nervous and aggressively rumble—but they didn't necessarily leave. I didn't think it was going to be that clear cut.

Q28 hammer The hammer bone of the elephant's inner ear is proportionally very large for a mammal, but typical for animals that use vibrational signals Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the elephant has an unusually large hammer bone inside its ear, which is normal for animals that detect vibrations from the ground.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "hammer" refers to a specific bone located inside an elephant's ear that helps it sense vibrational sounds.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph B, which explains how an elephant's ear anatomy allows it to detect seismic sounds. The text states that the **hammer** bone in the inner ear is proportionally very large, which is a key physical trait of creatures that detect and use vibrational signals.
Q29 body First, its enormous body, which allows it to generate low-frequency sounds almost as powerful as those of a jet takeoff, provides an ideal frame for receiving ground vibrations and conducting them to the inner ear Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that an elephant's giant body serves as an excellent frame to catch vibrations traveling through the ground and guide them straight to its ears.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "body" refers to the physical shape and large frame of an elephant, which helps it pick up and transmit ground vibrations.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph C, which mentions that the elephant's large frame helps it sense vibrations from the ground. Keywords include "enormous body" and "frame for receiving ground vibrations", showing that their physical body is specially built to conduct these seismic signals directly to the inner ear.
Q30 pad Second, the elephant's toe bones rest on a fatty pad that might help focus vibrations from the ground into the bone Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that underneath the bones of an elephant's toes is a fatty pad that helps channel ground vibrations into the skeleton.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "pad" refers to a soft layer of fat under an elephant's toes that helps direct vibrations from the ground into its bones.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph C, which explains the physical features that allow elephants to detect ground vibrations. The text notes that the elephant's toe bones rest on a "fatty pad," which aids in focusing vibrations coming from the ground into the bone.
Q31 cavities / sinus cavities The front of the skull is riddled with sinus cavities that may function as resonating chambers for vibrations from the ground Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the front part of an elephant's skull has many hollow spaces (sinus cavities) that may help echo and carry ground vibrations.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to empty spaces or hollow areas inside the front part of an elephant's head bones, known as sinus cavities.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph C, which discusses how an elephant's body structure helps it sense seismic signals. The text explicitly mentions that the front part of the elephant's skull contains "sinus cavities" that might act as chambers to amplify or resonate vibrations coming from the ground.
Q32 trunks and feet How the elephants sense these vibrations is still unknown but O'Connell-Rodwell who just earned a graduate degree in entomology at the University of Hawaii at Manoa suspects the pachyderms are listening with their trunks and feet Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that scientists do not yet fully understand how elephants feel these vibrations, but researcher O'Connell-Rodwell believes they "listen" using their long noses (trunks) and their feet.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "trunks and feet" refers to the specific body parts that elephants use to sense or "listen" to sound vibrations coming through the ground.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "trunks and feet" because Paragraph D discusses how elephants detect seismic vibrations. The researcher O'Connell-Rodwell suspects that these large animals (pachyderms) are "listening" using their trunks and feet, which contain special nerve endings designed to feel vibrations. In the passage, "then trunks and feet" corresponds directly to the summary sentence asking what body parts they are "listening" with.
Q33 infrasonic Both trunk and feet contain two kinds of pressure-sensitive nerve endings—one that detects infrasonic vibrations and another that responds to vibrations with slightly higher frequencies Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that an elephant's trunk and feet have two types of special nerves. One type senses very low-frequency (infrasonic) vibrations, and the other type senses slightly higher-frequency vibrations.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "infrasonic" refers to very low-frequency sound vibrations that are usually too deep for human ears to hear.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "infrasonic" because Paragraph D describes the two types of nerve endings found in the elephants' trunks and feet. The text states that one type detects "infrasonic vibrations" while the other detects vibrations with slightly higher frequencies. Matching this with the summary sentence, the missing word describing the first type of frequency is "infrasonic".
Q34 ecology "Our work is really at the interface of geophysics, neurophysiology and ecology," Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the researcher's work brings together three fields of study: geophysics, neurophysiology, and ecology.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "ecology" refers to the scientific study of how living organisms relate to each other and to their environment.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph D, which states that O'Connell-Rodwell describes her research as taking place at the "interface of geophysics, neurophysiology and ecology." In the summary, the word "combination" matches the word "interface" from the passage, making "ecology" the correct word to complete the list.
Q35 seismic messages But O'Connell-Rodwell was the first to suggest that a large land animal also is sending and receiving seismic messages Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that O'Connell-Rodwell was the very first scientist to propose that a large animal living on land communicates by sending and receiving ground vibration signals.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to vibrations or signals sent through the ground as a way for animals to communicate with each other.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "seismic messages" because the passage explicitly states that O'Connell-Rodwell was the first researcher to propose that a large land animal sends and receives these ground-borne signals. In paragraph E, the phrase "was the first to suggest that a large land animal also is sending and receiving seismic messages" directly matches the summary statement "was the first to indicate that a large land animal also is sending and receiving [input]". Key synonyms and parallels include "suggest" for "indicate" and "sending and receiving seismic messages" as the exact object of the action.
Q36 acoustic communication / acoustic communications It was just so fascinating to me, and it's what got me to think, maybe there's something else going on other than acoustic communication Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that observing this special behavior led the researcher to believe that the animals were using a different method to communicate rather than just sending sounds through the air.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "acoustic communication" refers to sending and receiving messages using ordinary sound that travels through the air.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "acoustic communication" because in Paragraph E, O'Connell-Rodwell describes her experiment with a male planthopper. Watching the insect freeze and push down on its legs made her realize that animals might be using another way to share information beside ordinary sound signals in the air. In the text, she says this behavior made her think "maybe there's something else going on other than acoustic communication."
Q37 mate The difficulty of finding a mate in this vast wilderness is compounded by
elephant reproductive biology		 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that it is very difficult for elephants to find a partner to reproduce with in such a large, wide area.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "mate" means a breeding partner with whom an animal reproduces.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "mate" because Paragraph F explains why long-distance communication is necessary for elephants. The passage mentions that it is very hard for elephants to find a breeding partner in a large wilderness area, so they need powerful long-distance calls to locate each other. In the summary, the phrase "finding a " directly matches the text's "finding a mate".
Q38 ground The results of our 2002 study showed us that elephants do indeed detect warning calls played through the ground Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the test done in 2002 proved elephants can feel and notice danger calls that travel through the earth.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "ground" means the solid surface of the earth that elephants walk on.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "ground" because paragraph G discusses the 2002 study where researchers sent signals through the earth. O'Connell-Rodwell stated that the study showed elephants can truly detect warning calls sent through the "ground".
Q39 A Breeding herds also use low-frequency vocalizations to warn of predators. Adult bulls and cows have no enemies, except for humans, but young elephants are susceptible to attacks by lions and hyenas. When a predator appears, older members of the herd emit intense warning calls that prompt the rest of the herd to clump together for protection, then flee Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that elephant groups use low-pitched sounds to alert each other when dangerous animals attack. Because baby elephants can be harmed by lions and hyenas, older elephants make loud warning sounds so the whole group gathers together for safety and runs away.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that elephants need long-distance communication to stay alive when dangerous hunting animals, like lions or hyenas, show up.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph F explains that communicating over long distances is vital for survival. The passage specifically mentions that herds use low-frequency sounds to warn each other about danger: "When a predator appears, older members of the herd emit intense warning calls" to help protect the young and escape together. In contrast, females (not males) experience estrus (ruling out D), humans are not the predators prompting these herd warning calls for young elephants (ruling out B), and older members warn the group so they can flee together rather than fleeing away from the group itself (ruling out C).
Q40 C Although still analyzing data from this experiment, O'Connell-Rodwell is able to make a few preliminary observations Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the researcher is still studying the data from the experiment with three recordings, meaning she can only share early, non-final thoughts about the results.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the findings from the experiment are still not completely final, certain, or fully concluded.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because in Paragraph H, the text mentions that the researcher is "still analyzing data" and can only make a few "preliminary observations." The word "preliminary" means early or not final, showing that the results are still being studied and are somewhat undecided or not yet fully established.
