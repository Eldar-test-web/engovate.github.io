Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 28-40, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

Serendipity: The Accidental Scientists

A. A paradox lies close to the heart of scientific discovery. If you know just what you are looking for, finding it can hardly count as a discovery, since it was fully anticipated. But if, on the other hand, you have no notion of what you are looking for, you cannot know when you have found it, and discovery, as such, is out of the question. In the philosophy of science, these extremes map onto the purist forms of deductivism and inductivism: In the former, the outcome is supposed to be logically contained in the premises you start with; in the latter, you are recommended to start with no expectations whatsoever and see what turns up.

B. As in so many things, the ideal position is widely supposed to reside somewhere in between these two impossible-to-realize extremes. You want to have a good enough idea of what you are looking for to be surprised when you find something else of value, and you want to be ignorant enough of your end point that you can entertain alternative outcomes. Scientific discovery should, therefore, have an accidental aspect, but not too much of one. Serendipity is a word that expresses a position something like that. It's a fascinating word, and the late Robert King Merton—‘the father of the sociology of science’—liked it well enough to compose its biography, assisted by the French cultural historian Elinor Barber.

C. Serendipity means a ‘happy accident’ or ‘pleasant surprise’; specifically, the accident of finding something good or useful without looking for it. The first noted use of ‘serendipity’ in the English language was by Horace Walpole (1717-1792). In a letter to Horace Mann (dated 28 January 1754) he said he formed it from the Persian fairy tale The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes ‘were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of’. The name stems from Serendip, an old name for Sri Lanka.

D. Besides antiquarians, the other community that came to dwell on serendipity to say something important about their practice was that of scientists. Many scientists, including the Harvard physiologist Walter Cannon and, later, the British immunologist Peter Medawar, liked to emphasize how much of scientific discovery was unplanned and even accidental. One of Cannon's favorite examples of such serendipity is Luigi Galvani's observation of the twitching of dissected frogs' legs, hanging from a copper wire, when they accidentally touched an iron railing, leading to the discovery of ‘galvanism’; another is Hans Christian Orsted's discovery of electromagnetism when he unintentionally brought a current-carrying wire parallel to a magnetic needle. The context in which scientific serendipity was most contested and had its greatest resonance was that connected with the idea of planned science. The serendipitists were not all inhabitants of academic ivory towers. Two of the great early-20th-century American pioneers of industrial research—Willis Whitney and Irving Langmuir, both of General Electric—made much play of serendipity, in the course of arguing against overly rigid research planning.

E. Yet what Cannon and Medawar took as a benign method, other scientists found incendiary. To say that science had a significant serendipitous aspect was taken by some as dangerous denigration. If scientific discovery were really accidental, then what was the special basis of expert authority?

F. In this connection, the aphorism of choice came from no less an authority on scientific discovery than Louis Pasteur: "Chance favors the prepared mind." Accidents may happen, and things may turn up unplanned and unforeseen, as one is looking for something else, but the ability to notice such events, to see their potential bearing and meaning, to exploit their occurrence and make constructive use of them—these are the results of systematic mental preparation. What seems like an accident is just another form of expertise. On closer inspection, it is insisted, accident dissolves into sagacity.

G. In 1936, as a very young man, Merton wrote a seminal essay on "The Unanticipated Consequences of Purposive Social Action." It is, he argued, the nature of social action that what one intends is rarely what one gets: Intending to provide resources for buttressing Christian religion, the natural philosophers of the Scientific Revolution laid the groundwork for secularism; people wanting to be alone with nature in Yosemite Valley wind up crowding one another. We just don't know enough—and we can never know enough—to ensure that the past is an adequate guide to the future: Uncertainty about outcomes, even of our best-laid plans, is endemic. All social action, including that undertaken with the best evidence and formulated according to the most rational criteria, is uncertain in its consequences.

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q28 iv A paradox lies close to the heart of scientific discovery. If you know just what you are looking for, finding it can hardly count as a discovery, since it was fully anticipated. But if, on the other hand, you have no notion of what you are looking for, you cannot know when you have found it, and discovery, as such, is out of the question. In the philosophy of science, these extremes map onto the purist forms of deductivism and inductivism: In the former, the outcome is supposed to be logically contained in the premises you start with; in the latter, you are recommended to start with no expectations whatsoever and see what turns up Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains a puzzle in science: if you already know what you are looking for, it is not a true discovery; but if you do not know at all, you will not realize when you find it. It connects these two extreme ideas to two philosophies of science: deductivism and inductivism.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is heading iv, which means the paragraph discusses two main ways of thinking or knowing related to a puzzle or contradiction in scientific discovery.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is iv because Paragraph A focuses on a core contradiction ("paradox") in making scientific discoveries. It introduces two opposite approaches to scientific thinking or knowledge: "deductivism" (where you already know what you expect to find) and "inductivism" (where you start with no expectations at all). These two concepts represent the "two basic knowledge" approaches mentioned in the heading.
Q29 ix As in so many things, the ideal position is widely supposed to reside somewhere in between these two impossible-to-realize extremes Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage means that the best state is located in the middle ground between two extreme, unachievable approaches.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "ix" matches the heading "The optimum balance between the two extremes".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is ix because Paragraph B explains that the best way to make scientific discoveries is not at either extreme (knowing everything or knowing nothing), but somewhere in the middle. The phrase "ideal position ... somewhere in between" directly corresponds to "optimum balance", and the passage explicitly refers back to "these two impossible-to-realize extremes" discussed in Paragraph A.
Q30 i The first noted use of ‘serendipity’ in the English language was by Horace Walpole (1717-1792). In a letter to Horace Mann (dated 28 January 1754) he said he formed it from the Persian fairy tale The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes ‘were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of’. The name stems from Serendip, an old name for Sri Lanka Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains who first created the word "serendipity", which story inspired it, and what old place name it comes from, clearly describing the origin of the word.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "i" means that the best title for Paragraph C is "The origin of serendipity".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading i because Paragraph C explains where the word "serendipity" comes from. It talks about its definition, the person who first created and used the word in English (Horace Walpole in 1754), the fairy tale that inspired it (*The Three Princes of Serendip*), and the geographical root of the name (*Serendip*, an old name for Sri Lanka). These details directly describe the "origin" (history and beginning) of the word.
Q31 v One of Cannon's favorite examples of such serendipity is Luigi Galvani's observation of the twitching of dissected frogs' legs, hanging from a copper wire, when they accidentally touched an iron railing, leading to the discovery of ‘galvanism’; another is Hans Christian Orsted's discovery of electromagnetism when he unintentionally brought a current-carrying wire parallel to a magnetic needle. The serendipitists were not all inhabitants of academic ivory towers. Two of the great early-20th-century American pioneers of industrial research—Willis Whitney and Irving Langmuir, both of General Electric—made much play of serendipity, in the course of arguing against overly rigid research planning Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage provides specific examples of scientific discoveries that happened by accident, and it shows that this concept was used not only by university scientists but also by industrial researchers outside of pure academia.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "v" means that Paragraph D should be titled "The accidental evidences in and beyond science."
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is v because Paragraph D presents real-world examples (evidence) of accidental discoveries in science, such as Luigi Galvani discovering galvanism and Hans Christian Orsted discovering electromagnetism. Furthermore, the paragraph explains that the belief in serendipity extended beyond traditional academic science into industrial research companies like General Electric.
Q32 iii Yet what Cannon and Medawar took as a benign method, other scientists found incendiary. To say that science had a significant serendipitous aspect was taken by some as dangerous denigration. If scientific discovery were really accidental, then what was the special basis of expert authority Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that some scientists strongly disliked the idea of serendipity, feeling that calling science accidental insulted and lowered the importance of scientists' expertise.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "iii" refers to heading iii, "Arguments against serendipity", which means reasons why some people disagree with or oppose the idea of lucky accidents playing a major role in scientific discovery.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading iii because Paragraph E discusses the negative reactions and objections some scientists had toward serendipity. While some researchers viewed accidental discovery positively, other scientists viewed it as "incendiary" and a "dangerous denigration" (unfair criticism) that questioned the value and authority of scientific experts.
Q33 vii Accidents may happen, and things may turn up unplanned and unforeseen, as one is looking for something else, but the ability to notice such events, to see their potential bearing and meaning, to exploit their occurrence and make constructive use of them—these are the results of systematic mental preparation Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even though unexpected things (accidents) can happen, a person needs a trained and ready mind (mental preparation) to understand and make good use of those surprises.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the best heading for Paragraph F is "Accident and mental preparation".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is vii because Paragraph F discusses how accidental discoveries require a ready and trained mind to be noticed and used properly. The paragraph quotes Louis Pasteur's famous saying, "Chance favors the prepared mind," and explains that handling unexpected "accidents" successfully comes from "systematic mental preparation" and expertise.
Q34 Horace Walpole The first noted use of ‘serendipity’ in the English language was by Horace Walpole (1717-1792). In a letter to Horace Mann (dated 28 January 1754) he said he formed it from the Persian fairy tale The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes ‘were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of’ Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Horace Walpole was the first person to use the word "serendipity" in English, which he created and mentioned in a letter sent to Horace Mann.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to the person named Horace Walpole, who was the first individual to invent and use the word "serendipity" in a letter.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "Horace Walpole" because paragraph C states that the first noted use of the word "serendipity" was by him in a letter written to Horace Mann. In the sentence, the phrase "coined in the writing of" matches the passage's idea that the "first noted use... was by Horace Walpole... In a letter to Horace Mann".
Q35 fairy tale In a letter to Horace Mann (dated 28 January 1754) he said he formed it from the Persian fairy tale The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes ‘were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of’ Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Horace Walpole made the word "serendipity" based on a Persian fairy tale, where the characters constantly made unexpected discoveries.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "fairy tale" refers to a traditional or magical story, usually told to children.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "fairy tale" because Paragraph C states that Horace Walpole created ("formed", which matches "derived") the word serendipity from a "Persian fairy tale The Three Princes of Serendip". In this story, the main characters ("heroes") were famous for discovering valuable things unexpectedly ("by accidents").
Q36 Sri Lanka The name stems from Serendip, an old name for Sri Lanka Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the origin of the name comes from Serendip, which was the historical name for Sri Lanka.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is Sri Lanka, an island country in South Asia.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "Sri Lanka" because the text explicitly states that "Serendip" was an earlier name used for this country. In the passage, the summary phrase "a former name for" matches the text's phrase "an old name for". Therefore, "Sri Lanka" correctly completes the blank.
Q37 A in the latter, you are recommended to start with no expectations whatsoever and see what turns up Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that under inductivism, a person is advised to begin without any expectations and simply see what happens.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means watching or studying something without having any prior expectations or ideas about what you will find at the start.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because paragraph A explains the two scientific extremes: deductivism and inductivism. The text describes inductivism ("in the latter") as a method where "you are recommended to start with no expectations whatsoever and see what turns up." Here, having "no expectations whatsoever" matches the meaning of "without anticipation at the beginning."
Q38 C As in so many things, the ideal position is widely supposed to reside somewhere in between these two impossible-to-realize extremes Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the best approach is to be in the middle of the two extreme methods rather than choosing completely one side or the other.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that making scientific discoveries should happen in the middle ground between two opposite, extreme ways of doing research.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Paragraph B states that the best way to make discoveries is to be "in between these two impossible-to-realize extremes." In Paragraph A, the two extremes are described: knowing exactly what will happen versus knowing nothing at all. Paragraph B concludes that a good scientific approach sits right in the middle—having some idea of what you are looking for while still leaving room for unexpected findings.
Q39 B One of Cannon's favorite examples of such serendipity is Luigi Galvani's observation of the twitching of dissected frogs' legs, hanging from a copper wire, when they accidentally touched an iron railing, leading to the discovery of ‘galvanism’ Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that Luigi Galvani discovered galvanism purely by chance when a frog's leg accidentally touched a metal railing, serving as a clear example of serendipity (a happy accident) in science.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means that Luigi Galvani's story is used as an example of an unexpected, lucky event that leads to an important finding in science.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Paragraph D explains that scientists like Walter Cannon highlighted how scientific findings are often unplanned and accidental. The text specifically calls Luigi Galvani's finding an example of "such serendipity", which Paragraph C defines as a "happy accident" or finding something useful by chance.
Q40 B It is, he argued, the nature of social action that what one intends is rarely what one gets: Intending to provide resources for buttressing Christian religion, the natural philosophers of the Scientific Revolution laid the groundwork for secularism; people wanting to be alone with nature in Yosemite Valley wind up crowding one another Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that people's actions often have surprising results. For example, people who travel to Yosemite Valley because they hope to be alone in nature actually end up making the place crowded.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the example is used to show that real results often turn out very differently from what people plan or expect.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Paragraph G discusses Merton's essay about unexpected results ("unanticipated consequences"). The author explains that "what one intends is rarely what one gets" (what people plan is rarely what actually happens). The Yosemite Valley example illustrates this idea: people go there because they want to be alone, but because many people have the same idea, they end up crowding each other. This shows that the reality turns out to be unpredictable compared to their original expectations.
