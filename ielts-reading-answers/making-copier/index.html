Making Copier

At first, nobody bought Chester Carlson's strange idea. But trillions of documents later, his invention is the biggest thing in printing since Gutenberg

A

Copying is the engine of civilization: culture is behavior duplicated. The oldest copier invented by people is language, by which an idea of yours becomes an idea of mine. The second great copying machine was writing. When the Sumerians transposed spoken words into stylus marks on clay tablets more than 5,000 years ago, they hugely extended the human network that language had created. Writing freed copying from the chain of living contact. It made ideas permanent, portable and endlessly reproducible.

B

Until Johann Gutenberg invented the printing press in the mid-1400s, producing a book in an edition of more than one generally meant writing it out again. Printing with moveable type was not copying, however. Gutenberg couldn't take a document that already existed, feed it into his printing press and run off facsimiles. The first true mechanical copier was manufactured in 1780, when James Watt, who is better known as the inventor of the modern steam engine, created the copying press. Few people today know what a copying press was, but you may have seen one in an antique store, where it was perhaps called a book press. A user took a document freshly written in special ink, placed a moistened sheet of translucent paper against the inked surface and squeezed the two sheets together in the press, causing some of the ink from the original to penetrate the second sheet, which could then be read by turning it over and looking through its back. The high cost prohibits the widespread use of this copier.

C

Among the first modern copying machines, introduced in 1950 by 3M, was the Thermo-Fax, and it made a copy by shining infrared light through an original document and a sheet of paper that had been coated with heat-sensitive chemicals. Competing manufacturers soon introduced other copying technologies and marketed machines called Dupliton, Dial-A-Matic Autostat, Verifax, Copease and Copymation. These machines and their successors were welcomed by secretaries, who had no other means of reproducing documents in hand, but each had serious drawbacks. All required expensive chemically treated papers. And all made copies that smelled bad, were hard to read, didn't last long and tended to curl up into tubes. For those reasons, copying presses were standard equipment in offices for nearly a century and a half. The machines were displaced, beginning in the late 1800s, by a combination of two 19th century inventions: the typewriter and carbon paper.

D

None of those machines is still manufactured today. They were all made obsolete by a radically different machine, which had been developed by an obscure photographic-supply company. That company had been founded in 1906 as the Haloid Company and is known today as the Xerox Corporation. In 1959, it introduced an office copier called the Haloid Xerox 914, a machine that, unlike its numerous competitors, made sharp, permanent copies on ordinary paper-a huge breakthrough. The process, which Haloid called xerography (based on Greek words meaning "dry" and "writing"), was so unusual and nonintuitive that physicists who visited the drafty warehouses where the first machines were built sometimes expressed doubt that it was even theoretically feasible.

E

Remarkably, xerography was conceived by one person- Chester Carlson, a shy, soft-spoken patent attorney, who grew up in almost unspeakable poverty and worked his way through junior college and the California Institute of Technology. Chester Carlson was born in Seattle in 1906. His parents-Olof Adolph Carlson and Ellen Josephine Hawkins—had grown up on neighboring farms in Grove City, Minnesota, a tiny Swedish farming community about 75 miles west of Minneapolis. Compared with competitors, Carlson was not a normal inventor in the 20th century. He made his discovery in solitude in 1937 and offered it to more than 20 major corporations, among them IBM, General Electric, Eastman Kodak and RCA. All of them turned him down, expressing what he later called "an enthusiastic lack of interest" and thereby passing up the opportunity to manufacture what Fortune magazine would describe as "the most successful product ever marketed in America."

F

Carlson's invention was indeed a commercial triumph. Essentially overnight, people began making copies at a rate that was orders of magnitude higher than anyone had believed possible. And the rate is still growing. In fact, most documents handled by a typical American office worker today are produced xerographically, either on copiers manufactured by Xerox and its competitors or on laser printers, which employ the same process (and were invented, in the 1970s, by a Xerox researcher). This year, the world will produce more than three trillion xerographic copies and laser-printed pages—about 500 for every human on earth.

G

Xerography eventually made Carlson a very wealthy man. (His royalties amounted to something like a 16th of a cent for every Xerox copy made, worldwide, through 1965.) Nevertheless, he lived simply. He never owned a second home or a second car, and his wife had to urge him not to buy third-class train tickets when he traveled in Europe. People who knew him casually seldom suspected that he was rich or even well-to-do; when Carlson told an acquaintance he worked at Xerox, the man assumed he was a factory worker and asked if he belonged to a union. "His possessions seemed to be composed of the number of things he could easily do without," his second wife said. He spent the last years of his life quietly giving most of his fortune to charities. When he died in 1968, among the eulogizers was the secretary-general of the United Nations.

Q1 FALSE When the Sumerians transposed spoken words into stylus marks on clay tablets more than 5,000 years ago, they hugely extended the human network that language had created Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that when writing began over 5,000 years ago, the Sumerians recorded spoken language by pressing marks onto tablets made of clay, not on papyrus.
Answer Explanation:
The answer FALSE means that the statement directly contradicts the facts given in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the statement claims that the earliest languages were recorded on "papyrus", whereas the passage clearly states that spoken words were first recorded as marks on "clay tablets" by the Sumerians over 5,000 years ago. Because the material used was clay tablets rather than papyrus, the statement is false.
Q2 NOT GIVEN Until Johann Gutenberg invented the printing press in the mid-1400s, producing a book in an edition of more than one generally meant writing it out again. Printing with moveable type was not copying, however. Gutenberg couldn't take a document that already existed, feed it into his printing press and run off facsimiles Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage discusses Gutenberg's invention and explains how it worked, but it says nothing about the training required to use it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the text does not say whether a person needed a lot of training to use Johann Gutenberg's printing press.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "NOT GIVEN" because paragraph B talks about Johann Gutenberg inventing the printing press and describes what the machine could and could not do. However, there is no mention anywhere in the text about how much training, skill, or practice a user needed to operate it. Because this detail is completely missing, the statement cannot be confirmed as true or false.
Q3 NOT GIVEN The first true mechanical copier was manufactured in 1780, when James Watt, who is better known as the inventor of the modern steam engine, created the copying press Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage tells us that James Watt made the copying press in 1780 and that people know him better for the steam engine, but it does not tell us when he made the steam engine.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the text does not say which invention came first: the steam engine or the copying machine.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because the passage never explains the timeline between James Watt's two inventions. In Paragraph B, the text mentions that James Watt created the copying press in 1780 and notes that he is more famous for inventing the modern steam engine. However, it does not state whether he invented the steam engine before or after the copying press. Because this information is missing, we cannot know if the statement is true or false.
Q4 TRUE Competing manufacturers soon introduced other copying technologies and marketed machines called Dupliton, Dial-A-Matic Autostat, Verifax, Copease and Copymation. These machines and their successors were welcomed by secretaries, who had no other means of reproducing documents in hand, but each had serious drawbacks. All required expensive chemically treated papers Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that companies sold copiers such as the Dupliton. These devices and the models that came after them had big problems because every one of them needed high-priced, chemically treated paper to make copies.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means that the statement correctly matches the facts described in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph C mentions that companies produced copying machines such as "Dupliton". The text explains that these machines and their "successors" (follower versions or later models) had drawbacks, including the fact that all of them needed "expensive" (costly) special paper to work. Therefore, using Dupliton and the versions that followed was expensive.
Q5 FALSE The machines were displaced, beginning in the late 1800s, by a combination of two 19th century inventions: the typewriter and carbon paper Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that older copying machines were replaced by the typewriter and carbon paper starting in the late 1800s, meaning people used them a lot, directly contradicting the statement that they were quickly replaced because they did not sell well.
Answer Explanation:
The answer FALSE means the statement is incorrect according to the passage.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the passage does not say typewriter and carbon paper were replaced quickly due to poor sales. Instead, paragraph C explains that they actually replaced earlier machines ("The machines were displaced, beginning in the late 1800s, by a combination of two 19th century inventions: the typewriter and carbon paper"), showing they were successful and widely adopted rather than failing to sell.
Q6 FALSE In 1959, it introduced an office copier called the Haloid Xerox 914, a machine that, unlike its numerous competitors, made sharp, permanent copies on ordinary paper-a huge breakthrough Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the Haloid Xerox 914 was special because it worked using regular paper instead of expensive, specially prepared paper.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is FALSE because the statement says the Haloid Xerox 914 needed special paper, but in reality, it could use normal, everyday paper.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the passage states that the Haloid Xerox 914 made copies on "ordinary paper," unlike previous machines that needed special, chemically treated paper. Therefore, the statement directly contradicts the facts in the text.
Q7 normal inventor / inventor Compared with competitors, Carlson was not a normal inventor in the 20th century. He made his discovery in solitude in 1937 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Carlson differed from typical 20th-century inventors because he created his invention by himself alone.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means a typical creator of new machines or tools.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text in Paragraph E, which states that Carlson was not like a typical creator of his time. The phrase in the notes "unlike a 20-century" matches the text's "was not a normal inventor in 20-century," and the note's phrase "like to work on his own" corresponds to the passage saying "He made his discovery in solitude."
Q8 corporations He made his discovery in solitude in 1937 and offered it to more than 20 major corporations, among them IBM, General Electric, Eastman Kodak and RCA Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage means that in 1937, Carlson created his invention by himself and offered his idea to over twenty large companies.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means large business companies.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph E, which explains that Chester Carlson made his discovery in 1937 and tried to offer it to over twenty big companies (such as IBM and General Electric). In the text, the phrase 'offered it to more than 20 major corporations' corresponds to the note's phrase 'invited 20 major to make his discovery'.
Q9 turned him down All of them turned him down, expressing what he later called "an enthusiastic lack of interest" and thereby passing up the opportunity to manufacture what Fortune magazine would describe as "the most successful product ever marketed in America." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that none of the big companies wanted Carlson's idea; instead, every single one of them said no to him.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "turned him down" means that every company rejected him and refused to accept or support his invention.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph E of the text. The summary notes state that Carlson invited 20 major companies, but his idea was not welcomed at first, and "all of them ". In Paragraph E, the passage mentions that Carlson offered his idea to more than 20 major corporations, but "All of them turned him down". Therefore, the exact words to fill in the blank are "turned him down".
Q10 commercial triumph Carlson's invention was indeed a commercial triumph Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Chester Carlson's new machine was truly a huge business success.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "commercial triumph" means a very big success in business or sales.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph F. The sentence in the notes states that Carlson's "creation was undeniably a" certain achievement. In the passage, "invention" matches the word "creation", and "indeed" matches "undeniably". Therefore, Carlson's invention was a "commercial triumph".
Q11 wealthy Xerography eventually made Carlson a very wealthy man Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that his invention, xerography, ultimately brought Carlson a great amount of money and made him rich.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means having a lot of money or being rich.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is confirmed by Paragraph G. The sentence in the notes states that due to xerography, Carlson became "a very [input] person." In the text, it clearly says that xerography made him "a very wealthy man." Here, the word "man" matches "person," and "wealthy" directly fills the blank.
Q12 possessions "His possessions seemed to be composed of the number of things he could easily do without," his second wife said Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Carlson's wife observed that his belongings were mostly things he did not really need and could easily manage without.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "possessions" means the personal belongings or things that someone owns.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is confirmed by paragraph G, which discusses how Carlson lived a very modest and simple life despite being rich. His wife remarked that his "possessions" were things he could easily live without, directly matching the summary statement that "he can live without his possessions."
Q13 charities He spent the last years of his life quietly giving most of his fortune to charities Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that during the final years of his life, Chester Carlson donated almost all of his great wealth to charitable organizations.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means organizations that help people in need or support good causes.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'charities' because paragraph G explains what Carlson did with his wealth near the end of his life. In the text, the phrase 'giving most of his fortune to' matches the summary phrase 'gave lots of his money to'. Therefore, the missing word that tells where he donated his money is 'charities'.
