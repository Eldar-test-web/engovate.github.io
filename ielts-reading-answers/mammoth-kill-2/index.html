Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14-26 which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

Mammoth kill

Mammoth is any species of the extinct genus Mammuthus, proboscideans commonly equipped with long, curved tusks and, in northern species, a covering of long hair. They lived from the Pliocene epoch from around 5 million years ago, into the Holocene at about 4,500 years ago and were members of the family Elephantidae, which contains, along with mammoths, the two genera of modern elephants and their ancestors.

A

Like their modern relatives, mammoths were quite large. The largest known species reached heights in the region of 4 m at the shoulder and weights of up to 8 tonnes, while exceptionally large males may have exceeded 12 tonnes. However, most species of mammoth were only about as large as a modern Asian elephant. Both sexes bore tusks. A first, small set appeared at about the age of six months and these were replaced at about 18 months by the permanent set. Growth of the permanent set was at a rate of about 1 to 6 inches per year. Based on studies of their close relatives, the modern elephants, mammoths probably had a gestation period of 22 months, resulting in a single calf being born. Their social structure was probably the same as that of African and Asian elephants, with females living in herds headed by a matriarch, whilst bulls lived solitary lives or formed loose groups after sexual maturity.

B

MEXICO CITY – Although it’s hard to imagine in this age of urban sprawl and automobiles, North America once belonged to mammoths, camels, ground sloths as large as cows, bear-sized beavers and other formidable beasts. Some 11,000 years ago, however, these large-bodied mammals and others – about 70 species in all – disappeared. Their demise coincided roughly with the arrival of humans in the New World and dramatic climatic change – factors that have inspired several theories about the die-off. Yet despite decades of scientific investigation, the exact cause remains a mystery. Now new findings offer support to one of these controversial hypotheses: that human hunting drove this megafaunal menagerie to extinction. The overkill model emerged in the 1960s when it was put forth by Paul S. Martin of the University of Arizona. Since then, critics have charged that no evidence exists to support the idea that the first Americans hunted to the extent necessary to cause these extinctions. But at the annual meeting of the Society of Vertebrate Paleontology in Mexico City last October, paleoecologist John Alroy of the University of California at Santa Barbara argued that, in fact, hunting-driven extinction is not only plausible, but it was also unavoidable. He has determined, using a computer simulation, that even a very modest amount of hunting would have wiped these animals out.

C

Assuming an initial human population of 100 people that grew no more than 2 percent annually, Alroy determined that if each band of, say, 50 people killed 15 to 20 large mammals a year, humans could have eliminated the animal populations within 1,000 years. Large mammals, in particular, would have been vulnerable to the pressure because they have longer gestation periods than smaller mammals and their young require extended care.

D

Not everyone agrees with Alroy’s assessment. For one, the results depend in part on population-size estimates for the extinct animals – figures that are not necessarily reliable. But a more specific criticism comes from mammalogist Ross D. E. MacPhee of the American Museum of Natural History in New York City, who points out that the relevant archaeological record contains barely a dozen examples of stone points embedded in mammoth bones (and none, it should be noted, are known from other megafaunal remains) – hardly what one might expect if hunting drove these animals to extinction. Furthermore, some of these species had huge ranges – the giant Jefferson’s ground sloth, for example, lived as far north as the Yukon and as far south as Mexico – which would have made slaughtering them in numbers sufficient to cause their extinction rather implausible, he says.

E

MacPhee agrees that humans most likely brought about these extinctions (as well as others around the world that coincided with human arrival), but not directly. Rather he suggests that people may have introduced hyper lethal disease, perhaps through their dogs or hitchhiking vermin, which then spread wildly among the immunologically naive species of the New World. As in the overkill model, populations of large mammals would have a harder time recovering. Repeated outbreaks of a hyper disease could thus quickly drive them to the point of no return. So far MacPhee does not have empirical evidence for the hyper disease hypothesis, and it won’t be easy to come by: a hyper lethal disease would kill far too quickly to leave its signature on the bones themselves. But he hopes that analyses of tissue and DNA from the last mammoths to perish will eventually reveal murderous microbes.

F

The third explanation for what brought on this North American extinction does not involve human beings. Instead, its proponents blame the loss on the weather. The Pleistocene epoch witnessed considerable climatic instability, explains palaeontologist Russell W. Graham of the Denver Museum of Nature and Science. As a result, certain habitats disappeared, and species that had once formed communities split apart. For some animals, this change brought opportunity. For much of the megafauna, however, the increasingly homogeneous environment left them with shrinking geographical ranges – a death sentence for large animals, which need large ranges. Although these creatures managed to maintain viable populations through most of the Pleistocene, the final major fluctuation – the so-called Younger Dryas event – pushed them over the edge, Graham says. For his part, Alroy is convinced that human hunters demolished the titans of the Ice Age. The overkill model explains everything the disease and climate scenarios explain, he asserts, and makes accurate predictions about which species would eventually go extinct. “Personally, I’m a vegetarian,” he remarks, “and I find all of this kind of gross – but believable.”

Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "hunting" because the summary discusses the first theory explaining why large mammals died out 11,000 years ago. Paragraph B mentions that an early theory proposed in the 1960s was that "human hunting" caused these large animals to disappear, which matches the idea that human activity directly caused the extinction.
Q15 overkill model The overkill model emerged in the 1960s when it was put forth by Paul S. Martin of the University of Arizona Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the idea named the "overkill model" was first introduced during the 1960s by a researcher named Paul S. Martin.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "overkill model" refers to the scientific theory proposing that humans hunted and killed so many large animals that it caused their extinction.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "overkill model" because the summary refers to a theory that started in the 1960s and was proposed by an expert. Paragraph B directly mentions that "The overkill model emerged in the 1960s when it was put forth by Paul S. Martin". Here, "emerged" means began, and being "put forth by Paul S. The answer refers to an extremely dangerous illness or infection that caused the large animals to die out.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph E, which explains Ross D. E. MacPhee's theory. MacPhee proposes that humans indirectly caused the extinctions by bringing along a "hyper lethal disease" that quickly infected and killed off the native animal species. In the summary sentence, the word "deadly" matches the passage's description of "hyper lethal", leaving "disease" (or "hyperdisease") as the correct term to fill the blank.

The passage explains that MacPhee does not yet have real physical proof or factual data to support his idea about a deadly disease killing these animals.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means real, observable proof based on scientific tests or facts.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "empirical evidence" because the summary discusses Ross MacPhee's theory about a deadly disease causing extinctions, noting that his idea needs more proof to be verified. In paragraph E, the text states that MacPhee currently "does not have empirical evidence for the hyper disease hypotheses". Therefore, "empirical evidence" accurately fits the blank describing what is missing to test or support his hypothesis.
Q18 climatic instability The Pleistocene epoch witnessed considerable climatic instability, explains palaeontologist Russell W. Graham of the Denver Museum of Nature and Science. As a result, certain habitats disappeared, and species that had once formed communities split apart Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that during the Pleistocene time period, the weather changed a lot and was not stable. Because of these climate changes, the places where animals lived vanished, and groups of animals were separated.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means big and unpredictable changes in weather and temperatures over time.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph F. The summary mentions Graham's third hypothesis about what happened in the Pleistocene epoch that caused species to disappear. In the passage, Russell W. Graham explains that the Pleistocene epoch saw "climatic instability," and because of this ("As a result"), habitats disappeared and animal communities split apart.
Q19 geographical ranges For much of the megafauna, however, the increasingly homogeneous environment left them with shrinking geographical ranges – a death sentence for large animals, which need large ranges Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that as the environment changed, the living areas (geographical ranges) for giant animals became smaller (shrinking), which was deadly for them because big animals require large amounts of territory to survive.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "geographical ranges" means the areas of land where these large animals lived and traveled.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph F, which discusses Russell W. Graham's theory about climate change. The text states that the changing environment left large animals with "shrinking geographical ranges," which acted as "a death sentence" because large creatures require a lot of space. In the summary, the word "shrinking" corresponds to "reduced," and "a death sentence for large animals" corresponds to "posed a dangerous signal to these giants." Therefore, the phrase that correctly completes the blank is "geographical ranges."
Q20 Younger Dryas event Although these creatures managed to maintain viable populations through most of the Pleistocene, the final major fluctuation – the so-called Younger Dryas event – pushed them over the edge, Graham says Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even though these large animals survived most of the time, the last big change in climate, known as the Younger Dryas event, ultimately caused them to die out.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to a specific period of abrupt climate change called the "Younger Dryas event."
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph F, which discusses the third theory proposed by Russell W. Graham regarding climate change. The passage states that while megafauna survived most of the Pleistocene epoch, "the final major fluctuation – the so-called Younger Dryas event – pushed them over the edge." In the summary, "finally wiped them out" matches the phrase "pushed them over the edge" and identifies the event responsible.
Q21 A For his part, Alroy is convinced that human hunters demolished the titans of the Ice Age. The overkill model explains everything the disease and climate scenarios explain, he asserts, and makes accurate predictions about which species would eventually go extinct Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that John Alroy strongly believes humans hunted these giant animals to extinction. He states that the hunting theory correctly predicts which kinds of animals would die out forever.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'A' means that John Alroy believed that human hunting clearly shows which animal types would die out in the end.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because John Alroy supports the 'overkill model', which says that human hunters caused the extinction of these animals. In paragraph F, he states that this hunting model accurately predicts which species would ultimately die out. In this context, 'makes accurate predictions about' matches the idea of 'well explained', and 'which species would eventually go extinct' matches 'which species would finally disappear'.

Q23 A paleoecologist John Alroy of the University of California at Santa Barbara argued that, in fact, hunting-driven extinction is not only plausible, but it was also unavoidable Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that John Alroy believed it was certain that hunting by humans caused the animals to completely die out.

Answer Explanation:

MacPhee does not believe humans caused the mass die-off directly through hunting; instead, he suggests that humans brought "hyper lethal disease" (deadly illness) that spread rapidly among the animals and wiped them out.

Answer Explanation:
The answer B corresponds to Ross D. E. MacPhee.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Ross D. E. MacPhee expressed doubt about the overkill theory by looking at the fossil and archaeological evidence. He observed that only a tiny number of mammoth bones have stone weapon points stuck in them, and other ancient animal bones have none at all. This lack of physical evidence in the fossil remains made him question whether human hunting truly wiped out these beasts.

The answer "C" means that Russell W. Graham believes that changes in the climate were the main cause of the animals dying out.

The correct answer is C because Paragraph F discusses the theory that extinction was caused by nature rather than humans. Palaeontologist Russell W. Graham points out that the time period suffered from "climatic instability" (shifts in climate). He explains that the final big climate change, the Younger Dryas event, finally pushed these large animals to disappear.
