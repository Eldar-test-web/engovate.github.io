The Anderton Boat Lift

Section I

When the Trent and Mersey Canal opened in 1777, the Cheshire town of Anderton was the obvious place to transfer goods to and from the nearby River Weaver. There was just one problem: the canal was fifteen metres above the river.

Pathways, inclined planes, and chutes were constructed to ease the task of moving cargo by hand. Primitive railways were laid to move cargoes, cranes were built, and steam engines were later installed to power lifting. In the early 1870s, however, the Weaver Navigation Trustees decided to eliminate the cost, effort, and wastage involved in hand transportation when the engineers Edward Leader Williams and Edwin Clarke suggested a 'boat carrying lift'.

Section II

Their design was a unique and magnificent example of the Victorians' mastery of cast iron and hydraulics. Completed in 1875, graceful in appearance, simple in use, and above all efficient, the lift was hailed as a marvel of the era, and became a prototype for larger versions on the waterways of France and Belgium.

The operating mechanism consisted of two vertical sets of interconnected hydraulic cylinders and pistons set into the bed of the river and each piston supported a boat-carrying tank 22.86 metres long and 4.72 metres wide. At rest, one tank was level with the canal and the other level with the river and to move the tanks, a small amount of water was removed from the bottom tank making it lighter than the top tank.

Because the two hydraulic cylinders were connected, the heavier top tank moved down and forced hydraulic liquid through the connecting pipe into the other cylinder pushing that piston and the lighter tank upwards. Watertight gates both on the tanks and at the entrance to the canal contained the water while the tanks were moving. A hydraulic pump driven by steam supplied the small amount of additional energy required to effect a reasonably rapid movement and to enable the tanks to be precisely levelled at the end of their journey.

Section III

All went well for the first ten years, then pitting and grooving of the cylinders and pistons occurred. Investigations showed that the canal water used as the hydraulic liquid was contaminated by chemicals and was corrosive, therefore causing the damage.

It was immediately changed to distilled water from the steam engine powering the hydraulic pump. Corrosion was dramatically reduced but the damage had been done.

In addition, the boiler for the steam engine needed renewing, so in 1906 the Trustees ordered the construction of a new lift, to a design by their engineer J A Saner.

Section IV

The new lift was built over the top of the Victorian structure, utilizing the Victorian front and rear columns. The main structure had strong A-frames at either side of the new lift to support the enormous weight of the platform that now formed the top of the framework: on it was located the new operating mechanism, which included seventy-two pulleys weighing up to 35 tonnes each.

Each of the boat-carrying tanks was now suspended on wire ropes which ran from the tank to the top of the lift, around pulleys, and down to cast-iron weights at the side of the structure. These were equal to the weight of the water-filled tank. Turning the pulleys one way or the other moved the ropes, so that one tank was raised or lowered independently of the other tank. Because the tanks were counterbalanced by the weights, only a small electrical motor was required to turn the pulleys and so move the tanks up or down.

Completed in 1908 the lift was reliable, cheap and easy to operate. Unlike the Victorian lift it was not the least bit elegant, but it was functional and it worked.

Section V

Both the 1875 and the 1908 versions carried large volumes of commercial traffic and the principal cargoes transported were coal, china clay, salt, manufactured goods, including china ware, and agricultural produce.

Sadly, trade on inland waterways in Britain declined dramatically in the 1950s, and goods traffic via the lift effectively ended in the 1960s. The 1970s increase in pleasure boating briefly prolonged its active life, but in 1982 the 'Cathedral of the Canals' was finally closed.

Section VI

Demolition seemed inevitable, but, after a long campaign by concerned groups, British Waterways agreed, in 1999, to save the lift.

Some wanted it 'conserved as found', but that would entail replacing much of the existing structure, virtually creating a replica lift. The steel of the 1908 structure had been badly corroded by pollutants from the local chemical industries and would need replacing if it were to support the overhead machinery and 500-tonne counterweights. In addition, safety considerations would require the installation of a back-up braking system.

It was decided, therefore, to revert to the 1875 hydraulically-operated system, using the original cast-iron structure. Although the counterweights had to be removed, the 1908 framework and pulleys would be retained as a static monument.

It was a huge and expensive project, and not without difficulties. Eventually, in 2002, the Anderton Boat Lift was officially reopened. Boat owners and visitors alike can once again ride 'the world's first boat lift'.

Q15 i The operating mechanism consisted of two vertical sets of interconnected hydraulic cylinders and pistons set into the bed of the river and each piston supported a boat-carrying tank 22.86 metres long and 4.72 metres wide Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains the system and parts used to make the original lift work, including connected cylinders and pistons that held and moved the boat tanks.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "i" means the most appropriate title for Section II is "How the original lift worked".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "i" because Section II provides a detailed explanation of the mechanical operation of the first (original) boat lift completed in 1875. The paragraph explicitly details the "operating mechanism", describing how the interconnected hydraulic cylinders, pistons, tanks, water levels, and steam pump functioned together to move boats up and down between the river and the canal.
Q16 e All went well for the first ten years, then pitting and grooving of the cylinders and pistons occurred. Investigations showed that the canal water used as the hydraulic liquid was contaminated by chemicals and was corrosive, therefore causing the damage Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that after ten years of good operation, the metal parts (cylinders and pistons) suffered damage because the water used in the mechanism contained harmful chemicals.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "e" means that the best heading for Section III is "Mechanical problems".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "e" because Section III describes issues and breakdowns with the lift's machinery after ten years of operation. The text mentions "pitting and grooving of the cylinders and pistons", damage caused by corrosive water, and a steam engine boiler that "needed renewing". These issues represent "mechanical problems" that ultimately forced the construction of a new lift.
Q17 h The main structure had strong A-frames at either side of the new lift to support the enormous weight of the platform that now formed the top of the framework: on it was located the new operating mechanism, which included seventy-two pulleys weighing up to 35 tonnes each Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the new lift had strong frames supporting the top structure, and on top of this frame sat the new working machinery, including many large pulleys.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'h' means that this section describes a new structural frame and the new mechanical parts that were built for the lift.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'h' because Section IV details the construction of the new 1908 structure. It specifically explains the new physical frame ('the top of the framework' with 'strong A-frames') and the new mechanical system ('the new operating mechanism' including pulleys, wire ropes, weights, and an electrical motor) used to raise and lower the boat tanks.
Q18 a Both the 1875 and the 1908 versions carried large volumes of commercial traffic and the principal cargoes transported were coal, china clay, salt, manufactured goods, including china ware, and agricultural produce Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that both models of the lift were actively used to carry a lot of trading boats and different kinds of goods.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "a" means the heading for Section V is "The lift in use", which discusses how the boat lift was utilized to transport goods and boats over the years.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "a" because Section V describes how both versions of the lift were operated during their working years. It details the types of commercial cargo transported (such as coal, salt, and manufactured goods) and mentions how the lift was later used for pleasure boating before it was closed.
Q19 c Demolition seemed inevitable, but, after a long campaign by concerned groups, British Waterways agreed, in 1999, to save the lift Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that although it looked like the lift would be destroyed, groups fought to protect it, and British Waterways finally agreed in 1999 to save and fix it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "c" means the best heading for Section VI is "Restoring the lift", which refers to repairing the boat lift and bringing it back into working order.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "c" because Section VI describes the major effort to rescue, repair, and rebuild the boat lift after it was closed down. The text mentions that people campaigned to "save the lift", worked through options to repair and return to the original system, and completed a "huge and expensive project" so that it "officially reopened" in 2002. These details all describe the process of restoring the lift.
Q20 the platform / platform The main structure had strong A-frames at either side of the new lift to support the enormous weight of the platform that now formed the top of the framework: on it was located the new operating mechanism, which included seventy-two pulleys weighing up to 35 tonnes each Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that strong frames supported a very heavy platform at the top of the structure, where the lift's operating parts and heavy pulleys were placed.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to a flat, heavy structure or surface at the top of the framework that held the lift's new operating machinery.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text in Section IV describing the 1908 structure. The passage explains that strong A-frames on both sides were built to hold up the heavy "platform" which made up the top of the structure and held the pulleys and machinery.
Q21 strong A-frames / A-frames / strong A-frame / A-frame The main structure had strong A-frames at either side of the new lift to support the enormous weight of the platform that now formed the top of the framework: on it was located the new operating mechanism, which included seventy-two pulleys weighing up to 35 tonnes each Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the sides of the new lift had strong supports shaped like an "A" to hold up the heavy top platform containing the machinery.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to "A-frames," which is strong structures shaped like the letter "A" that support heavy weight.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Section IV, which describes the construction of the new lift in 1908. It states that strong "A-frames" were placed on both sides of the main structure to hold up the very heavy platform at the top.
Q22 pulleys / pulley Each of the boat-carrying tanks was now suspended on wire ropes which ran from the tank to the top of the lift, around pulleys, and down to cast-iron weights at the side of the structure Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that wire ropes connected the tanks to heavy weights by going around wheels called pulleys at the top of the lift.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to wheels with grooved rims over which ropes pass to help lift and lower the tanks.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text in Section IV, which describes the parts of the 1908 lift mechanism. Wire ropes were attached to each tank, went up to the top of the structure, passed around "pulleys", and connected to weights. Turning these pulleys moved the ropes to raise or lower the tanks.
Q23 boat-carrying tank / tank The operating mechanism consisted of two vertical sets of interconnected hydraulic cylinders and pistons set into the bed of the river and each piston supported a boat-carrying tank 22.86 metres long and 4.72 metres wide Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the machine works using pistons that hold and lift a large tank built to carry boats.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to the large container or "tank" that holds and carries a boat while moving it up or down.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text explaining the mechanism of the boat lift. The lift works by holding boats inside water-filled containers called a "boat-carrying tank" (or simply "tank"), which are supported and moved by hydraulic pistons or wire ropes.
Q24 cast-iron weights / weights / cast-iron weight / weight Each of the boat-carrying tanks was now suspended on wire ropes which ran from the tank to the top of the lift, around pulleys, and down to cast-iron weights at the side of the structure Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says each boat tank hung on wire ropes. The ropes went up to the top, around pulleys, and down to heavy cast-iron weights at the side.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the missing part of the diagram is the cast-iron weights at the end of the ropes.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'cast-iron weights' because the passage says the ropes go 'down to cast-iron weights'. Later, the passage says the tanks were 'counterbalanced by the weights'. Pay attention to the words 'ropes', 'pulleys', and 'down to'. They show what is at the end of the ropes.
Q25 France and Belgium Completed in 1875, graceful in appearance, simple in use, and above all efficient, the lift was hailed as a marvel of the era, and became a prototype for larger versions on the waterways of France and Belgium Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that after the Anderton Boat Lift was completed in 1875, it was used as a model to build bigger lifts in France and Belgium.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to the two European countries, France and Belgium, where bigger versions of the boat lift were made.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Section II, which states that the Anderton Boat Lift served as an early model or 'prototype' for 'larger versions' (similar lifts) constructed along the canals and rivers in 'France and Belgium'.
Q26 a hydraulic pump / hydraulic pump A hydraulic pump driven by steam supplied the small amount of additional energy required to effect a reasonably rapid movement and to enable the tanks to be precisely levelled at the end of their journey Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says a steam-driven hydraulic pump gave a little extra energy. This made the tanks move fairly fast and stop at exactly the right level.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the extra power for moving the tanks came from a hydraulic pump.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "hydraulic pump" because the notes say "Extra power", and the passage says the pump gave "additional energy". "Extra" and "additional" mean the same. The pump is the thing that gave this energy, and it is only two words, so it fits the limit.
Q27 cylinders and pistons then pitting and grooving of the cylinders and pistons occurred. Investigations showed that the canal water used as the hydraulic liquid was contaminated by chemicals and was corrosive, therefore causing the damage Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that using canal water inside the machine caused damage (pitting and grooving) to the parts called cylinders and pistons because the water had harmful chemicals in it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to the metal parts of the machine, specifically the "cylinders and pistons", that got damaged.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "cylinders and pistons" because the text in Section III explains what was harmed when dirty canal water was used. The passage notes that "pitting and grooving of the cylinders and pistons occurred" because the "canal water used as the hydraulic liquid was contaminated by chemicals and was corrosive, therefore causing the damage." In this context, "causing the damage" matches the question's phrase "harmed".
