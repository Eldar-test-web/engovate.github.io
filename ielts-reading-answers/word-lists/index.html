From IELTS Mock Tests Academic Reading Test 57 · Part 2 · Questions 15–27

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 15-27 which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

Word lists

A As any language learner knows, the acquisition of vocabulary is of critical importance. Grammar is useful, yet communication occurs without it. Consider the utterance: 'Me station.' Certainly, 'I'd like to go to the station' is preferable, but a taxi driver will probably head to the right place with 'Me station.' If the passenger uses the word 'airport' instead of 'station', however, the journey may well be fraught. Similarly, 'What time train Glasgow?' signals to a station clerk that a timetable is needed even though 'What time does the train go to Glasgow?' is correct. In both of these requests, nouns - 'station', 'time', 'train', and 'Glasgow' - carry most of the meaning; and, generally speaking, foreign-language learners, like infants in their mother tongue, acquire nouns first. Verbs also contain unequivocal meaning; for instance, 'go' indicates departure, not the arrival. Furthermore, 'Go' is a common word, appearing in both requests above, while 'the' and 'to' are the other frequent items. Thus, for a language learner, there may be two necessities: to acquire both useful and frequent words, including some that function grammatically. It is a daunting fact that English contains around half a million words, of which a graduate knows 25,000. So how does a language learner decide which ones to learn?

B The General Service List (GSL), devised by the American, Michael West, in 1953, was one renowned lexical aid. Consisting of 2,000 headwords, each representing a word family, GSL words were listed alphabetically, with definitions and example sentences, while a number alongside each word showed its number of occurrences per five million words, and a percentage beside each meaning indicated how often that meaning occurred. For 50 years, particularly in the US, the GSL wielded great influence: graded readers and other materials for primary schools were written with reference to it, and American teachers of English as a foreign language (EFL) relying heavily upon it.

C Understandably, West's 1953 GSL has been updated several times because, firstly, his list contained archaisms such as 'shilling', while lacking words that existed in 1953 but which were popularized later, like 'drug', 'OK', 'television', and 'victim'. Naturally, his list did not contain neologisms such as 'email'. However, around 80% of West's original inclusions were still considered valid, according to researchers Billuroğlu and Neufeld (2005). Secondly, what constituted a headword and a word family in West's GSL was not entirely logical, and rules for this were formulated by Bauer and Nation (1995). Thirdly, technological advance has meant that billions of words can now be analysed by computer for frequency, context, and regional variation. West's frequency data was based on a 2.5-million-word corpus drawn from research by Thorndike and Lorge (1944), and some of it was unreliable. A 2013 incarnation of the GSL, called the New General Service List (NGSL), used a 273-million-word subsection of the Cambridge English Corpus (CEC), and research indicates this list provides a higher degree of coverage than West's.

D A partner to the NGSL is the 2013 New Academic Word List (NAWL) with 2,818 headwords – a modification of Averill Coxhead's 2000 AWL. The NAWL excludes NGSL words, focusing on academic language, but, nevertheless, items in it are generally serviceable - they are merely not used often enough to appear in the NGSL. An indication of the difference between the two lists can be seen in just four words: the NGSL begins with 'a' and ends with 'zonings', whereas 'abdominal' and 'yeasts' open and close the NAWL.

E Over time, linguistics and EFL have become more dependent upon computerized statistical analysis, and large bodies of words have been collected to aid academics, teachers, and learners. One such body, known by the Latin word for body, 'corpus', is the CEC, created at Cambridge University in the UK. This well-known collection has two billion words of written and spoken, formal and informal, British, American, and other Englishes. Continually updated, its sources are very wide indeed – far wider than West's. Although the CEC is one of many English-language corpora, it is not the largest, but it was the one used by the creators of the NGSL and the NAWL.

F Still, a learner cannot easily access corpora, and even though the NGSL and NAWL are free online, a learner may not know how best to use them. Linguists have demonstrated that words should be learnt in a context (not singly, not alphabetically); that items in the same lexical set should be learnt together; that it takes at least six different sightings or hearings to learn one item; that written language differs significantly from spoken; and, that concrete language is easier to acquire than abstract. Admittedly, a list of a few thousand words is not so hard to learn, but language learning is not only about frequency and utility, but also about passion and poetry. Who cares if a word you like isn't in the top 5,000? If you like it or the way it sounds, you're likely to learn it. And, if you use it correctly, at least your IELTS examiner will be impressed.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q15 iii The General Service List (GSL), devised by the American, Michael West, in 1953, was one renowned lexical aid. Consisting of 2,000 headwords, each representing a word family, GSL words were listed alphabetically, with definitions and example sentences, while a number alongside each word showed its number of occurrences per five million words, and a percentage beside each meaning indicated how often that meaning occurred Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage describes what the General Service List (GSL) looks like, how many words are in it, how it is organized, and what extra details (like definitions and numbers showing word frequency) are given for each word.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'iii' corresponds to the heading 'Description of the GSL', which means an explanation of what the General Service List is and what it contains.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading iii because Paragraph B introduces the General Service List (GSL) created by Michael West and provides a detailed breakdown of its structure. The paragraph explains that it contains 2,000 headwords, is arranged alphabetically, includes definitions and example sentences, and provides statistics on word frequency and meaning. Therefore, the paragraph serves as a clear description of the GSL.
Q16 vii Understandably, West's 1953 GSL has been updated several times because, firstly, his list contained archaisms such as 'shilling', while lacking words that existed in 1953 but which were popularized later, like 'drug', 'OK', 'television', and 'victim' Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that West's 1953 vocabulary list had to be remade and updated for several reasons, starting with the fact that it contained very old words and missed many common modern words.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Paragraph C mainly explains the causes and needs for changing, modernizing, and improving the General Service List (GSL).
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is vii because Paragraph C directly outlines the reasons why the original 1953 GSL had to be changed and improved over time. The paragraph explicitly introduces this topic with 'West's 1953 GSL has been updated several times because...' and then lists the specific points using linking words: 'firstly' (old words and missing modern words), 'Secondly' (unclear rules for word families), and 'Thirdly' (better computer technology to analyze language data). Here, the word 'overhauling' means completely revising or updating something to make it better, which matches 'updated several times'.
Q17 ii The NAWL excludes NGSL words, focusing on academic language, but, nevertheless, items in it are generally serviceable - they are merely not used often enough to appear in the NGSL. An indication of the difference between the two lists can be seen in just four words: the NGSL begins with 'a' and ends with 'zonings', whereas 'abdominal' and 'yeasts' open and close the NAWL Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains the differences between the two word lists, pointing out what each list includes or excludes and comparing the words that start and finish each list.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "ii" means that Paragraph D is best summarized by the heading "Comparison of the NGSL and the NAWL".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is ii because Paragraph D discusses how the New General Service List (NGSL) and the New Academic Word List (NAWL) relate to each other. It explains that the NAWL leaves out words from the NGSL to focus on academic vocabulary, and it highlights "the difference between the two lists" by comparing their first and last words.
Q18 ix Over time, linguistics and EFL have become more dependent upon computerized statistical analysis, and large bodies of words have been collected to aid academics, teachers, and learners. One such body, known by the Latin word for body, 'corpus', is the CEC, created at Cambridge University in the UK Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that as time went on, researchers began gathering huge collections of words with computers to help teachers and students, and it introduces these collections under the name 'corpus'.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'ix' means that Paragraph E is mainly about the arrival and creation of large language databases, which are called 'corpora' (the plural form of 'corpus').
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is ix because Paragraph E describes how linguistic study began to use computer data, leading to the creation of huge collections of language known as 'corpora'. The word 'advent' means the arrival or start of something new. In the paragraph, the author explains that large word databases ('corpus' / 'corpora') were collected over time, introducing the Cambridge English Corpus (CEC) and mentioning that many such corpora now exist.
Q19 v Still, a learner cannot easily access corpora, and even though the NGSL and NAWL are free online, a learner may not know how best to use them Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage points out that language students have trouble using corpora easily and often do not know the best way to study from word lists, showing the clear limitations and difficulties with both tools.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'v' means having doubts, hesitations, or pointing out limitations regarding the use of word lists and language corpora.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading v because Paragraph F focuses on the drawbacks and limitations of relying only on word lists and corpora. The paragraph explains that students cannot easily access corpora, often do not know how to effectively study from lists like the NGSL and NAWL, and that memorizing isolated words goes against how language is naturally learned. The word 'reservations' means doubts or concerns, which directly reflects the paragraph's discussion about the problems with studying solely from lists and corpora.
Q20 A The General Service List (GSL), devised by the American, Michael West, in 1953, was one renowned lexical aid Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that Michael West created the vocabulary tool called the General Service List (GSL) in the year 1953.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'A' means that Michael West is the person who made the General Service List (GSL).
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because paragraph B clearly states that the General Service List (GSL) was 'devised by the American, Michael West, in 1953'. In English, the verb 'devise' is a direct synonym for 'create'. Therefore, Michael West is the one who created the list.
Q21 E A partner to the NGSL is the 2013 New Academic Word List (NAWL) with 2,818 headwords – a modification of Averill Coxhead's 2000 AWL Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that a newer list was made by changing the 2000 AWL, which was originally made by Averill Coxhead.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "E" means that Coxhead is the person who made the word list called the AWL.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because paragraph D mentions that the 2013 list is "a modification of Averill Coxhead's 2000 AWL." This tells us that the AWL belongs to and was originally created by Averill Coxhead.
Q22 C Secondly, what constituted a headword and a word family in West's GSL was not entirely logical, and rules for this were formulated by Bauer and Nation (1995) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Michael West did not have logical rules for defining headwords and word families, so Bauer and Nation made the standard rules for them.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'C' means that Bauer and Nation are the people who created official rules to define headwords and word families.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Paragraph C explains that Michael West's definition of a headword and a word family was not completely logical. Therefore, Bauer and Nation (1995) created the specific guidelines for them. In this context, the phrase 'rules for this were formulated by' means the same thing as the keyword 'standardised'.
Q23 B However, around 80% of West's original inclusions were still considered valid, according to researchers Billuroğlu and Neufeld (2005) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that researchers Billuroğlu and Neufeld found that about 80% of the words West originally put in his list were still valid and useful.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means that the researchers named Billuroğlu and Neufeld are the ones who checked if the words in the GSL were still appropriate and correct.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because paragraph C mentions that researchers Billuroğlu and Neufeld (2005) looked at West's original list and found that around 80% of the included words were still "considered valid". Here, checking whether the list's inclusions were "valid" matches reviewing the list for "content validity".
Q24 D West's frequency data was based on a 2.5-million-word corpus drawn from research by Thorndike and Lorge (1944), and some of it was unreliable Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Michael West relied on 1944 research by Thorndike and Lorge, which was limited to only 2.5 million words and was not completely reliable.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is D because Thorndike and Lorge are the researchers whose older study was limited or small in scope.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Paragraph C mentions that Michael West's word data came from an earlier study in 1944 by Thorndike and Lorge. That study used a corpus of only 2.5 million words, which is described as far narrower and less comprehensive than modern databases containing hundreds of millions or billions of words. Key words in the text include 'research by Thorndike and Lorge (1944)' and '2.5-million-word corpus', which contrast with newer, far wider research.
Q25 2 billion / two billion This well-known collection has two billion words of written and spoken, formal and informal, British, American, and other Englishes Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says the Cambridge English Corpus is a famous group of texts. It has two billion words from writing and speech, formal and informal, and from many kinds of English.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the whole Cambridge English Corpus has two billion words.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is '2 billion' because paragraph E says the corpus 'has two billion words.' Do not choose '273-million-word' from paragraph C. That is only a 'subsection' (a small part) of the corpus, not the 'complete' one.
Q26 6 / Six that it takes at least six different sightings or hearings to learn one item Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage means that a person needs to see or hear a word at least six times in order to learn it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means the number 6 (or "six").
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "6" (or "six") because Paragraph F discusses how students learn vocabulary. The text states that linguists found a learner needs "at least six different sightings or hearings" to learn a word. Therefore, a learner must see or hear a word a minimum of six times.
Q27 Passion and poetry language learning is not only about frequency and utility, but also about passion and poetry Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that to learn a language, it is not just about using practical or common words; people also need strong interest, emotion, and an appreciation for the beauty of language.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means having strong feelings of excitement and an appreciation for the beauty or sound of words.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "Passion and poetry" because the writer notes in paragraph F that learning words is not merely about how often a word is used (frequency) or how useful it is (utility). The writer explains that "language learning is not only about frequency and utility, but also about passion and poetry." Therefore, besides utility and frequency, having a feeling or sense of passion and poetry is also important for learning words.
