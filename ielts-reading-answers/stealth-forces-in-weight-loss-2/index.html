Reading Passage

Stealth Forces in Weight Loss

The field of weight loss is like the ancient fable about the blind men and the elephant. Each man investigates a different part of the animal and reports back, only to discover their findings are bafflingly incompatible.

A The various findings by public-health experts, physicians, psychologists, geneticists, molecular biologists, and nutritionists are about as similar as an elephant's tusk is to its tail. Some say obesity is largely predetermined by our genes and biology; others attribute it to an overabundance of fries, soda, and screen-sucking; still others think we're fat because of viral infection, insulin, or the metabolic conditions we encountered in the womb. "Everyone subscribes to their own little theory," says Robert Berkowitz, medical director of the Center for Weight and Eating Disorders at the University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine. We're programmed to hang onto the fat we have, and some people are predisposed to create and carry more fat than others. Diet and exercise help, but in the end the solution will inevitably be more complicated than pushing away the plate and going for a walk. "It's not as simple as 'You're fat because you're lazy,'" says Nikhil Dhurandhar, an associate professor at Pennington Biomedical Research Center in Baton Rouge. "Willpower is not a prerogative of thin people. It's distributed equally."

B Science may still be years away from giving us a miracle formula for fat-loss. The hormone leptin is a crucial player in the brain's weight-management circuitry. Some people produce too little leptin; others become desensitized to it. And when obese people lose weight, their leptin levels plummet along with their metabolism. The body becomes more efficient at using fuel and conserving fat, which makes it tough to keep the weight off. Obese dieters' bodies go into a state of chronic hunger, a feeling Rudolph Leibel, an obesity researcher at Columbia University, compares to thirst. "Some people might be able to tolerate chronic thirst, but the majority couldn't stand it," says Leibel. "Is that a behavioral problem—a lack of willpower? I don't think so."

C The government has long espoused moderate daily exercise—of the evening-walk or take-the-stairs variety—but that may not do much to budge the needle on the scale. A 150-pound person burns only 150 calories on a half-hour walk, the equivalent of two apples. It's good for the heart, less so for the gut. "Radical changes are necessary," says Deirdre Barrett, a psychologist at Harvard Medical School and author of Waistland. "People don't lose weight by choosing the small fries or taking a little walk every other day." Barrett suggests taking a cue from the members of the National Weight Control Registry (NWCR), a self-selected group of more than 5,000 successful weight-losers who have shed an average of 66 pounds and kept it off 5.5 years. Some registry members lost weight using low-carb diets; some went low-fat; others eliminated refined foods. Some did it on their own; others relied on counseling. That said, not everyone can lose 66 pounds and not everyone needs to. The goal shouldn't be getting thin, but getting healthy. It's enough to whittle your weight down to the low end of your set range, says Jeffrey Friedman, a geneticist at Rockefeller University. Losing even 10 pounds vastly decreases your risk of diabetes, heart disease, and high blood pressure. The point is to not give up just because you don't look like a swimsuit model.

D The negotiation between your genes and the environment begins on day one. Your optimal weight, writ by genes, appears to get edited early on by conditions even before birth, inside the womb. If a woman has high blood-sugar levels while she's pregnant, her children are more likely to be overweight or obese, according to a study of almost 10,000 mother-child pairs. Maternal diabetes may influence a child's obesity risk through a process called metabolic imprinting, says Teresa Hillier, an endocrinologist with Kaiser Permanente's Center for Health Research and the study's lead author. The implication is clear: Weight may be established very early on, and obesity largely passed from mother to child. Numerous studies in both animals and humans have shown that a mother's obesity directly increases her child's risk for weight gain. The best advice for moms-to-be: Get fit before you get pregnant. You'll reduce your risk of complications during pregnancy and increase your chances of having a normal-weight child.

E It's the $64,000 question: Which diets work? It got people wondering: Isn't there a better way to diet? A study seemed to offer an answer. The paper compared two groups of adults: those who, after eating, secreted high levels of insulin, a hormone that sweeps blood sugar out of the bloodstream and promotes its storage as fat, and those who secreted less. Within each group, half were put on a low-fat diet and half on a low-glycemic-load diet. On average, the low-insulin-secreting group fared the same on both diets, losing nearly 10 pounds in the first six months — but they gained about half of it back by the end of the 18-month study. The high-insulin group didn't do as well on the low-fat plan, losing about 4.5 pounds, and gaining back more than half by the end. But the most successful were the high-insulin-secretors on the low-glycemic-load diet. They lost nearly 13 pounds and kept it off.

F What if your fat is caused not by diet or genes, but by germs—say, a virus? It sounds like a sci-fi horror movie, but research suggests some dimension of the obesity epidemic may be attributable to infection by common viruses, says Dhurandhar. The idea of “infectobesity” came to him 20 years ago when he was a young doctor treating obesity in Bombay. He discovered that a local avian virus, SMAM-1, caused chickens to die, sickened with organ damage but also, strangely, with lots of abdominal fat. In experiments, Dhurandhar found that SMAM-1-infected chickens became obese on the same diet as uninfected ones, which stayed svelte.

G He later moved to the U.S. and onto a bona fide human virus, adenovirus 36 (AD-36). In the lab, every species of animal Dhurandhar infected with the virus became obese—chickens got fat, mice got fat, even rhesus monkeys at the zoo that picked up the virus from the environment suddenly gained 15 percent of their body weight upon exposure. In his latest studies, Dhurandhar has isolated a gene that, when blocked from expressing itself, seems to turn off the virus's fattening power. Stem cells extracted from fat cells and then exposed to AD-36 reliably blossom into fat cells—but when stem cells are exposed to an AD-36 virus with the key gene inhibited, the stem cells don't differentiate. The gene appears to be necessary and sufficient to trigger AD-36-related obesity, and the goal is to use the research to create a sort of obesity vaccine. Researchers have discovered 10 microbes so far that trigger obesity—seven of them viruses. It may be a long shot, but for people struggling desperately to be thin, even the possibility of an alternative cause of obesity offers some solace. "They feel better knowing there may be something beyond them that could be responsible," says Dhurandhar. "The thought that there could be something besides what they've heard all their lives—that they are greedy and lazy—helps."

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q14 E Within each group, half were put on a low-fat diet and half on a low-glycemic-load diet. On average, the low-insulin-secreting group fared the same on both diets, losing nearly 10 pounds in the first six months — but they gained about half of it back by the end of the 18-month study. The high-insulin group didn't do as well on the low-fat plan, losing about 4.5 pounds, and gaining back more than half by the end. But the most successful were the high-insulin-secretors on the low-glycemic-load diet. They lost nearly 13 pounds and kept it off Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains a study where people tried two different types of diets (low-fat and low-glycemic-load) and measures how much weight the participants lost and regained on each diet over time.
Answer Explanation:
The answer E means that Section E contains information assessing how effective different types of diets are at helping people lose weight.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because Section E describes a study that compared the results of different eating plans, specifically a "low-fat diet" and a "low-glycemic-load diet." The paragraph evaluates how many pounds people lost and kept off on each diet according to their body's insulin levels.
Q15 D If a woman has high blood-sugar levels while she's pregnant, her children are more likely to be overweight or obese, according to a study of almost 10,000 mother-child pairs Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that a research study tested nearly 10,000 pairs of mothers and their children to see if high blood sugar in pregnant women makes their children more likely to gain weight.
Answer Explanation:
The answer D refers to Section D of the passage, which describes a scientific study that looked at family members (mothers and their children).
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because paragraph D mentions a study that examined "almost 10,000 mother-child pairs." The phrase "mother-child pairs" matches the keyword "relatives of participants" in the question, as mothers and their children are related family members.
Q16 C Barrett suggests taking a cue from the members of the National Weight Control Registry (NWCR), a self-selected group of more than 5,000 successful weight-losers who have shed an average of 66 pounds and kept it off 5.5 years Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that there is a group called the National Weight Control Registry made up of thousands of people who lost a lot of weight and managed to stay at their lower weight for more than five years.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Section C mentions a real group of people who succeeded in losing weight and did not gain it back right away.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because paragraph C talks about the "National Weight Control Registry (NWCR)", which is described as a "group of more than 5,000 successful weight-losers" who lost an average of 66 pounds and "kept it off 5.5 years". This directly provides an example of people who did not immediately regain their lost weight.
Q17 B Obese dieters' bodies go into a state of chronic hunger, a feeling Rudolph Leibel, an obesity researcher at Columbia University, compares to thirst Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that when overweight people diet, their bodies enter a state of constant, ongoing hunger, which is similar to feeling constantly thirsty.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "B" means that Section B discusses how dieters experience long-term hunger when they lose weight.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Section B explicitly discusses how people trying to lose weight enter a condition of ongoing or lasting hunger. The text uses the key phrase "chronic hunger" to describe this "long term hunger" and explains that while some individuals might tolerate it, most people cannot handle it over time.
Q18 G The gene appears to be necessary and sufficient to trigger AD-36-related obesity, and the goal is to use the research to create a sort of obesity vaccine Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that scientists have identified the specific gene responsible for virus-linked weight gain, and their aim is to use these findings to develop a vaccine against obesity.
Answer Explanation:
The answer identifies Paragraph G as the section describing ongoing scientific experiments that could result in a practical solution (like a vaccine) for weight management, outside of food choices or genetic inheritance.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is G because the paragraph details Dhurandhar's continued lab experiments investigating the AD-36 virus in animals and stem cells. It mentions that his latest experiments aim to develop a real-world medical solution: "the goal is to use the research to create a sort of obesity vaccine." This vaccine represents a practical application beyond typical diet control or hereditary factors.
Q19 C "It's not as simple as 'You're fat because you're lazy,'" says Nikhil Dhurandhar, an associate professor at Pennington Biomedical Research Center in Baton Rouge. "Willpower is not a prerogative of thin people. It's distributed equally." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Nikhil Dhurandhar stating that being overweight is not just caused by laziness and that willpower belongs equally to everyone, meaning weight control involves deeper biological factors beyond simple effort.
Answer Explanation:
The answer C means that Nikhil Dhurandhar is the researcher linked to this idea.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because in Paragraph A, Nikhil Dhurandhar explains that body weight is not simply a matter of a person being lazy or lacking self-control. Thin and heavy people share willpower equally, indicating that biological factors and pre-programmed traits play a major role in body weight.
Q20 F Maternal diabetes may influence a child's obesity risk through a process called metabolic imprinting, says Teresa Hillier, an endocrinologist with Kaiser Permanente's Center for Health Research and the study's lead author Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Teresa Hillier found that a pregnant woman's blood sugar issues can influence and raise her child's chance of having weight problems later.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "F" refers to researcher Teresa Hillier.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because Paragraph D discusses research led by Teresa Hillier showing that a mother's high blood sugar or diabetes during pregnancy can affect her child's risk of becoming overweight through metabolic imprinting. The text directly connects Teresa Hillier to findings about how conditions in the womb and maternal weight/health affect the unborn child (fetus).
Q21 E The goal shouldn't be getting thin, but getting healthy. It's enough to whittle your weight down to the low end of your set range, says Jeffrey Friedman, a geneticist at Rockefeller University. Losing even 10 pounds vastly decreases your risk of diabetes, heart disease, and high blood pressure. The point is to not give up just because you don't look like a swimsuit model Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that geneticist Jeffrey Friedman believes the real target of losing weight is to get healthier and lower disease risks, not to look like a model.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "E" refers to Jeffrey Friedman, who stated that the purpose of losing weight is to improve health rather than just looking good.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because in Paragraph C, Jeffrey Friedman explains that losing weight should focus on becoming healthy rather than simply trying to look thin or attractive like a "swimsuit model." Key words and synonyms include "goal" (aim), "getting healthy" (keeping healthy), and "swimsuit model" (attractiveness).
Q22 D "Radical changes are necessary," says Deirdre Barrett, a psychologist at Harvard Medical School and author of Waistland. "People don't lose weight by choosing the small fries or taking a little walk every other day." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Deirdre Barrett saying that people need to make big changes to lose weight, because small habits like eating smaller fries or taking short walks do not work.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" refers to Deirdre Barrett, who stated that making tiny changes to your daily habits is not enough to lose a significant amount of weight.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because in Paragraph C, psychologist Deirdre Barrett explains that "Radical changes are necessary" and points out that "People don't lose weight by choosing the small fries or taking a little walk every other day." Here, "choosing the small fries or taking a little walk" matches the idea of "small changes in lifestyle," which she argues will not lead to real weight reduction.
Q23 A "Everyone subscribes to their own little theory," says Robert Berkowitz, medical director of the Center for Weight and Eating Disorders at the University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Robert Berkowitz explaining that each researcher or expert follows their own specific idea or belief about obesity and weight loss.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "A" refers to the researcher Robert Berkowitz.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because in Paragraph A, Robert Berkowitz is quoted as saying, "Everyone subscribes to their own little theory." This reflects the idea that different experts have their own individual perspectives and points of view regarding the causes and solutions of weight loss and obesity.
Q24 Chickens The idea of “infectobesity” came to him 20 years ago when he was a young doctor treating obesity in Bombay. He discovered that a local avian virus, SMAM-1, caused chickens to die, sickened with organ damage but also, strangely, with lots of abdominal fat. In experiments, Dhurandhar found that SMAM-1-infected chickens became obese on the same diet as uninfected ones, which stayed svelte Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that when Dr. Dhurandhar was a doctor in Bombay, he investigated how the avian virus SMAM-1 affected chickens and ran tests showing that infected chickens became fat.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "Chickens" refers to the birds that the young doctor studied in his early experiments in Bombay.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph F, which discusses Dr. Dhurandhar's early work in Bombay where he developed the idea of "infectobesity." It states that he ran experiments on "chickens" infected with the SMAM-1 virus and observed that they became obese compared to uninfected ones.
Q25 AD-36 / adenovirus 36 He later moved to the U.S. and onto a bona fide human virus, adenovirus 36 (AD-36) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that when the researcher relocated to the United States, he began studying a human virus called adenovirus 36, or AD-36.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "AD-36" refers to the specific name of the human virus that the researcher studied after moving to the United States.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "AD-36" because the summary asks for the name of the new virus tested after the doctor moved to America. Paragraph G directly states that after moving to the U.S., he studied a human virus named "adenovirus 36 (AD-36)". Since the task requires no more than one word, "AD-36" correctly fits the blank.
Q26 Gene In his latest studies, Dhurandhar has isolated a gene that, when blocked from expressing itself, seems to turn off the virus's fattening power Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that the researcher found and separated a specific gene which, when stopped from working, stops the virus from making subjects fat.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to a basic unit of genetic material ("gene") found in cells.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "gene" (or "Gene") because the summary describes something that can be isolated ("separated") to stop the virus from acting ("block the expressing power of the virus"). In Paragraph G, the text states that researcher Nikhil Dhurandhar "isolated a gene that, when blocked from expressing itself, seems to turn off the virus's fattening power." Thus, "gene" precisely fits the blank.
Q27 vaccine The gene appears to be necessary and sufficient to trigger AD-36-related obesity, and the goal is to use the research to create a sort of obesity vaccine Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that researchers have identified the specific gene that causes this type of weight gain, and their future target is to use this finding to make a vaccine against obesity.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "vaccine" refers to a medical substance designed to protect people against an illness or condition, in this case, obesity caused by a virus.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "vaccine" because the summary asks what the doctor aims to develop in the future to fight the virus. In Paragraph G, the text explains that after finding the gene responsible for the virus's fattening effects, "the goal is to use the research to create a sort of obesity vaccine." The word "goal" matches "aiming at," "create" matches "developing," and "vaccine" fits the blank perfectly within the one-word limit.
