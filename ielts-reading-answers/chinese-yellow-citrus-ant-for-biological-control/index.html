Chinese Yellow Citrus Ant For BIOLOGICAL CONTROL - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

Reading Passage

Chinese Yellow Citrus Ant for BIOLOGICAL CONTROL

A In 1476, the farmers of Berne in Switzerland decided, according to this story, there was only one way to rid their fields of the cutworms attacking their crops. They took the pests to court. The worms were tried, found guilty and excommunicated by the archbishop. In China, farmers had a more practical approach to pest control. Rather than rely on divine intervention, they put their faith in frogs, ducks and ants. Frogs and ducks were encouraged to mop up the pests in the paddies and the occasional plague of locusts. But the notion of biological control began with an ant. More specifically, the story says, it started with the predatory yellow citrus ant is a type of weaver ant, which has been polishing off pests in the orange groves of southern China for at least 1700 years. The yellow citrus ant is a type of weaver ant, which binds leaves and twigs with silk to form a neat, tent-like nest. In the beginning, farmers made do with the odd ants' nest here and there. But it wasn't long before growing demand led to the development of a thriving trade in nests and a new type of agriculture—ant farming.

B For an insect that bites, the yellow citrus ant is remarkably popular. Even by ant standards, Oecophylla smaragdina is a fearsome predator. It's big, runs fast and has a powerful nip - painful to humans but lethal to many of the insects that plague the orange groves of Guangdong and Guangxi in southern China. And for at least 17 centuries, Chinese orange growers have harnessed these sixlegged killing machines to keep their fruit groves healthy and productive. The story explains that citrus - a fruits evolved in the Far East and the Chinese discovered the delights of their flesh early on. As the ancestral home of oranges, lemons and pomelos, China also has the greatest diversity of citrus pests. And the trees that produce the sweetest fruits, the mandarins—or kan-attract a host of planteating insects, from black ants and sap-sucking mealy bugs to leaf-devouring caterpillars. With so many enemies, fruit growers clearly had to have some way of protecting their orchards.

C The West did not discover the Chinese orange growers' secret weapon until the early 20th century. At the time, Florida was suffering an epidemic of: citrus canker and in 1915 Walter Swingle, a plant physiologist working for the US Department of Agriculture, was, the story says, sent to China in search of varieties of orange that were resistant to the disease. Swingle spent some time studying the citrus orchards around Guangzhou, and there he came across the story of the cultivated ant. These ants, he was told, were "grown" by the people of a small village nearby who sold them to the orange growers by the nestful.

D The earliest report of citrus ants at work among the orange trees appears in a book on tropical and subtropical botany written by Hsi Han in AD 304. "The people of Chiao-Chih sell in their markets ants in bags of rush matting. The nests are like silk. The bags are all attached to twigs and leaves which, with the ants inside the nests, are for sale. The ants are reddish-yellow in colour, bigger than ordinary ants. In the south if the kan trees do not have this kind of ant, the fruits will all be damaged by many harmful insects, and not a single fruit will be perfect."

E Initially, farmers relied on nests which they collected from the wild or bought in the market — where trade in nests was brisk. 'It is said that in the south orange trees which are free of ants will have wormy fruits. Therefore the people race to buy nests for their orange trees,' wrote Liu Hsun in Strange Things Noted in the South, written about AD 890. The business quickly became more sophisticated. From the 10th century, country people began to trap ants in artificial nests baited with fat. "Fruit growing families buy these ants from vendors who make a business of collecting and selling such creatures," wrote Chuang Chi-Yu in 1130. "They trap them by filling hogs' or sheep's bladders with fat and placing them with the cavities open next to the ants' nests. They wait until the ants have migrated into the bladders and take them away. This is known as 'rearing orange ants'." Farmers attached the bladders to their trees, and in time the ants spread to other trees and built new nests. By the 17th century, growers were building bamboo walkways between their trees to speed the colonization of their orchards. The ants ran along these narrow bridges from one tree to another and established nests "by the hundreds of thousands".

F Did it work? The orange growers clearly thought so. One authority, Chi Ta—Chun, writing in 1700, stressed how important it was to keep the fruit trees free of insect pests, especially caterpillars. "It is essential to eliminate them so that the trees are not injured. But hand labour is not nearly as efficient as ant power..." Swingle was just as impressed. Yet despite these reports, many Western biologists were skeptical. In the West, the idea of using one insect to destroy another was new and highly controversial. The first breakthrough had come in 1888, when the infant orange industry in California had been saved from extinction by the Australian vedalia beetle. This beetle was the only thing that had made any inroad into the explosion of cottony cushion scale that was threatening to destroy the state's citrus crops. But, as Swingle now knew, California’s "first" was nothing of the sort. The Chinese had been expert in biocontrol for many centuries.

G The story goes on to say that the long tradition of ants in the Chinese orchards only began to waver in the 1950s and 1960s with the introduction of powerful organic chemical insecticides. Although most fruit growers switched to chemicals, a few hung onto their ants. Those who abandoned ants in favour of chemicals quickly became disillusioned. As costs soared and pests began to develop resistance to the chemicals, growers began to revive the old ant patrols. They had good reason to have faith in their insect workforce. Research in the early 1960s showed that as long as there were enough ants in the trees, they did an excellent job of dispatching some pests—mainly the larger insects—and had modest success against others. Trees with yellow ants produced almost 20 per cent more healthy leaves than those without. More recent trials have shown that these trees yield just as big a crop as those protected by expensive chemical sprays.

H One apparent drawback of using ants—and one of the main reasons for the early skepticism by Western scientists—was that citrus ants do nothing to control mealy bugs, waxy-coated scale insects which can do considerable damage to fruit trees. In fact, the ants protect mealy bugs in exchange for the sweet honeydew they secrete. The orange growers always denied this was a problem but Western scientists thought they knew better. Research in the 1980s suggests that the growers were right all along. Where mealy bugs proliferate under the ants' protection they are usually heavily parasitized and this limits the harm they can do. Orange growers who rely on carnivorous ants rather than poisonous chemicals maintain a better balance of species in their orchards. While the ants deal with the bigger insect pests, other predatory species keep down the numbers of smaller pests such as scale insects and aphids. In the long run, ants do a lot less damage than chemicals-and they're certainly more effective than excommunication.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q14 F The earliest report of citrus ants at work among the orange trees appears in a book on tropical and subtropical botany written by Hsi Han in AD 304 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the oldest written document showing ants being used in orange orchards comes from a book written in the year AD 304.
Answer Explanation:
The answer F refers to the time period AD 304 (labeled as AD), which is when the very first written account of using ants against pests was created.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because Paragraph D states that the earliest written record about citrus ants working in orange trees is found in a botany book written in AD 304. Key matching phrases include "earliest report" (synonym for "First record") and "citrus ants at work among the orange trees" (matching "ant against pests").
Q15 C in 1915 Walter Swingle, a plant physiologist working for the US Department of Agriculture, was, the story says, sent to China in search of varieties of orange that were resistant to the disease. Swingle spent some time studying the citrus orchards around Guangzhou, and there he came across the story of the cultivated ant Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in 1915, Walter Swingle went to China and spent time studying fruit orchards, where he learned about farmers raising and using ants.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "C" means that the year 1915 is when Walter Swingle (WS) went to China and learned about using ants to protect orange trees.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Paragraph C states that in the year "1915", "Walter Swingle" (referred to by initials WS) was sent to "China". While "studying the citrus orchards", he discovered the method of using farmed ants to protect fruit trees ("the story of the cultivated ant"). Therefore, 1915 matches description C.
Q16 A The first breakthrough had come in 1888, when the infant orange industry in California had been saved from extinction by the Australian vedalia beetle Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in 1888, California's young orange-growing business was saved from being wiped out thanks to a type of insect called the Australian vedalia beetle.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'A' represents the year 1888.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because paragraph F states that the first major success with using an insect in the West happened in 1888, when the orange industry in California was rescued from destruction by an Australian beetle. In the text, "first breakthrough" matches "first case," "saved from extinction" matches "rescued," "orange industry" matches "orange crops," and "California" / "In the West" refers to the "western world."
Q17 G The story goes on to say that the long tradition of ants in the Chinese orchards only began to waver in the 1950s and 1960s with the introduction of powerful organic chemical insecticides. Although most fruit growers switched to chemicals, a few hung onto their ants Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that during the 1950s and 1960s, chemical insect sprays were introduced, leading most Chinese fruit farmers to switch from using ants to using chemical methods.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "G" refers to the year 1950 (the 1950s), which is when Chinese orange growers began switching from using predatory ants to using chemical insecticides.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is G because Paragraph G states that the ancient practice of using ants in Chinese orchards started to change in the 1950s when powerful organic chemical insecticides were introduced. During this time, most fruit growers chose to switch to chemicals instead of using ants. The year 1950 corresponds directly to choice G.
Q18 E "Fruit growing families buy these ants from vendors who make a business of collecting and selling such creatures," wrote Chuang Chi-Yu in 1130. "They trap them by filling hogs' or sheep's bladders with fat and placing them with the cavities open next to the ants' nests. They wait until the ants have migrated into the bladders and take them away. This is known as 'rearing orange ants'." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that in 1130 a writer named Chuang Chi-Yu described how people trap ants. They put fat inside animal bladders and place them near ant nests. The ants move in, and people take them away.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the book that described ways to trap ants was written in 1130, which is choice E.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because the keywords 'trap them' match 'trap ants' in the question, and 'wrote Chuang Chi-Yu in 1130' gives the year. Other years do not talk about trapping ants. For example, AD 304 (F) is about ants sold in markets.
Q19 TRUE As the ancestral home of oranges, lemons and pomelos, China also has the greatest diversity of citrus pests Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that because China is where citrus fruits like oranges, lemons, and pomelos originally come from, it also has the largest variety of pests that attack citrus trees.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means that the passage confirms that China has the highest number of different kinds of citrus pests compared to anywhere else.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph B clearly states that China has the "greatest diversity of citrus pests." In this context, "greatest diversity" means the largest variety or most types of pests. Therefore, the statement matches the information given in the passage.
Q20 FALSE At the time, Florida was suffering an epidemic of: citrus canker and in 1915 Walter Swingle, a plant physiologist working for the US Department of Agriculture, was, the story says, sent to China in search of varieties of orange that were resistant to the disease Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Walter Swingle went to China for the US Department of Agriculture to find orange tree types that could survive the plant disease citrus canker.
Answer Explanation:
The answer FALSE means that the statement does not match what the passage says. Walter Swingle was not sent to China to find an insect.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because Paragraph C clearly explains the real reason Walter Swingle traveled to China. The US Department of Agriculture sent him to look for types of orange trees ("varieties of orange") that could resist a plant disease called citrus canker, not to look for an insect. He only learned about the citrus ants by chance while visiting orchards in China.
Q21 FALSE Swingle was just as impressed. Yet despite these reports, many Western biologists were skeptical. In the West, the idea of using one insect to destroy another was new and highly controversial Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even though Swingle liked the idea, many scientists in the West did not easily believe it and thought the method was controversial.
Answer Explanation:
The answer FALSE means the statement goes directly against what the text says.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because Western biologists were not impressed; instead, they doubted the idea. Paragraph F states that although Swingle was impressed, "many Western biologists were skeptical" and considered the concept of using insects to fight other pests "new and highly controversial." Because the passage clearly shows doubt and disagreement rather than being impressed, the statement is false.
Q22 TRUE As costs soared and pests began to develop resistance to the chemicals, growers began to revive the old ant patrols Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that as the prices of chemical sprays increased rapidly and bugs stopped being killed by them, the farmers started using the ants again.
Answer Explanation:
The answer TRUE means the passage clearly states that Chinese farmers saw the price of chemical sprays (pesticides) become very high.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph G discusses the fruit growers who switched to chemical insecticides. It states that these growers became unhappy because "costs soared" (meaning the prices rose very high / became expensive) and pests became resistant to the chemicals. Therefore, the statement accurately matches the facts in the reading passage.
Q23 TRUE As costs soared and pests began to develop resistance to the chemicals, growers began to revive the old ant patrols Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that when chemicals became too expensive and pests stopped being affected by them, fruit growers started using ants again instead of relying on chemical sprays.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means the passage agrees that some Chinese fruit growers stopped using chemical pesticides and returned to using ants.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph G mentions that after switching to chemicals, farmers faced rising costs and pests that resisted the chemicals. Consequently, "growers began to revive the old ant patrols," showing they began abandoning chemical insecticides in favor of biological pest control.
Q24 TRUE Trees with yellow ants produced almost 20 per cent more healthy leaves than those without Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that fruit trees protected by yellow ants grew nearly 20% more healthy leaves than trees that did not have ants on them.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means the passage confirms that trees lacking ants had more damaged or unhealthy leaves compared to trees that had ants.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph G states that trees with yellow ants grew almost 20 percent more healthy leaves than trees without them. This directly implies that trees without ants ended up with more unhealthy leaves than those with ants.
Q25 FALSE More recent trials have shown that these trees yield just as big a crop as those protected by expensive chemical sprays Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that recent tests show trees using ants produce the exact same amount of fruit as trees treated with chemical pesticides.
Answer Explanation:
The answer FALSE means that the statement directly contradicts what is written in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the passage states that trees protected by ants produce "just as big a crop" as trees treated with chemical sprays, meaning the harvest size is equal, not larger. The statement claims that the yield using ants is "larger", which contradicts the information in the text.
Q26 NOT GIVEN The West did not discover the Chinese orange growers' secret weapon until the early 20th century Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Western countries did not learn about Chinese farmers using ants until the 1900s, but it says nothing about whether Chinese farmers thought this method would or would not work outside China.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the passage does not provide any information about whether Chinese orange growers claimed or suggested that using ants for pest protection would fail outside of China.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because although the text mentions Chinese farmers using ants and Western scientists learning about or being skeptical of this method, it never states that Chinese farmers believed or proposed that ant protection would not work in other countries.
