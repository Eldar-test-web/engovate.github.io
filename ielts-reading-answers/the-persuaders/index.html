The Persuaders - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

From IELTS Past Papers Academic Reading Test 14 · Part 3 · Questions 27–40

Reading Passage

Read the text below and answer questions 27-40.

The Persuaders

A We have long lived in an age where powerful images, catchy soundbites and too-good-to miss offers bombard from every quarter. All around us the persuaders are at work. Occasionally their methods are unsubtle—the planting of a kiss on a baby’s head by a wannabe political leader, or a liquidation sale in a shop that has been “closing down” for well over a year, but generally the persuaders know what they are about and are highly capable. Be they politicians, supermarket chains, salespeople or advertisers, they know exactly what to do to sell us their images, ideas or produce. When it comes to persuasion, these giants rule supreme. They employ the most skilled image-makers and use the best psychological tricks to guarantee that even the most cautious among us are open to manipulation.

B We spend more time in them than we mean to, we buy 75 percent of our food from them and end up with products that we did not realize we wanted. Right from the start, supermarkets have been ahead of the game. For example, when Sainsbury introduced shopping baskets into its 1950s stores, it was a stroke of marketing genius. Now shoppers could browse and pick up items they previously would have ignored. Soon after came trolleys, and just as new roads attract more traffic, the same applied to trolley space. Prof Merlin Stone, IBM Professor of Relationship Marketing at Bristol Business School, says aisles are laid out to maximize profits. Stores pander to our money-rich, time-poor lifestyle. Low turnover products such as clothes and electrical goods are stocked at the back while high turnover items command position at the front.

C Stone believes supermarkets work hard to “stall” us because the more time we spend in them, the more we buy. Thus, great efforts are made to make the environment pleasant. Stores play music to relax us and some even pipe air from the in-store bakery around the shop. In the USA, fake aromas are sometimes used. Smell is both the most evocative and subliminal sense. In experiments, pleasant smells are effective in increasing our spending. A casino that fragranced only half its premises saw profit soar in the aroma—filled areas. The other success story from the supermarkets’ perspective is the loyalty card. Punters may assume that they are being rewarded for their fidelity, but all the while they are trading information about their shopping habits. Loyal shoppers could be paying 30% more by sticking to their favourite shops for essential cosmetics

D Research has shown that 75 percent of profit comes from just 30 percent of customers. Ultimately, reward cards could be used to identify and better accommodate these “elite” shoppers. It could also be used to make adverts more relevant to individual consumers—rather like Spielberg’s futuristic thriller Minority Report, in which Tom Cruise’s character is bombarded with interactive personalized ads. If this sounds far-fetched, the data-gathering revolution has already seen the introduction of radio—frequency identification—a way to electronically tag products to see who is buying what, RFID means they can follow the product into people's homes.

E No matter how savvy we think we are to their ploys, the ad industry still wins. Adverts focus on what products do or on how they make us feel. Researcher Laurette Dube, in the Journal of Advertising Research, says when attitudes are based on “cognitive foundations” (logical reasoning), advertisers use informative appeals. This works for products with little emotional draw but high functionality, such as bleach. Where attitudes are based on affect (i.e, emotions), ad teams try to tap into our feelings. Researchers at the University of Florida recently concluded that our emotional responses to adverts dominate over “cognition”.

F Advertisers play on our need to be safe (commercials for insurance), to belong (make customers feel they are in the group in fashion ads) and for self-esteem (aspirational adverts). With time and space at a premium, celebrities are often used as a quick way of meeting these needs—either because the celeb epitomizes success or because they seem familiar and so make the product seem “safe”. A survey of 4,000 campaigns found ads with celebs were 10 percent more effective than without. Humor also stimulates a rapid emotional response. Hwiman Chung, writing in the International Journal of Advertising, found that funny ads were remembered for longer than straight ones. Combine humor with sexual imagery—as in Wonderbra’s “Hello Boys” ads—and you are on to a winner.

G Slice-of-life ads are another tried and tested method—they paint a picture of life as you would like it, but still one that feels familiar. Abhilasha Mehta, in the Journal of Advertising Research, noted that the more one’s self-image tallies with the brand being advertised, the stronger the commercial. Ad makers also use behaviorist theories, recognizing that the more sensation we receive from an object, the better we know it. If an advert for a chocolate bar fails to cause salivation, it has probably failed. No wonder advertisements have been dubbed the “nervous system of the business world”.

H Probably all of us could make a sale if the product was something we truly believed in, but professional salespeople are in a different league. The best of them can always sell different items to suitable customers in a best time. They do this by using very basic psychological techniques. Stripped to its simplest level, selling works by heightening the buyer’s perception of how much they need a product or service. Buyers normally have certain requirements by which they will judge the suitability of a product. The seller therefore attempts to tease out what these conditions are and then explains how their products’ benefits can meet these requirements.

I Richard Hession, author of Be a Great Salesperson says it is human nature to prefer to speak rather than to listen, and good salespeople pander to this. They ask punters about their needs and offer to work with them to achieve their objectives. As a result, the buyer feels they are receiving a “consultation” rather than a sales pitch. All the while, the salesperson presents with a demeanour that takes it for granted that the sale will be made. Never will the words “if you buy” be used, but rather “when you buy”.

J Dr Rob Yeung, a senior consultant at business psychologists Kiddy and Partner, says most salespeople will build up a level of rapport by asking questions about hobbies, family and lifestyle. This has the double benefit of making the salesperson likeable while furnishing him or her with more information about the client’s wants. Yeung says effective salespeople try as far as possible to match their style of presenting themselves to how the buyer comes across. If the buyer cracks jokes, the salespeople will respond in kind. If the buyer wants detail, the seller provides it, if they are more interested in the feel of the product, the seller will focus on this. At its most extreme, appearing empathetic can even include the salesperson attempting to “mirror” the body language of the buyer.

K Whatever the method used, all salespeople work towards one aim: “closing the deal”. In fact, they will be looking for “closing signals” through their dealings with potential clients. Once again the process works by assuming success. The buyer is not asked “are you interested?” as this can invite a negative response. Instead the seller takes it for granted that the deal is effectively done: when the salesman asks you for a convenient delivery date or asks what color you want, you will probably respond accordingly. Only afterwards might you wonder why you proved such a pushover.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q27 B For example, when Sainsbury introduced shopping baskets into its 1950s stores, it was a stroke of marketing genius. Now shoppers could browse and pick up items they previously would have ignored Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that providing baskets allowed customers to walk around browsing and collecting extra products that they otherwise would have ignored.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "B" means that supermarkets introduced shopping baskets to encourage people to look around and spend more time buying things.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Paragraph B explains that when baskets were introduced, it allowed customers to "browse" (look around while spending time) and select things they would normally not have taken. Combined with the opening line of the paragraph mentioning that people spend more time in supermarkets than intended, giving customers baskets encourages them to browse and stay longer while shopping.
Q28 A the best of them can always sell different items to suitable customers in a best time Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the top salespeople have the special skill of always selling the appropriate goods to the matching buyers at the best possible time.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "Sell the right product to right person" means that a top salesperson is able to offer and sell the correct item to the customer who needs or wants it.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph H directly describes the special ability of top salespeople. The passage states that "the best of them can always sell different items to suitable customers in a best time." Here, selling "different items to suitable customers" directly matches the idea of selling the right product to the right person.
Q29 D Richard Hession, author of Be a Great Salesperson says it is human nature to prefer to speak rather than to listen, and good salespeople pander to this Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that people prefer talking instead of listening, and skilled salespeople know how to please and work with people using this knowledge.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that good salespeople need to have very strong communication and relationship-building skills with people.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph I. Richard Hession explains that people naturally like talking more than listening. Good salespeople use this human nature by listening carefully, asking customers about their needs, and making them feel like they are getting helpful advice rather than a regular sales speech. This demonstrates showing high-level communication and interpersonal skills.
Q30 C Stone believes supermarkets work hard to “stall” us because the more time we spend in them, the more we buy. Thus, great efforts are made to make the environment pleasant. Stores play music to relax us and some even pipe air from the in-store bakery around the shop. In the USA, fake aromas are sometimes used Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that supermarkets try to slow down shoppers by making the store environment nice, playing relaxing music, and using nice smells from the bakery or artificial scents.
Answer Explanation:
The answer identifies paragraph C as the section describing how supermarkets distract customers and slow them down.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because paragraph C details the specific methods supermarkets use to "stall" or delay shoppers so that they spend more time inside. These techniques include playing relaxing music and filling the air with pleasant bakery smells or artificial aromas to influence and distract consumers into staying longer and buying more.
Q31 J Dr Rob Yeung, a senior consultant at business psychologists Kiddy and Partner, says most salespeople will build up a level of rapport by asking questions about hobbies, family and lifestyle Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that salespeople create a good personal connection and relationship with a buyer by asking friendly questions about the buyer's life, family, and interests.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "J" means that paragraph J explains the ways a seller creates a friendly, close connection with a customer.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is J because paragraph J describes how salespeople create a connection with customers. It explains that sellers "build up a level of rapport" (which means building a close and friendly relationship) by asking about personal topics like hobbies, family, and lifestyle, as well as matching their communication style to the buyer.
Q32 F Humor also stimulates a rapid emotional response. Hwiman Chung, writing in the International Journal of Advertising, found that funny ads were remembered for longer than straight ones Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that using humor triggers feelings quickly and helps people remember funny commercials for a longer period of time.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "F" means that paragraph F discusses how people are strongly affected by and remember funny advertisements.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because this paragraph specifically talks about the effect of humor in advertising. It states that "humor" creates a quick emotional reaction and that "funny ads" leave a stronger impression because they are "remembered for longer" compared to serious or plain advertisements.
Q33 K Whatever the method used, all salespeople work towards one aim: “closing the deal”. In fact, they will be looking for “closing signals” through their dealings with potential clients. Once again the process works by assuming success. The buyer is not asked “are you interested?” as this can invite a negative response. Instead the seller takes it for granted that the deal is effectively done: when the salesman asks you for a convenient delivery date or asks what color you want, you will probably respond accordingly Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that salespeople aim to finish the sale by assuming the customer has already decided to buy, asking practical questions like delivery dates or color choices rather than giving the buyer a chance to say no.
Answer Explanation:
The answer identifies Paragraph K as the section that discusses how salespeople successfully complete or secure a sale.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is K because Paragraph K talks directly about the goal of "closing the deal" (which means getting the order) and describes the specific methods used to achieve it. It explains that sellers look for "closing signals" and use the technique of "assuming success" by asking for details like a convenient delivery date or preferred color instead of asking if the customer is interested.
Q34 K The buyer is not asked “are you interested?” as this can invite a negative response. Instead the seller takes it for granted that the deal is effectively done: when the salesman asks you for a convenient delivery date or asks what color you want, you will probably respond accordingly Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that salespeople avoid asking if you want to buy because you might say no. Instead, they ask questions about delivery dates or color choices, making you feel the deal is already finished so you agree more easily.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "K" means that Paragraph K describes how salespeople use specific types of questions to help make a sale.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is K because it explains the way salespeople use questions to lead customers toward buying. Instead of asking questions that might get a "no" (such as "are you interested?"), salespeople ask questions that assume the sale is already agreed on (such as asking for a preferred delivery date or color), which guides the customer to complete the purchase.
Q35 D Research has shown that 75 percent of profit comes from just 30 percent of customers Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that most of a store's profit (75 percent) is made from only a small portion of its shoppers (30 percent).
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" means that paragraph D discusses how various groups of shoppers generate different amounts of money or earnings for businesses.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because paragraph D states that a small group of customers (30 percent) produces the majority of the profit (75 percent). This shows that different customer groups bring different profits to stores.
Q36 aisles Prof Merlin Stone, IBM Professor of Relationship Marketing at Bristol Business School, says aisles are laid out to maximize profits Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that an expert named Merlin Stone states that the walkways between store shelves (aisles) are designed and arranged specifically to make as much profit as possible.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to passageways between the shelves, which supermarkets arrange carefully to make the highest amount of money.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph B. The text states that Merlin Stone explains that "aisles are laid out to maximize profits." In the summary notes, "generate the most profits" directly matches the phrase "maximize profits" in the passage.
Q37 experiments In experiments, pleasant smells are effective in increasing our spending Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that scientific tests showed that nice fragrances cause customers to spend more money.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "experiments" refers to scientific tests or studies carried out to discover or prove something.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "experiments" because the passage mentions scientific tests where nice scents caused people to spend more money. In Paragraph C, the text states that "In experiments, pleasant smells are effective in increasing our spending." Here, the phrase "satisfying aromas" matches "pleasant smells", and "motivate people buy more products" corresponds to "increasing our spending". Therefore, "experiments" is the exact word from the text that completes the note.
Q38 loyalty card The other success story from the supermarkets’ perspective is the loyalty card. Punters may assume that they are being rewarded for their fidelity, but all the while they are trading information about their shopping habits Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that supermarkets introduce loyalty cards, which shoppers believe are meant to reward them for continuing to shop at the same store.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means a special store card given to reward frequent, regular shoppers.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "loyalty card" because Paragraph C discusses supermarket strategies beyond making a comfortable environment. It mentions the "loyalty card" as another major success story, where customers ("punters") believe they are receiving rewards for their "fidelity" (faithfulness or regular patronage).
Q39 cosmetics Loyal shoppers could be paying 30% more by sticking to their favourite shops for essential cosmetics Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that customers who remain loyal to their favorite stores may pay 30% more money when buying essential beauty items (cosmetics).
Answer Explanation:
The answer "cosmetics" refers to beauty and makeup products such as skin creams, powders, or lotions.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph C. The passage discusses customer loyalty and notes that loyal shoppers can end up paying 30% more when they keep going to their favorite stores to purchase necessary beauty items. In the text, "essential cosmetics" matches the phrase "everyday necessary cosmetics" in the summary sentence.
Q40 group Advertisers play on our need to be safe (commercials for insurance), to belong (make customers feel they are in the group in fashion ads) and for self-esteem (aspirational adverts) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that fashion commercials appeal to our desire to fit in by making shoppers feel like they are part of a group.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "group" refers to a set of people who are connected or belong together.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "group" because paragraph F discusses how fashion advertisements appeal to a person's desire to belong by making them feel like they are part of a group. In the text, "fashion ads" matches "Clothes shops use advertisements", and "to belong (make customer feel they are in the group...)" matches "make buyer think they are belonging to part of a [group]".
