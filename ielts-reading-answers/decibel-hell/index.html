Decibel Hell - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

From IELTS Mock Tests Academic Reading Test 17 · Part 1 · Questions 1–13

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 1-13 which are based on Reading Passage 1 below.

decibel Hell

Section A decibel Hell:

It's not difficult for a person to encounter sound at levels that can cause adverse health effects. During a single day, people living in a typical urban environment can experience a wide range of sounds in many locations, even once-quiet locales have become polluted with noise. In fact, it's difficult today to escape sound completely. In its 1999 Guidelines for Community Noise, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared, “Worldwide, noise-induced hearing impairment is the most prevalent irreversible occupational hazard, and it is estimated that 120 million people worldwide have disabling hearing difficulties.” Growing evidence also points to many other health effects of too much volume.

Mark Stephenson, a Cincinnati, Ohio-based senior research audiologist at the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), says his agency's definition of hazardous noise is sound that exceeds the time-weighted average of 85 dBA, meaning the average noise exposure measured over a typical eight-hour workday. Other measures and definitions are used for other purposes.

Section B Growing Volume

In the United States, about 30 million workers are exposed to hazardous sound levels on the job, according to NIOSH. Industries having a high number of workers exposed to loud sounds include construction, agriculture, mining, manufacturing, utilities, transportation, and the military.

Noise in U.S. industry is an extremely difficult problem to monitor, acknowledges Craig Moulton, a senior industrial hygienist for the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA). “Still,” he says, "OSHA does require that any employer with workers overexposed to noise provide protection for those employees against the harmful effects of noise. Additionally, employers must implement a continuing, effective hearing conservation program as outlined in OSHA's Noise Standard."

Section C Scary Sound Effects

Numerous scientific studies over the years have confirmed that exposure to certain levels of sound can damage hearing. Prolonged exposure can actually change the structure of the hair cells in the inner ear, resulting in hearing loss. It can also cause tinnitus, a ringing, roaring, buzzing, or clicking in the ears.

NIOSH studies from the mid to late 1990s show that 90% of coal miners have hearing impairment by age 52 – compared to 9% of the general population – and 70% of male metal/nonmetal miners will experience hearing impairment by age 60 (Stephenson notes that from adolescence onward, females tend to have better hearing than males). Rick Neitzel of the National Hearing Conservation Association says nearly half of all construction workers have some degree of hearing loss. “NIOSH research also reveals that by age twenty-five, the average carpenter's hearing is equivalent to an otherwise healthy fifty-year-old male who hasn't been exposed to noise,” he says.

William Luxford, medical director of the House Ear Clinic of St. Vincent Medical Center in Los Angeles, points out one piece of good news: "It's true that continuous noise exposure will lead to the continuation of hearing loss, but as soon as the exposure is stopped, the hearing loss stops. So a change in environment can improve a person's hearing health."

Research is catching up with this anecdotal evidence. In the July 2001 issue of Pediatrics, researchers from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported that, based on audiometric testing of 5,249 children as part of the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, an estimated 12.5% of American children have noise-induced hearing threshold shifts or dulled hearing – in one or both ears. Most children with noise-induced hearing threshold shifts have only limited hearing damage, but continued exposure to excessive noise can lead to difficulties with high-frequency sound discrimination. The report listed stereos, music concerts, toys (such as toy telephones and certain rattles), lawnmowers, and fireworks as producing potentially harmful sounds.

Section D Beyond the Ears

The effects of sound don't stop with the ears. Nonauditory effects of noise exposure are those effects that don't cause hearing loss but still can be measured, such as elevated blood pressure, loss of sleep, increased heart rate, cardiovascular constriction, labored breathing, and changes in brain chemistry.

The nonauditory effects of noise were noted as early as 1930 in a study published by E.L. Smith and D.L. Laird in volume 2 of the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America. The results showed that exposure to noise caused stomach contractions in healthy human beings. Reports on noise's nonauditory effects published since that pioneering study have been both contradictory and controversial in some areas.

In 1975 the psychologist Arline Bronzaft took part in a study of a school beside an elevated train line. Pupils in classrooms facing the tracks did worse on reading tests than those at the quieter back of the building, and by sixth grade they had fallen a full grade behind. Bronzaft and the school principal persuaded the school board to have acoustical tile installed in the classrooms adjacent to the tracks. The Transit Authority also treated the tracks near the school to make them less noisy. A follow-up study published in the September 1981 issue of the Journal of Environmental Psychology found that children's reading scores improved after these interventions were put in place.

Section E Fighting for Quiet

Anti-noise activists say that Europe and several countries in Asia are more advanced than the United States in terms of combating noise. "Population pressure has prompted Europe to move more quickly on the noise issue than the United States has,” says Ken Hume, a physiology lecturer at Manchester Metropolitan University. In the European Union, countries with cities of at least 250,000 people are creating noise maps of those cities to help leaders determine noise pollution policies. Paris has already prepared its first noise maps. The map data, which must be finished by 2007, will be fed into computer models that will help test the sound impact of street designs or new buildings before construction begins.

Activists in other countries say they too want the United States to play a more leading role on the noise issue. But as in other areas of environmental health, merely having a more powerful government agency in place that can set more regulations is not the ultimate answer, according to other experts. Bronzaft stresses that governments worldwide need to increase funding for noise research and do a better job coordinating their noise pollution efforts so they can establish health and environmental policies based on solid scientific research. “Governments have a responsibility to protect their citizens by curbing noise pollution," she says.

Q1 85 dBa Mark Stephenson, a Cincinnati, Ohio-based senior research audiologist at the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), says his agency's definition of hazardous noise is sound that exceeds the time-weighted average of 85 dBA, meaning the average noise exposure measured over a typical eight-hour workday Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that according to the safety agency, dangerous noise is any sound that goes above an average level of 85 dBA during a normal workday.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means a sound measurement level of 85 decibels (85 dBA), which is considered dangerous noise.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Section A of the text. The summary refers to what dangerous noise is according to an agency's definition ("referring to the agency's definition"). In the passage, Mark Stephenson states that his agency defines hazardous noise as sound that "exceeds the time-weighted average of 85 dBA". Here, "beyond the average of" matches "exceeds the time-weighted average of". Therefore, the exact noise level needed to fill in the blank is "85 dBA".
Q2 hearing impairment / hearing NIOSH studies from the mid to late 1990s show that 90% of coal miners have hearing impairment by age 52 – compared to 9% of the general population – and 70% of male metal/nonmetal miners will experience hearing impairment by age 60 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that research done between the mid-1990s and late 1990s showed that most mine workers lose some of their hearing by the time they reach age 52 or 60.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means problems with hearing or losing the ability to hear sounds clearly.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Section C of the passage. The summary describes studies from the mid to late 1990s showing sound-related problems as people reach older ages. The passage mentions that "NIOSH studies from the mid to late 1990s" found that workers (such as coal miners and metal miners) suffer from "hearing impairment" by older ages like 52 or 60. Therefore, "hearing" or "hearing impairment" correctly completes the sentence.
Q3 high-frequency Most children with noise-induced hearing threshold shifts have only limited hearing damage, but continued exposure to excessive noise can lead to difficulties with high-frequency sound discrimination Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that if children keep hearing very loud sounds, it can become hard for them to tell apart sounds that have a high pitch.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "high-frequency" refers to sounds that have a high pitch, like a whistle or high notes in music.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the study of 5,249 children mentioned in Section C. The summary describes children who are constantly exposed to loud noise having trouble with sound discrimination. In the text, "continued exposure to excessive noise" corresponds directly to "constantly exposed to excessive noise", and "can lead to difficulties with" matches "may have trouble in". The text states that these difficulties are specifically with "high-frequency" sound discrimination.
Q4 stomach contractions / stomach The results showed that exposure to noise caused stomach contractions in healthy human beings Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that when healthy people were around loud noise, it made their stomach muscles squeeze and tighten.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "stomach (contractions)" refers to the tightening or cramping movements of the belly or stomach.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Section D of the passage. The summary describes non-ear-related problems caused by loud sounds, noting that "exposure to noise may lead to the unease of [blank] in healthy people." The passage states that an early study showed "exposure to noise caused stomach contractions in healthy human beings." Here, "healthy human beings" matches "healthy people," and "caused stomach contractions" matches "lead to the unease of stomach (contractions)."
Q5 noise maps In the European Union, countries with cities of at least 250,000 people are creating noise maps of those cities to help leaders determine noise pollution policies Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that in the European Union, countries with big cities (at least 250,000 people) are making noise maps. These maps help leaders decide noise pollution policies.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says that big European cities are creating "noise maps" to help make noise pollution policies.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "noise maps" because the summary says "cities of over 250,000 people are creating ___ to help to create noise pollution policies." This matches the passage: "cities of at least 250,000 people are creating noise maps... to help leaders determine noise pollution policies." Pay attention to the keywords "250,000 people," "creating," and "noise pollution policies." It is also two words, so it fits the limit.
Q6 B William Luxford, medical director of the House Ear Clinic of St. Vincent Medical Center in Los Angeles, points out one piece of good news: "It's true that continuous noise exposure will lead to the continuation of hearing loss, but as soon as the exposure is stopped, the hearing loss stops. So a change in environment can improve a person's hearing health." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that William Luxford shares good news: if a person leaves a loud place and stops hearing loud noises, their hearing loss stops getting worse, meaning changing surroundings helps protect hearing health.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "B" means that William Luxford is the person who stated that people can improve their ear health by moving to a quieter setting.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Section C directly quotes William Luxford discussing how stopping noise exposure stops hearing damage. He concludes with the key idea: "So a change in environment can improve a person's hearing health." This matches the statement in the question perfectly.
Q7 D Bronzaft stresses that governments worldwide need to increase funding for noise research and do a better job coordinating their noise pollution efforts so they can establish health and environmental policies based on solid scientific research Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Bronzaft strongly emphasizes that governments across the world should give more financial support to study noise and work together better to create rules based on science.
Answer Explanation:
The answer choice "D" means that Arline Bronzaft is the person who said governments must provide money to support research about noise.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Section E states that Bronzaft believes governments around the world need to give more money ("increase funding") for study into noise ("noise research"). This idea matches the statement that the government should support research against noise with funds.
Q8 C “Still,” he says, "OSHA does require that any employer with workers overexposed to noise provide protection for those employees against the harmful effects of noise Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Craig Moulton stating that OSHA rules force bosses and business owners to protect workers who are around too much harmful noise.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "C" refers to Craig Moulton, a senior industrial hygienist working for the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA).

The correct answer is C because Craig Moulton explains that OSHA requires employers to protect their workers from dangerous noise levels. In the question, "companies" corresponds to "employer[s]", "protect the employees" matches "provide protection for those employees", and "avoid noise" refers to protecting them "against the harmful effects of noise."
Q9 E In the July 2001 issue of Pediatrics, researchers from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported that, based on audiometric testing of 5,249 children as part of the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, an estimated 12.5% of American children have noise-induced hearing threshold shifts or dulled hearing – in one or both ears Excerpt/Passage Explanation:

The correct answer is E because the text states that researchers from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention found that about 12.5% of American children suffer from hearing damage caused by noise. In this context, "noise-induced hearing threshold shifts or dulled hearing" directly corresponds to the statement that noise has affected "children's hearing ability".
Q10 A In its 1999 Guidelines for Community Noise, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared, “Worldwide, noise-induced hearing impairment is the most prevalent irreversible occupational hazard, and it is estimated that 120 million people worldwide have disabling hearing difficulties.” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the World Health Organization stated that loud noise hurts people's ears permanently across the globe, leaving around 120 million people with severe hearing trouble.

The text states that according to the WHO, noise-induced hearing damage happens worldwide and affects an estimated 120 million people with disabling hearing problems. Keywords like "worldwide" match directly between the question and the statement by the WHO.
Q11 C Bronzaft and the school principal persuaded the school board to have acoustical tile installed in the classrooms adjacent to the tracks Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that researchers convinced school officials to put sound-absorbing tiles into the classrooms next to the train tracks to lower the noise level.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the school leaders agreed to add sound-dampening materials into the classrooms to reduce loud noise from outside.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Section D mentions that Bronzaft and the principal "persuaded the school board to have acoustical tile installed in the classrooms adjacent to the tracks." Here, the word "persuaded" matches the question's word "convinced," and "acoustical tile" refers to the "special material" used to reduce noise from the nearby tracks.
Q12 D In the European Union, countries with cities of at least 250,000 people are creating noise maps of those cities to help leaders determine noise pollution policies Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that European cities with 250,000 or more people are studying noise levels on maps so that government leaders can decide on the best rules to control noise pollution.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that research on noise in large European cities is done to help city leaders make better rules and choices when managing the city.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because Section E explains that cities with at least 250,000 people make noise maps to assist decision-makers. In the text, "help leaders determine noise pollution policies" and testing the impact of "street designs or new buildings" directly matches "helping the authorities better make a decision on management of the city." Here, "leaders" corresponds to "authorities," and creating policies and planning construction relates to city management.

Answer Explanation:
The answer C means that the most suitable title for the first section is "The Effects of Living in a Noisy World".
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Section A focuses on how modern city environments are filled with inescapable noise and describes the negative health consequences this causes. The passage specifically mentions "people living in a typical urban environment" (which matches "Living in a Noisy World") and discusses "adverse health effects", "hearing impairment", and "other health effects of too much volume" (which matches "The Effects"). The other options are incorrect because this paragraph does not discuss children learning, high-tech fighting against noise, or coping strategies.
