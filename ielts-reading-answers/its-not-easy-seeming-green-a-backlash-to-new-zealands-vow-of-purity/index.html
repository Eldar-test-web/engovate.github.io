Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 15-28, which are based on Reading Passage 2 on the following pages.

IT'S NOT EASY SEEMING GREEN A backlash to New Zealand's vow of purity

A Fans combing internet sites are not the only people eagerly anticipating a pair of epic fantasy movies based on "The Hobbit", by J.R.R. Tolkien, that it is planned will start filming this year. New Zealand's tourist industry, too, is eager to see the islands' sweeping and unsullied vistas revealed once more to millions of cinemagoers, as they were almost a decade ago when the first of the three films based on Tolkien's "The Lord of The Rings" was released. Those films did a great deal to boost the country's tourism trade (Air New Zealand promoted itself as the "airline to Middle Earth"), fitting nicely with the country's "100% Pure New Zealand" marketing slogan, first used a couple of years earlier.

B But how much of this is, indeed, a fantasy? Last November, in his "Greenwash" column for the Guardian, a British newspaper, environmental journalist Fred Pearce pointed out that New Zealand's greenhouse-gas emissions had risen 22% since 1990 (its commitment under the Kyoto Protocol was to keep them level) and were now 60% greater per head than Britain's. The image New Zealand attempted to show the world amounted to a "shameless two fingers to the global community" in the face of a far dirtier reality, including the world's third-highest rate of car ownership, and methane-belching cows that help to push agricultural emissions to almost half the country's total.

C When tackled on these claims by an Australian reporter, New Zealand's normally amiable prime minister John Key angrily dismissed them as "bollocks", pointing to his country's efforts to tackle its emissions by energetically planting trees that would re-absorb them. But local papers took up the theme. "New Zealand: 100 per cent pure hype" trumpeted the New Zealand Herald. "We are clean and green, but only relatively speaking and by accident rather than conscious effort." The ruggedness of much of New Zealand's terrain may have protected its film-friendly uplands, but at lower elevations farming has stripped away forests, eroded hills and clogged rivers with silt and fertiliser run-off.

D On previous occasions New Zealand has been able to see off threats to its green image. When concerns about "food miles" started to be widely voiced a few years ago, New Zealand's agricultural exports – 40% of its total exports – looked at risk. Few foodstuffs other than those on the space station rack up more miles than a leg of New Zealand lamb on a British dinner table. The government latched on to research by New Zealand's Lincoln University which showed that the country's agricultural exports were relatively efficient in terms of energy use, by comparison to agricultural production in Europe, even when shipping was taken into account. Europe's concerns, it was implied, had more to do with fear of competition from unsubsidised antipodeans than concern about environmental degradation.

E New Zealand has also followed some policies designed to match reality to green rhetoric. In 2007 an "Environmental Performance Review of New Zealand" by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) noted a bunch of improvements over the past ten years: better approaches to recycling and water treatment, reforestation of pasture to prevent erosion, strengthened efforts to preserve endangered species, the removal of agricultural and fishing subsidies, and by OECD standards, a high use of renewable energy sources, at 30% of supply. However, the report also pointed to new environmental pressures, such as growing demand for electricity that was leading to greater use of fossil fuels for power generation, and climbing car ownership unhindered by measures such as road pricing.

F Mr Key's centre-right government, elected in 2008, although less green-in-spirit than the previous Labour-led coalition, has taken some environmental action. Just before last year's Copenhagen summit, it enacted an emissions-trading scheme just as an Australian attempt to do the same was foundering. At Copenhagen, Tim Groser, the minister for climate-change negotiations and a pragmatic former trade diplomat, pushed strongly for a "global alliance" to research ways of reducing agricultural greenhouse-gas emissions.

G From an environmentalist's perspective, though, these positives are outweighed by much larger negatives. The emissions-trading scheme excludes agricultural emissions until 2015, and its generous allocations of free carbon credits to business have been lambasted by environmentalists. The country's transport strategy favours road-building over already-scant public transport, and there is much talk of the need to ease resource-management rules that act as barriers to business. In February, the government revealed it was considering opening some of the country's pristine public land up for mining – an activity to which the dwarves in "The Hobbit" are much given, but which is not popular with more elvish sensibilities. Energetic lobbying by environmental groups forced it to scale back the amount of land under consideration, but on March 22nd it announced that it still intended to open 7,000 hectares of conservation land to mining, with other conservation areas to be surveyed for their mineral potential.

H Even the government's boasts of reforestation to replace forests cleared for dairy farming are less green than they might appear. As the Guardian reported, these forests are not long-term sinks for carbon, but plantations, which will when harvested return carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. The Sustainability Council of New Zealand, an independent body, has also criticised this approach as playing games with Kyoto targets.

I In many ways, the dilemma New Zealand faces is not different to that of other rich countries – how to balance economic growth with the need to address environmental degradation. But it is particularly acute in a country so dependent on the export of commodities and landscape-driven tourism. The difference between New Zealand and other places is that New Zealand has actively sold itself as "100% Pure". Now that New Zealanders themselves are acknowledging the gap between the claim and reality, and the risk to their reputation this poses, it is time for the country to find itself a more sustainable brand, and soon.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q15 iii Those films did a great deal to boost the country's tourism trade (Air New Zealand promoted itself as the "airline to Middle Earth"), fitting nicely with the country's "100% Pure New Zealand" marketing slogan, first used a couple of years earlier Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that popular fantasy films helped grow the country's travel business because they matched the famous advertisement phrase "100% Pure New Zealand" that pictures the country as clean and natural.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'iii' means the main idea of Paragraph A is about how New Zealand's travel and tourism business promotes an image of clean, beautiful, and pure nature.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading iii because Paragraph A focuses on how New Zealand's travel sector relies on clean and untouched landscapes to attract visitors. The paragraph highlights that movies like "The Lord of the Rings" helped "boost the country's tourism trade" by showing "unsullied vistas" (clean and pure views). It also mentions that this clean image aligned perfectly with the tourism campaign slogan "100% Pure New Zealand". Therefore, "tourist industry" directly matches "tourism trade", and "green image" matches the "unsullied vistas" and the "100% Pure New Zealand" brand.
Q16 viii The image New Zealand attempted to show the world amounted to a "shameless two fingers to the global community" in the face of a far dirtier reality, including the world's third-highest rate of car ownership, and methane-belching cows that help to push agricultural emissions to almost half the country's total Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that although New Zealand tries to present itself as clean, the real situation is much dirtier and embarrassing because of very high car ownership and heavy emissions from farming.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the true environmental situation is dirty and embarrassing, which goes against the clean and pure image the country promotes.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading viii because Paragraph B focuses on exposing the embarrassing truth behind New Zealand's pure and green marketing image. The author cites a journalist who reveals that emissions have increased significantly, highlighting a "shameless" attitude compared to its "far dirtier reality" of high car ownership and agricultural pollution.
Q17 i When tackled on these claims by an Australian reporter, New Zealand's normally amiable prime minister John Key angrily dismissed them as "bollocks", pointing to his country's efforts to tackle its emissions by energetically planting trees that would re-absorb them. But local papers took up the theme. "New Zealand: 100 per cent pure hype" trumpeted the New Zealand Herald Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows that the prime minister (the government) strongly denied the criticisms, whereas local newspapers (the media) agreed with them and called the pure green image exaggerated.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'i' means that the government and the news media reacted in completely different, opposing ways.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'i' because Paragraph C focuses on two contrasting reactions to the environmental criticisms. On one hand, the government—represented by Prime Minister John Key—rejected the criticism angrily and defended the country's actions. On the other hand, the media—represented by local newspapers like the 'New Zealand Herald'—agreed with the criticism and highlighted that the green image was just 'pure hype'. Therefore, heading 'i' accurately matches the paragraph.
Q18 v The government latched on to research by New Zealand's Lincoln University which showed that the country's agricultural exports were relatively efficient in terms of energy use, by comparison to agricultural production in Europe, even when shipping was taken into account Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that university research showed New Zealand's farming exports were actually very energy-efficient when compared to European food production, even when the energy needed to ship the food is included.
Answer Explanation:
The answer chooses heading "v", which means that Paragraph D discusses proof that farming in New Zealand uses energy in an efficient way.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "v" because Paragraph D describes how New Zealand defended its farm exports against concerns over "food miles." To do this, the government used university research ("research by New Zealand's Lincoln University") as clear proof ("evidence") showing that its farming products used less energy ("relatively efficient in terms of energy use") compared to farming in Europe, even after shipping.
Q19 ii New Zealand has also followed some policies designed to match reality to green rhetoric Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that New Zealand introduced specific government policies to try to live up to its clean, green claims.
Answer Explanation:
The answer matches Paragraph E with heading ii, which means rules and actions created to look or be friendly to the environment.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is heading ii because Paragraph E discusses the rules and steps New Zealand took to support its green image. The paragraph begins by directly stating that the country followed policies to make its real actions match its "green rhetoric" (words claiming to be clean and eco-friendly). It then lists environmental actions and improvements such as recycling, water treatment, planting trees, protecting endangered animals, and using renewable energy.
Q20 millions of cinemagoers / cinemagoers New Zealand's tourist industry, too, is eager to see the islands' sweeping and unsullied vistas revealed once more to millions of cinemagoers, as they were almost a decade ago when the first of the three films based on Tolkien's "The Lord of The Rings" was released Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says New Zealand's tourism business wants to show its wide, clean views to millions of movie viewers again, like it did about ten years ago with the first 'Lord of the Rings' film.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the clean New Zealand was shown to the people who watch movies in cinemas, in the millions.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'cinemagoers' because the question asks 'to who' the films showed the clean land. The passage says the 'unsullied vistas' (clean views) were 'revealed... to millions of cinemagoers' when the first film came out. 'Unsullied' means the same as 'clean'.
Q21 60% / 60 percent / 60 per cent Last November, in his "Greenwash" column for the Guardian, a British newspaper, environmental journalist Fred Pearce pointed out that New Zealand's greenhouse-gas emissions had risen 22% since 1990 (its commitment under the Kyoto Protocol was to keep them level) and were now 60% greater per head than Britain's Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says a writer in the Guardian newspaper reported that New Zealand's greenhouse-gas emissions went up 22% since 1990. It also says they are now 60% greater for each person than Britain's.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says New Zealand's greenhouse-gas emissions per person are 60% higher than Britain's.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 60% because the question asks 'how much greater' than Britain, and the passage says 'now 60% greater per head than Britain's'. Pay attention to the keywords 'Guardian', 'greater' and 'Britain'. Do not choose 22%, because that is how much New Zealand's own emissions rose since 1990.
Q22 farming at lower elevations farming has stripped away forests, eroded hills and clogged rivers with silt and fertiliser run-off Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that in lower land areas, agricultural work has removed trees, worn away the hills, and filled rivers with dirt and chemicals.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means growing crops or raising animals on land.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "farming" because the text directly states that in areas of lower ground ("lower elevations"), farming is the activity that has damaged the environment by wearing down and washing away the hills ("eroded hills").
Q23 global alliance At Copenhagen, Tim Groser, the minister for climate-change negotiations and a pragmatic former trade diplomat, pushed strongly for a "global alliance" to research ways of reducing agricultural greenhouse-gas emissions Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that at the Copenhagen summit, a minister named Tim Groser urged countries to create a worldwide partnership ("global alliance") to study how to cut down harmful gases caused by farming.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to a worldwide partnership or group of countries working together.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is confirmed in paragraph F. The text states that during the Copenhagen meeting, Tim Groser strongly advocated or proposed a "global alliance" to coordinate research across countries into lowering farm emissions. Therefore, the exact term he brought forward is "global alliance". Key phrases to look for include "pushed strongly for" (which matches "brought forward") and "Copenhagen".
Q24 FALSE New Zealand's greenhouse-gas emissions had risen 22% since 1990 (its commitment under the Kyoto Protocol was to keep them level) Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that under the Kyoto agreement, New Zealand promised to keep its emissions flat and not let them increase, but in reality, its emissions went up by 22%.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is FALSE because the statement contradicts what is written in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because New Zealand did not keep its greenhouse gas emissions at the promised amount. According to the Kyoto Protocol, New Zealand committed to keeping its emissions level (the same), but the text points out that its emissions actually increased by 22% since 1990. Therefore, the country released significantly more greenhouse gas than it promised, making the statement false.
Q25 FALSE In 2007 an "Environmental Performance Review of New Zealand" by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) noted a bunch of improvements over the past ten years: better approaches to recycling and water treatment, reforestation of pasture to prevent erosion, strengthened efforts to preserve endangered species, the removal of agricultural and fishing subsidies, and by OECD standards, a high use of renewable energy sources, at 30% of supply Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says the OECD reviewed New Zealand in 2007. It found many good changes, such as better recycling, better water treatment, and more renewable energy.
Answer Explanation:
The answer says the statement is false. The OECD did not criticize all of the government's efforts.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the OECD praised many efforts ('noted a bunch of improvements'). It also mentioned some problems, but only some, not all. So 'all' makes the statement wrong. Pay attention to the words 'improvements' and 'all'.
Q26 TRUE The emissions-trading scheme excludes agricultural emissions until 2015, and its generous allocations of free carbon credits to business have been lambasted by environmentalists Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the government's carbon-trading program does not include pollution from agriculture until the year 2015.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "TRUE" means the passage agrees that the emissions-trading plan currently leaves out pollution from farming.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is TRUE because Paragraph G clearly mentions that the country's carbon plan leaves out farm emissions until 2015. Since the text was written before 2015, "until 2015" confirms that agricultural emissions are excluded right now. Therefore, the statement matches the facts in the text.
Q27 FALSE Energetic lobbying by environmental groups forced it to scale back the amount of land under consideration Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that active pressure from environmental lobby groups successfully made the government reduce the land area being considered for mining.
Answer Explanation:
The answer FALSE means that the statement is incorrect because lobbying groups can and do have an effect on the government's decisions.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is FALSE because the text shows an example where pressure from lobbying groups successfully changed government plans. In Paragraph G, environmental groups engaged in "energetic lobbying," which actually "forced" the government to reduce ("scale back") the amount of land it was thinking of using for mining. This directly contradicts the idea that lobbying groups rarely have an influence.
Q28 NOT GIVEN But it is particularly acute in a country so dependent on the export of commodities and landscape-driven tourism Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that New Zealand relies heavily on selling commodities abroad (exports) and welcoming visitors (tourism), but it never says anything about depending on importing commodities.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the text does not provide enough information to say if the statement is true or false.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because the passage mentions that New Zealand relies heavily on the export of commodities (selling goods to other countries) and tourism, but it never mentions whether the country relies on commodity imports (buying goods from other countries). Because there is no information about New Zealand's reliance on commodity imports, the statement cannot be verified.
