Reading Passage

Bright Children

A BY the time Laszlo Polgar's first baby was born in 1969 he already had firm views on child-rearing. An eccentric citizen of communist Hungary, he had written a book called "Bring up Genius" and one of his favourite sayings was “Geniuses are made, not born”. An expert on the theory of chess, he proceeded to teach little Zsuzsa at home, spending up to ten hours a day on the game. Two more daughters were similarly hot-housed. All three obliged their father by becoming world-class players. The youngest, Judit, is currently ranked 13th in the world, and is by far the best female chess player of all time. Would the experiment have succeeded with a different trio of children? If any child can be turned into a star, then a lot of time and money are being wasted worldwide on trying to pick winners.

B America has long held "talent searches", using test results and teacher recommendations to select children for advanced school courses, summer schools and other extra tuition. This provision is set to grow. In his state-of-the-union address in 2006, President George Bush announced the “American Competitiveness Initiative", which, among much else, would train 70,000 high-school teachers to lead advanced courses for selected pupils in mathematics and science. Just as the superpowers' space race made Congress put money into science education, the thought of China and India turning out hundreds of thousands of engineers and scientists is scaring America into prodding its brightest to do their best.

C The philosophy behind this talent search is that ability is innate; that it can be diagnosed with considerable accuracy, and that it is worth cultivating. In America, bright children are ranked as “moderately", "highly", "exceptionally” and "profoundly” gifted. The only chance to influence innate ability is thought to be in the womb or the first couple of years of life. Hence the fad for “teaching aids" such as videos and flashcards for newborns, and “whale sounds" on tape which a pregnant mother can strap to her belly.

D In Britain, there is a broadly similar belief in the existence of innate talent, but also an egalitarian sentiment which makes people queasy about the idea of investing resources in grooming intelligence. Teachers are often opposed to separate provision for the best-performing children, saying any extra help should go to stragglers. In 2002, in a bid to help the able while leaving intact the ban on most selection by ability in state schools, the government set up the National Academy for Gifted and Talented Youth. This outfit runs summer schools and master-classes for children nominated by their schools. To date, though, only seven in ten secondary schools have nominated even a single child. Last year all schools were told they must supply the names of their top 10%.

E Picking winners is also the order of the day in ex-communist states, a hangover from the times when talented individuals were plucked from their homes and ruthlessly trained for the glory of the nation. But in many other countries, opposition to the idea of singling out talent and grooming it runs deep. In Scandinavia, a belief in virtues like modesty and social solidarity makes people flinch from the idea of treating brainy children differently.

F And in Japan there is a widespread belief that all children are born with the same innate abilities—and should therefore be treated alike. All are taught together, covering the same syllabus at the same rate until they finish compulsory schooling. Those who learn quickest are expected then to teach their classmates. In China, extra teaching is provided, but to a self-selected bunch. “Children's palaces'' in big cities offer a huge range of after-school classes. Anyone can sign up; all that is asked is excellent attendance.

G Statistics give little clue as to which system is best. The performance of the most able is heavily affected by factors other than state provision. Most state education in Britain is nominally non-selective, but middle-class parents try to live near the best schools. Ambitious Japanese parents have made private, out-of-school tuition a thriving business. And Scandinavia's egalitarianism might work less well in places with more diverse populations and less competent teachers. For what it's worth, the data suggest that some countries—like Japan and Finland, see table—can eschew selection and still thrive. But that does not mean that any country can ditch selection and do as well.

H Mr Polgar thought any child could be a prodigy given the right teaching, an early start and enough practice. At one point he planned to prove it by adopting three baby boys from a poor country and trying his methods on them. (His wife vetoed the scheme.) Some say the key to success is simply hard graft. Judit, the youngest of the Polgar sisters, was the most driven, and the most successful; Zsofia, the middle one, was regarded as the most talented, but she was the only one who did not achieve the status of grand master. “Everything came easiest to her,” said her older sister. “But she was lazy.”

  2. Genius tend to happen on first child
  3. Geniuses can be educated later on

  1. Practice makes genius

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q28 YES America has long held "talent searches", using test results and teacher recommendations to select children for advanced school courses, summer schools and other extra tuition
In America, bright children are ranked as “moderately", "highly", "exceptionally” and "profoundly” gifted		 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that America has spent a long time searching for gifted students using tests and teacher advice, and that these smart students are classified into different groups according to how gifted they are.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "YES" means that the passage confirms America has a long-standing practice of finding talented students and grouping or classifying them into distinct levels.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because the passage states in Paragraph B that "America has long held 'talent searches'" to pick students for special programs. Furthermore, Paragraph C explains that in America, smart children are put into specific levels or categories, being ranked as "moderately", "highly", "exceptionally", and "profoundly" gifted. Therefore, the statement matches the facts presented in the passage.
Q29 NO Teachers are often opposed to separate provision for the best-performing children, saying any extra help should go to stragglers Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that British teachers often disagree with giving special teaching to high-achieving children and believe that extra assistance should be given to students who are falling behind.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NO" means that the statement directly contradicts the facts provided in the text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NO because the text clearly states that teachers in Britain disagree with giving separate support to top-performing students, preferring extra help to go to struggling students instead. Therefore, they do not have a welcoming attitude toward selecting gifted students.
Q30 YES Most state education in Britain is nominally non-selective, but middle-class parents try to live near the best schools Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that even though state schools in Britain are not supposed to pick students based on ability, middle-class mothers and fathers still try to live in areas close to the top-performing schools.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "YES" means that the statement accurately reflects the information given in the reading text.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because Paragraph G mentions that in Britain, middle-class parents make an effort to live close to the best schools. Here, "middle-class parents" corresponds to "Some parents", and "live near the best schools" matches the idea of moving or choosing to reside "near reputable schools in Britain". Therefore, the statement is true.
Q31 NOT GIVEN Most state education in Britain is nominally non-selective, but middle-class parents try to live near the best schools Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that middle-class parents choose to live near good schools, but it does not state whether they participate in their children's education.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NOT GIVEN" means that the passage does not provide enough information to know whether middle-class parents take part in their children's education.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NOT GIVEN because while the text mentions that middle-class parents in Britain try to live close to the best schools, it never mentions whether they actively participate or get involved in their children's daily learning or school activities. Key words to notice are "middle-class parents" and "participate". Since this information is missing from the text, the statement cannot be confirmed as true or false.
Q32 NO For what it's worth, the data suggest that some countries—like Japan and Finland, see table—can eschew selection and still thrive Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that information shows that countries such as Japan and Finland can avoid separating or choosing students based on ability and still do very well.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "NO" means that the statement is false according to the text, because Japan and Finland actually avoid selecting students rather than following a selection policy.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is NO because Paragraph G states that countries like Japan and Finland can "eschew selection and still thrive." The word "eschew" means to avoid or abstain from doing something. Therefore, saying that Japan and Finland "comply with" (follow) a policy of selecting students directly contradicts what the passage says.
Q33 YES And Scandinavia's egalitarianism might work less well in places with more diverse populations and less competent teachers. For what it's worth, the data suggest that some countries—like Japan and Finland, see table—can eschew selection and still thrive. But that does not mean that any country can ditch selection and do as well Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that treating all students equally without selecting the top ones works well in some places, like Japan and Finland. However, it might not work well in places with different types of people or weaker teachers, showing that not every country can stop selecting students and achieve the same success.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "YES" means that, according to the text, a school system that avoids choosing or separating students by ability is only successful under certain special conditions.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is YES because Paragraph G discusses how countries without student selection (such as Finland and Japan) can still perform well, but notes that this equal approach might not work in places with different conditions, such as more diverse populations or less skilled teachers. The author concludes: "But that does not mean that any country can ditch selection and do as well." Here, key phrases like "eschew selection" and "ditch selection" match the idea of an "avoiding-selection-policy," and the text confirms that this approach does not succeed everywhere, meaning it works only in specific environments.
Q34 C Mr Polgar thought any child could be a prodigy given the right teaching, an early start and enough practice Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Mr. Polgar believed any kid could become super talented if they received good instruction, started young, and practiced hard.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "C" means that Laszlo Polgar believed anyone can become extremely smart and talented through learning, training, and practice, rather than just being born that way.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text states that Laszlo Polgar believed "Geniuses are made, not born" and that any child could become a prodigy with the right education and enough practice. This directly matches the idea that geniuses can be created through education and training rather than natural talent alone.
Q35 A Some say the key to success is simply hard graft. Judit, the youngest of the Polgar sisters, was the most driven, and the most successful; Zsofia, the middle one, was regarded as the most talented, but she was the only one who did not achieve the status of grand master. “Everything came easiest to her,” said her older sister. “But she was lazy.” Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that hard work is the secret to success. Even though Zsofia had natural talent and learned easily, she was lazy and did not work hard, so she did not achieve the highest title in chess like her hardworking sister did.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that working hard and practicing is what creates real genius and success.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph H discusses whether natural talent or hard work leads to success. The author notes that "the key to success is simply hard graft" (hard work/practice). To demonstrate this point, Zsofia's example is cited: although she was considered the "most talented" naturally, she was "lazy" and did not practice as hard, making her the only sister who failed to become a grandmaster. This shows that talent without practice is not enough, and that practice makes genius.
Q36 B And in Japan there is a widespread belief that all children are born with the same innate abilities—and should therefore be treated alike. All are taught together, covering the same syllabus at the same rate until they finish compulsory schooling. Those who learn quickest are expected then to teach their classmates Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in Japan, all students learn together, and the children who learn the fastest are asked to teach and help the other children in the class.
Answer Explanation:
The answer B means Japan is the country where students who learn faster help teach their fellow classmates.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Paragraph F describes the education approach in Japan. In Japanese schools, students are taught together at the same speed. Students who learn the fastest are expected to help and teach their classmates who need more time or help.
Q37 D In China, extra teaching is provided, but to a self-selected bunch. “Children's palaces'' in big cities offer a huge range of after-school classes. Anyone can sign up; all that is asked is excellent attendance Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in China, additional after-school lessons are available, and any child is allowed to join them as long as they show up regularly.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "D" means that China is the country where any student can join extra classes.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is D because paragraph F describes how after-school education works in China. It explains that extra teaching is offered to children who choose it themselves, and at places called "Children's palaces," anyone is allowed to enroll or sign up for classes as long as they attend regularly. Therefore, extra teaching is open to anyone in China.
Q38 A In Scandinavia, a belief in virtues like modesty and social solidarity makes people flinch from the idea of treating brainy children differently Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in Scandinavia, values such as being humble and supporting the whole group make people uncomfortable with giving special care or separation to very smart students.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "A" means that Scandinavia is the place where people do not like giving special treatment to smart children because of their cultural values.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because Paragraph E states that people in Scandinavia do not like treating smart children differently due to social values like modesty and solidarity. Here, "modesty and social solidarity" are the "social characteristics," and "flinch from the idea of treating brainy children differently" means people are "reluctant to favor gifted children."
Q39 C In Britain, there is a broadly similar belief in the existence of innate talent, but also an egalitarian sentiment which makes people queasy about the idea of investing resources in grooming intelligence Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that people in Britain believe children can be born with special talent, but they also believe strongly in fairness and equality, which makes them uncomfortable with giving special training only to smart students.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "C" means Britain is the place where people believe both in natural-born talent (innate ability) and in equal treatment for all students (egalitarian ideas) at the same time.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because Paragraph D clearly states that Britain shares a belief in natural-born talent ("belief in the existence of innate talent") alongside an attitude favoring equality ("egalitarian sentiment"). These two ideas existing together match the phrase "both view of innate and egalitarian co-existed".
Q40 E In America, bright children are ranked as “moderately", "highly", "exceptionally” and "profoundly” gifted. The only chance to influence innate ability is thought to be in the womb or the first couple of years of life. Hence the fad for “teaching aids" such as videos and flashcards for newborns, and “whale sounds" on tape which a pregnant mother can strap to her belly Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in America, people believe a baby's intelligence can only be shaped before birth or right after. Therefore, it became a trend to use videos and audio tapes attached to a pregnant mother's belly to teach babies early.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "E" means America is the country connected to the craze of audio and video teaching tools for pregnant mothers and very young babies.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because paragraph C discusses the situation in "America", explaining that people believe natural intelligence can only be influenced while a baby is still in the womb or in their first years. Because of this belief, there is a popular trend or "fad" (craze) for "videos" and audio recordings like "whale sounds" on tape that a pregnant mother puts on her belly.
