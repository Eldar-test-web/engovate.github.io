A New Fair Trade Organisation - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14 – 26, which are based on Reading Passage 2.

A New Fair Trade Organisation

Trade has, so far, proved ineffective in solving the major problems faced by most nations. However, the answer to the injustices of the existing trade regime is not no trade, but fair trade.

The existing regime forbids poor nations from following the path taken by the rich. With the exceptions of Switzerland, Belgium and the Netherlands, all the nations that have become independently wealthy did so with the help of a mechanism economists call ‘infant industry protection’: defending new sectors from foreign competition until they are big enough to compete on equal terms. The textile industry in Britain, for example, on which the Industrial Revolution was built in the nineteenth century, was nurtured and promoted by means of tariffs (or trade taxes) and the outright prohibition of competing goods. Between 1864 and 1913, the US was the most heavily protected nation on earth. Only when these countries had established technological and commercial superiority did they suddenly discover the virtues of unimpeded competition.

For nations to develop in direct competition with countries with established industries is like learning to swim in a fast-flowing river: you are likely to be swept away and drowned long before you acquire the necessary expertise. Your competitors have experience, legal rights and established marketing networks on their side; your infant industries have none of these. It is all but impossible, in other words, for poor nations to extract money from the rich unless they can safeguard some key parts of their economies.

Clearly, nations that are currently poor should be permitted to defend certain industries from foreign competition with the help of tariff barriers and subsidies. Rich nations, on the other hand, should be permitted neither to subsidise their industries nor to impose tariffs on imports. Nations should be forced gradually to lift their protections as they develop. So, the first function of what we might call the Fair Trade Organisation (FTO) would be to lay down the rules governing the protections and privileges permitted at different stages of development.

A fair-trade system should, or so we should hope, slowly push the world towards genuine free trade, which is likely to be the most equitable means of governing nations’ relationships with each other. This system could provide a potent means by which the world could begin to move towards the economic equality that is an essential precondition for political equality. It would not, however, directly address some of the other critical problems that the people of poor nations confront – such as inadequate working conditions, environmental devastation and the inordinate power of the multinational corporations.

Many campaigners in the rich world have suggested that the best way to raise standards is to discriminate, through tariffs or other measures, against imports from countries where workers or the environment are mistreated. This approach has also been advocated by trades unions seeking to protect members’ jobs from foreigners. Unsurprisingly, it is deeply resented by the very people it is supposed to help: the workers of the poor world.

If our purpose is to regulate international trade, then it surely makes sense to address the behaviour, not of nation states, but of the multinational corporations operating between them. So a second function of the FTO could be to set the standards to which those corporations must conform. A corporation would not be permitted to trade between nations unless it could demonstrate that, at every stage of manufacture and distribution, its own operations and those of its suppliers met the necessary standards.

If, for example, a food-processing corporation based in Europe wished to import cocoa from an African country, it would need to demonstrate that the plantation owners it bought from were not using banned pesticides, expanding into protected forests or failing to conform to whatever other standards the FTO set. The company’s performance would be assessed, at its own expense, by monitors accredited to the organisation.

One other precondition of justice is that producers and consumers should carry their own costs, rather than dumping them on other people. The monitors deployed by the FTO could determine whether or not companies are paying a fair price for the resources they use. Companies would, among other costs, have to buy enough of a nation’s carbon quota to cover the fossil fuel they consume.

One of the many beneficial impacts of such full-cost accounting would be that everything that could be processed in the country of origin would be. No multinational company would export logs, coffee beans or cotton, as it requires far more (costly) energy to transport these bulky resources from one place to another than would be involved in exporting the finished products – furniture, instant coffee and T-shirts (all currently manufactured on the other side of the world). Those nations which are currently locked into the export of raw materials would become the most favoured locations for manufacturing.

Under this scheme, export growth comes to measure something quite different. At present it represents a mixture of gains and losses, which are misleadingly compounded into a single figure. The loss of natural resources is ‘added’ to the genuine addition of value provided by the application of labour. The FTO system would effectively separate these measures. The extraction and export of natural resources would in most cases be accounted as a loss. The application of human labour would be measured as a gain. Nations would be able to see immediately whether they were being enriched or impoverished through trade. To introduce these measures in the face of the resistance of the world’s most powerful governments and companies would require severe and unusual methods. But the goal of universal fair trade would permit the global economic levelling without which there can be no justice.

Questions

Questions 14–19 One Choice

Choose the correct letter, A, B, C or D.

14 The writer refers to textile production in Britain in order to
  1. point out how differently industries were financed in the past.
  2. show how unnecessary tariff barriers are for countries today.
  3. help the reader understand how infant industry protection works.
  4. compare European trade development with that of the United States.
15 What is the writer’s main point in the third paragraph?
  1. Businesses will succeed if they learn from established companies.
  2. Detailed market research is often neglected in developing countries.
  3. You have to be prepared to adapt your products quickly to follow fashion.
  4. New industries in poor countries will probably fail without protection.
16 According to the writer, a fair trade system could have the effect of
  1. improving safety in the majority of workplaces around the world.
  2. preventing the continued destruction of endangered wildlife habitats.
  3. encouraging states to work together in a more even-handed way.
  4. making politicians agree to more representative systems of government.
17 What point is the writer making in the sixth paragraph?
  1. The trades unions’ aim is to help foreign workers gain better conditions.
  2. The trades unions are concerned about the effects of imports on local jobs.
  3. Workers in poor countries are grateful for the trades unions’ support.
  4. Campaigners are right to suggest imposing tariffs against bad treatment.
18 According to the writer, what is one of the benefits of full-cost accounting?
  1. Factories would be set up and jobs created in the country of origin.
  2. Multinational companies would consume fewer natural resources.
  3. The export of finished products around the world would decrease.
  4. Countries would be able to keep their resources for the domestic market.
19 What conclusion does the writer come to about the FTO system?
  1. It would help to combat injustice in its many different forms.
  2. It would be difficult to introduce but would be worth the effort.
  3. States all over the world would earn more through trade as a result of it.
  4. Multinationals would accept it because it measures exports more precisely.

Questions 20–26 Summary Completion

Complete the summary below.

Choose NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage for each answer.

A Proposal for Regulating Multinational Corporations

The FTO would determine the 20 for the multinational corporations to follow. In this way, a multinational corporation would have to prove that all aspects of the way it produced its goods and the systems for their 21 to customers were in line with FTO requirements. Similarly it would need to satisfy the FTO that the processes employed by any 22 that it used were also acceptable.

As an illustration, in order to source cocoa from Africa, a corporation would have to ensure that no illegal 23 were being used by the 24 during cultivation and that they had not taken over land from 25.

It would not be sufficient for multinational corporations to say that these points had been checked. Their conduct would have to be inspected by 26 appointed by the FTO.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q14 C With the exceptions of Switzerland, Belgium and the Netherlands, all the nations that have become independently wealthy did so with the help of a mechanism economists call ‘infant industry protection’: defending new sectors from foreign competition until they are big enough to compete on equal terms. The textile industry in Britain, for example, on which the Industrial Revolution was built in the nineteenth century, was nurtured and promoted by means of tariffs (or trade taxes) and the outright prohibition of competing goods Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that almost all rich nations grew wealthy by shielding their young businesses from foreign competitors until they grew strong. It then gives the British textile industry as a clear example of how trade taxes (tariffs) and bans on outside goods helped protect and grow a crucial sector.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the author mentions British textile production as an example to help readers understand the idea of protecting new, growing businesses (called "infant industry protection").
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text introduces the concept of "infant industry protection" (defending new business areas from foreign competitors) and immediately presents the British textile industry as a specific "example" of how this system was used to grow and succeed through tariffs and trade restrictions. Keywords to pay attention to include "infant industry protection" and "for example".
Q15 D For nations to develop in direct competition with countries with established industries is like learning to swim in a fast-flowing river: you are likely to be swept away and drowned long before you acquire the necessary expertise. Your competitors have experience, legal rights and established marketing networks on their side; your infant industries have none of these. It is all but impossible, in other words, for poor nations to extract money from the rich unless they can safeguard some key parts of their economies Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that when new industries in developing nations directly compete with strong, experienced foreign companies, they are likely to fail ('be swept away and drowned'). Therefore, poor countries must protect ('safeguard') their young industries to have a chance of succeeding.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that brand-new businesses and sectors in poorer nations are almost certain to collapse if they are not defended against competition from stronger, established countries.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the third paragraph, where the author compares developing an industry against established foreign competitors to trying to swim in a dangerous, fast-moving river. The author explains that new ('infant') industries lack experience and established networks, meaning poor nations cannot succeed or gain wealth unless they protect ('safeguard') key parts of their economies.
Q16 C A fair-trade system should, or so we should hope, slowly push the world towards genuine free trade, which is likely to be the most equitable means of governing nations’ relationships with each other Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that fair trade can lead to real free trade, which is the fairest way for different countries to manage their relationships with one another.
Answer Explanation:
The answer choice means that a fair-trade system could help countries deal with and treat each other more fairly and equally.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text states in paragraph 5 that a fair-trade system can guide the world toward genuine free trade, which would be the "most equitable means of governing nations’ relationships with each other." Here, the word "equitable" means "even-handed" or fair, "nations" means "states", and "governing nations' relationships" refers to countries working together.
Q17 B This approach has also been advocated by trades unions seeking to protect members’ jobs from foreigners Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that trade unions support this policy because they want to safeguard their own members' jobs from competition with foreign workers.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that labor groups (trade unions) worry that bringing in goods from other countries could cause their own workers to lose their jobs.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because the sixth paragraph explains that trade unions support placing restrictions and tariffs on imports because they are "seeking to protect members’ jobs from foreigners." In this context, "protect members' jobs" refers to trade unions being concerned about the negative impact of foreign goods (imports) on the employment of their own local members.
Q18 A One of the many beneficial impacts of such full-cost accounting would be that everything that could be processed in the country of origin would be. No multinational company would export logs, coffee beans or cotton, as it requires far more (costly) energy to transport these bulky resources from one place to another than would be involved in exporting the finished products – furniture, instant coffee and T-shirts (all currently manufactured on the other side of the world). Those nations which are currently locked into the export of raw materials would become the most favoured locations for manufacturing Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that under this system, raw materials like wood, coffee beans, and cotton would no longer be sent away to be processed. Instead, making the final products would happen right where the materials are found, making those countries preferred places for manufacturing.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that goods would be made locally where the raw materials come from, bringing factories and work to those areas.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the tenth paragraph of the passage. The author explains that under full-cost accounting, it would be too expensive to transport heavy, unprocessed materials across the world. Therefore, goods would be processed in the "country of origin," turning countries that currently only export raw resources into top places for "manufacturing" (making goods in factories).
Q19 B To introduce these measures in the face of the resistance of the world’s most powerful governments and companies would require severe and unusual methods. But the goal of universal fair trade would permit the global economic levelling without which there can be no justice Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that bringing in these trade rules will be tough because powerful countries and corporations will fight against them. However, setting up universal fair trade is necessary because true fairness in the world cannot exist without it.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that starting this new trade system would be very hard because powerful groups will oppose it, but doing it is still necessary and valuable for global fairness.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because at the very end of the passage, the writer summarizes their final conclusion about the FTO system. The author mentions that bringing in these rules against the pushback of strong governments and big companies would require hard measures ('difficult to introduce'), but achieving fair trade is essential to create economic equality and justice ('worth the effort').
Q20 standards / rules So a second function of the FTO could be to set the standards to which those corporations must conform Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that another duty of the FTO is to create the specific rules and standards that large international companies are required to obey.
Answer Explanation:
The answer refers to the official requirements, levels of quality, or rules that large international companies must follow.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by paragraph 7, which discusses how the Fair Trade Organisation (FTO) would regulate multinational corporations. The text states that a key job of the FTO is to "set the standards to which those corporations must conform." In this context, "set" matches the summary's "determine", and "must conform" matches "to follow". Therefore, "standards" (or "rules") correctly completes the blank.
Q21 distribution A corporation would not be permitted to trade between nations unless it could demonstrate that, at every stage of manufacture and distribution, its own operations and those of its suppliers met the necessary standards Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that a company is only allowed to trade internationally if it can show that all stages of making and delivering its products follow the required rules.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "distribution" means the process of delivering and supplying goods to shops or customers.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "distribution" because the passage explains that a company must prove that every step of making ("manufacture") and delivering goods ("distribution") meets the required rules. In the summary, "produced its goods" corresponds to "manufacture", and "systems for their [distribution] to customers" refers directly to "distribution".
Q22 suppliers A corporation would not be permitted to trade between nations unless it could demonstrate that, at every stage of manufacture and distribution, its own operations and those of its suppliers met the necessary standards Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that a company cannot trade internationally unless it proves that both its own activities and those of the companies supplying it follow all the required rules.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "suppliers" refers to the companies or individuals that provide raw materials or goods to a business.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "suppliers" because the text explains that a corporation must show that both its own work and the work of its "suppliers" follow the required rules. In the summary, the phrase "processes employed by any [suppliers] that it used" matches the passage's mention of "those of its suppliers" meeting the necessary standards.
Q23 pesticides If, for example, a food-processing corporation based in Europe wished to import cocoa from an African country, it would need to demonstrate that the plantation owners it bought from were not using banned pesticides, expanding into protected forests or failing to conform to whatever other standards the FTO set Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that if a company wants to buy cocoa from an African country, it must prove that the farm owners are not using banned (illegal) chemicals (pesticides) to grow the crops.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "pesticides" refers to chemical substances used in farming to kill insects or other harmful organisms that damage crops.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "pesticides" because the summary discusses the rules a corporation must follow when buying cocoa from Africa. The passage states that the company must prove that suppliers are not using "banned pesticides". In the summary, the word "illegal" corresponds directly to "banned", so the missing word indicating what cannot be used is "pesticides".
Q24 plantation owners If, for example, a food-processing corporation based in Europe wished to import cocoa from an African country, it would need to demonstrate that the plantation owners it bought from were not using banned pesticides, expanding into protected forests or failing to conform to whatever other standards the FTO set Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that if a company wants to buy cocoa from an African country, it must prove that the people who own the cocoa farms (plantation owners) are not using banned chemicals, destroying protected forests, or breaking rules.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "plantation owners" refers to the people or businesses that own large farms (plantations) where crops like cocoa are grown.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is confirmed by paragraph 8. The passage provides an example of a European company buying cocoa from Africa. In that example, the company must show that "the plantation owners it bought from" were not using forbidden chemicals (pesticides). In the summary, "during cultivation" corresponds to growing the crops on the plantation, and "illegal [pesticides] were being used by the [plantation owners]" directly matches the text.
Q25 protected forests If, for example, a food-processing corporation based in Europe wished to import cocoa from an African country, it would need to demonstrate that the plantation owners it bought from were not using banned pesticides, expanding into protected forests or failing to conform to whatever other standards the FTO set Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that a company buying cocoa must prove that the plantation owners are not clearing or taking over land inside forests that are legally protected.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "protected forests" refers to areas of woodland or forest that are legally guarded and kept safe from farming, destruction, or unauthorized development.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "protected forests" because the summary discusses the rules plantation owners must follow regarding land. The passage states that a food-processing company wishing to import cocoa from Africa must prove that the growers are not "expanding into protected forests". Therefore, taking over land from "protected forests" fits the context perfectly.
Q26 monitors The company’s performance would be assessed, at its own expense, by monitors accredited to the organisation Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that official inspectors (monitors) approved by the organisation would check the company’s actions, and the company would have to pay for this inspection.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "monitors" refers to official checkers or inspectors who examine and judge whether a company is obeying the rules.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "monitors" because the passage explains that a company's actions would be evaluated by inspectors linked to the Fair Trade Organisation. In the text, the phrase "performance would be assessed" matches "conduct would have to be inspected", and "accredited to the organisation" matches "appointed by the FTO". Therefore, the people doing the inspecting are the "monitors".
