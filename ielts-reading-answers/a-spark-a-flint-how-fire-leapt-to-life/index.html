A spark, a flint: How fire leapt to life

The control of fire was the first and perhaps greatest of humanity’s steps towards a life-enhancing technology

To early man, fire was a divine gift randomly delivered in the form of lightning, forest fire or burning lava. Unable to make flame for themselves, the earliest peoples probably stored fire by keeping slow burning logs alight or by carrying charcoal in pots.

How and where man learnt how to produce flame at will is unknown. It was probably a secondary invention, accidentally made during tool-making operations with wood or stone. Studies of primitive societies suggest that the earliest method of making fire was through friction. European peasants would insert a wooden drill in a round hole and rotate it briskly between their palms. This process could be speeded up by wrapping a cord around the drill and pulling on each end.

The Ancient Greeks used lenses or concave mirrors to concentrate the sun’s rays and burning glasses were also used by Mexican Aztecs and the Chinese.

Percussion methods of fire-lighting date back to Paleolithic times, when some Stone Age tool-makers discovered that chipping flints produced sparks. The technique became more efficient after the discovery of iron, about 5000 years ago. In Arctic North America, the Eskimos produced a slow-burning spark by striking quartz against iron pyrites, a compound that contains sulphur. The Chinese lit their fires by striking porcelain with bamboo. In Europe, the combination of steel, flint and tinder remained the main method of fire-lighting until the mid 19th century.

Fire-lighting was revolutionised by the discovery of phosphorus, isolated in 1669 by a German alchemist trying to transmute silver into gold. Impressed by the element’s combustibility, several 17th century chemists used it to manufacture fire-lighting devices, but the results were dangerously inflammable. With phosphorus costing the equivalent of several hundred pounds per ounce, the first matches were expensive.

The quest for a practical match really began after 1781 when a group of French chemists came up with the Phosphoric Candle or Ethereal Match, a sealed glass tube containing a twist of paper tipped with phosphorus. When the tube was broken, air rushed in, causing the phosphorus to self-combust. An even more hazardous device, popular in America, was the Instantaneous Light Box — a bottle filled with sulphuric acid into which splints treated with chemicals were dipped.

The first matches resembling those used today were made in 1827 by John Walker, an English pharmacist who borrowed the formula from a military rocket-maker called Congreve. Costing a shilling a box, Congreves were splints coated with sulphur and tipped with potassium chlorate. To light them, the user drew them quickly through folded glass paper.

Walker never patented his invention, and three years later it was copied by a Samuel Jones, who marketed his product as Lucifers. About the same time, a French chemistry student called Charles Sauria produced the first “strike-anywhere” match by substituting white phosphorus for the potassium chlorate in the Walker formula. However, since white phosphorus is a deadly poison, from 1845 match-makers exposed to its fumes succumbed to necrosis, a disease that eats away jaw-bones. It wasn’t until 1906 that the substance was eventually banned.

That was 62 years after a Swedish chemist called Pasch had discovered non-toxic red or amorphous phosphorus, a development exploited commercially by Pasch’s compatriot J E Lundstrom in 1885. Lundstrom’s safety matches were safe because the red phosphorus was non-toxic; it was painted on to the striking surface instead of the match tip, which contained potassium chlorate with a relatively high ignition temperature of 182 degrees centigrade.

America lagged behind Europe in match technology and safety standards. It wasn’t until 1900 that the Diamond Match Company bought a French patent for safety matches — but the formula did not work properly in the different climatic conditions prevailing in America and it was another 11 years before scientists finally adapted the French patent for the US.

The Americans, however, can claim several “firsts” in match technology and marketing. In 1892 the Diamond Match Company pioneered book matches. The innovation didn’t catch on until after 1896, when a brewery had the novel idea of advertising its product in match books. Today book matches are the most widely used type in the US, with 90 percent handed out free by hotels, restaurants and others.

Other American innovations include an anti-afterglow solution to prevent the match from smouldering after it has been blown out; and the waterproof match, which lights after eight hours in water.

Answers & Explanations Summary

# Answer Evidence Explanation
Q1 preserve Unable to make flame for themselves, the earliest peoples probably stored fire by keeping slow burning logs alight or by carrying charcoal in pots Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that because early humans could not create fire, they saved it by keeping wood logs burning or by carrying hot charcoal in containers.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'preserve' means to keep something in its original state or save it so that it continues to exist.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'preserve' because the text explains that early humans did not know how to start fires on their own. Instead, they had to find ways to keep existing fire going for as long as possible. The passage uses the word 'stored' to describe how they kept logs burning or carried charcoal, which is a synonym for preserving something for future use.
Q2 unaware Unable to make flame for themselves, the earliest peoples probably stored fire by keeping slow burning logs alight or by carrying charcoal in pots Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that because the first humans did not know how to create fire, they had to keep natural fire burning in wood or pots to save it for later use.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'unaware' means that the first humans did not know or realize that they had the ability to start a fire by themselves.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is chosen because the passage states that early people were 'Unable to make flame for themselves.' This lack of ability suggests they did not yet have the knowledge or skills to produce fire. Because they didn't know it was possible to create fire, they were 'unaware' of it and chose to 'preserve' (store) fire from natural sources instead.
Q3 chance It was probably a secondary invention, accidentally made during tool-making operations with wood or stone Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that people likely discovered how to make flames by mistake while they were busy working on tools made of wood or stone.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "chance" means that the ability to create fire was not a planned invention, but rather something that happened by accident.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "chance" because the passage explains that the first way to make fire was likely a "secondary invention" that was "accidentally made." This implies that early humans were not trying to invent fire; instead, it happened by luck or "chance" while they were focused on other tasks like making tools.
Q4 friction Studies of primitive societies suggest that the earliest method of making fire was through friction Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that based on research into very old cultures, the first way humans learned to start a fire was by rubbing materials together.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "friction" refers to the act of rubbing two objects or surfaces against each other to create heat.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "friction" because the passage explicitly mentions it as the suspected first way humans created fire. The summary uses the phrase "It is suspected," which corresponds to the passage's phrase "Studies... suggest." Furthermore, the summary refers to "the first man-made flames," which directly relates to the passage's mention of the "earliest method of making fire."
Q5 rotating European peasants would insert a wooden drill in a round hole and rotate it briskly between their palms Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage states that long ago, people in Europe would put a piece of wood into a hole and turn it quickly with their hands to start a fire.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "rotating" means turning something around and around in a circle very quickly.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by the text's description of early fire-making techniques. The passage mentions that people in Europe used a "wooden drill" and would "rotate it briskly" inside a hole to create fire. In the summary, the word "rapidly" is used instead of briskly, but both mean the same thing (fast). Therefore, "rotating" correctly describes the action used to make fire with a stick.
Q6 percussion Percussion methods of fire-lighting date back to Paleolithic times, when some Stone Age tool-makers discovered that chipping flints produced sparks. The technique became more efficient after the discovery of iron, about 5000 years ago. In Arctic North America, the Eskimos produced a slow-burning spark by striking quartz against iron pyrites, a compound that contains sulphur. The Chinese lit their fires by striking porcelain with bamboo. In Europe, the combination of steel, flint and tinder remained the main method of fire-lighting until the mid 19th century Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that percussion is an ancient way to make fire by hitting stones together to make sparks. It mentions that this way was used by the Chinese, the Eskimos, and by people in Europe until the middle of the 1800s.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'percussion' refers to a method of making fire by hitting or striking two hard objects (like stone and metal) against each other to create sparks.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'percussion' because the passage explicitly links the term 'percussion' with the act of 'chipping flints' or striking objects together to start a fire. The summary sentence describes a method using 'percussion or persistent chipping,' which matches the passage's description of 'percussion methods' as 'chipping flints' to produce sparks. Furthermore, the passage notes that this method was used by people in Europe, China, and by the Eskimos, which fits the context of the summary perfectly.
Q7 Eskimos In Arctic North America, the Eskimos produced a slow-burning spark by striking quartz against iron pyrites, a compound that contains sulphur. The Chinese lit their fires by striking porcelain with bamboo Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage confirms that the Eskimo people in North America and the Chinese people both used the technique of hitting two objects together to create sparks for starting a fire.
Answer Explanation:
The answer identifies the Eskimos as a group of people who used a method of hitting objects together (percussion) to create fire.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'Eskimos' because the passage includes them in the section about percussion (striking) methods. It explains that while Europeans used steel and flint, other groups like the Chinese and the Eskimos also used similar striking methods with different materials to create the sparks needed for fire.
Q8 despite In Europe, the combination of steel, flint and tinder remained the main method of fire-lighting until the mid 19th century
Fire-lighting was revolutionised by the discovery of phosphorus, isolated in 1669 by a German alchemist trying to transmute silver into gold		 Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage tells us that people in Europe used old tools like steel and flint as their main way to start fires until the middle of the 1800s. It also mentions that phosphorus, which changed how fires were made, was actually found much earlier, in the year 1669.
Answer Explanation:
The answer 'despite' is used to show that something continued to happen even though there was a reason why it might have changed.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is 'despite' because it links two contrasting facts from the passage. One fact is that the old 'percussion' method (using steel and flint) was the primary way people in Europe lit fires until the 'mid 19th century' (the 1850s). The other fact is that phosphorus, a more modern fire-lighting element, had been 'isolated in 1669', which is many years earlier. The word 'despite' explains that people kept using the old method even though the new discovery was already available.
Q9 F Lundstrom’s safety matches were safe because the red phosphorus was non-toxic Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Lundstrom's matches were safe because they were made with red phosphorus, and this material was not toxic (it was not a poison).
Answer Explanation:
The answer is Lundstrom’s safety match, which was a type of match that did not use dangerous chemicals.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because J E Lundstrom made safety matches using "red phosphorus." According to the text, this kind of phosphorus was "non-toxic," which means it was not poisonous. This was much safer than the "white phosphorus" used in older matches, which the passage calls a "deadly poison" that caused a serious disease called necrosis in workers.
Q10 D Walker never patented his invention, and three years later it was copied by a Samuel Jones, who marketed his product as Lucifers Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that Samuel Jones sold a match called Lucifers after copying an invention made by someone named Walker three years earlier.
Answer Explanation:
The answer, Lucifers, refers to a type of match that was exactly the same as an earlier invention.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is Lucifers (D) because the text states that Samuel Jones "copied" the match invention created by John Walker. In this context, "copied" means that the product Jones sold under the name Lucifers was the same as the "previous type" made by Walker. Because Walker did not get a patent (legal protection for an invention), other people were able to make the exact same thing.
Q11 E About the same time, a French chemistry student called Charles Sauria produced the first “strike-anywhere” match by substituting white phosphorus for the potassium chlorate in the Walker formula. However, since white phosphorus is a deadly poison, from 1845 match-makers exposed to its fumes succumbed to necrosis, a disease that eats away jaw-bones Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that the first match that could be struck anywhere contained a dangerous chemical. This chemical was very poisonous and caused factory workers to get a bad sickness that hurt the bones in their faces.
Answer Explanation:
The answer E refers to the first “strike-anywhere” match. This match was dangerous because it was made with a chemical that made people very sick.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because the text explains that the first “strike-anywhere” match used white phosphorus. This chemical is called a “deadly poison” in the passage. Because of this poison, people who worked in match factories got a “disease” called necrosis, which was serious enough to destroy their jaw-bones.
Q12 C The first matches resembling those used today were made in 1827 by John Walker, an English pharmacist who borrowed the formula from a military rocket-maker called Congreve. Costing a shilling a box, Congreves were splints coated with sulphur and tipped with potassium chlorate Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage says that in 1827, John Walker made the first matches that look like the ones we use now. These matches were named "Congreves" and were made of small wood sticks with chemicals on the ends.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the type of match called "Congreves" was the very first kind to look similar to the matches people use today.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text describes John Walker's matches from 1827 as the first ones that look like modern matches. In the passage, these matches are called "Congreves." The author notes that they were the first to resemble (look like) matches used today and describes them as "splints" (small sticks) with chemical tips, which is the same basic design as modern matches.
Q13 G In 1892 the Diamond Match Company pioneered book matches. The innovation didn’t catch on until after 1896, when a brewery had the novel idea of advertising its product in match books Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that book matches were first created in 1892, but they only became famous in 1896 when a beer company decided to use them to advertise their product.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is book matches because this was the first kind of match used to show advertisements for a business.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is G because the text says that book matches were started by the Diamond Match Company in 1892. They became popular when a company that makes beer (a brewery) had the new idea to use these matches for advertising in 1896. This matches the description of being the first matches used for promotion.
Q14 A the Phosphoric Candle or Ethereal Match, a sealed glass tube containing a twist of paper tipped with phosphorus. When the tube was broken, air rushed in, causing the phosphorus to self-combust Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that this specific match was made of a glass tube that was completely closed. Inside the tube was paper with a special chemical. When a person broke the glass, air entered quickly and made the chemical burn.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means that the Ethereal Match (Option A) was a fire-making tool that used a closed glass tube to keep air away from the chemicals inside.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because the passage describes the Ethereal Match (also called the Phosphoric Candle) as being inside a 'sealed glass tube.' In this context, 'sealed' is a synonym for 'airtight,' meaning it was closed so tightly that no air could get in or out. The device 'relied on' this container because the chemical inside would only catch fire ('self-combust') once the glass was broken and air reached it.
Q15 C The first matches resembling those used today were made in 1827 by John Walker, an English pharmacist who borrowed the formula from a military rocket-maker called Congreve Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in 1827, John Walker created matches by using a recipe (formula) he got from a person named Congreve, who designed rockets for the army (military).
Answer Explanation:
The answer is the type of match known as Congreves.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because the text states that John Walker, who made these matches, "borrowed the formula from a military rocket-maker named Congreve." The word "military" is a synonym for "army," and the "formula" for a rocket is a type of "design."
