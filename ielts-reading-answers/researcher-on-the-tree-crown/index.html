Researcher On The Tree Crown - IELTS Reading Answers & Explanations

From IELTS Mock Tests Academic Reading Test 65 · Part 2 · Questions 14–27

Reading Passage

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14-27 which are based on Reading Passage 2 below.

Researcher on the Tree Crown

A

The forest canopy – the term given to the aggregated crowns of trees in a forest – is thought to host up to 40 per cent of all species, of which ten per cent could be unique to the forest roof. "We're dealing with the richest, least known, most threatened habitat on Earth," says Andrew Mitchell, the executive director of the Global Canopy Programme (GCP), a collection of groups undertaking research into this lofty world. "The problem with our understanding of forests is that nearly all the information we have has been gleaned from just two meters above the soil, and yet we're dealing with trees that grow to heights of 60 meters, or in the case of the tallest redwood 112 meters. It's like doctors trying to treat humans by only looking at their feet."

B

Tropical rainforest comprises the richest of ecosystems, rivalled only by coral reefs for its diversity and complex interrelationships. And a great deal of that diversity lives up in the canopy – an estimated 70-90 per cent of life in the rainforest exists in the trees; one in ten of all vascular plants are canopy dwellers, and about 20-25 per cent of all invertebrates are thought to be unique to the canopy.

C

The first Briton to actually get into the canopy may have been Sir Francis Drake who, in 1573, gained his first glimpse of the Pacific Ocean from a tall tree in Darien, Panama. However, the first serious effort to reach and study the canopy didn't begin until 1929. The Oxford University Expedition to British Guiana, led by Major RWG Hingston, still ended up needing the help of locals when it came to building an observation platform. It was a successful expedition all the same, despite the colony's acting governor getting stuck high up on a winched seat during a visit. In terms of canopy access, the French have proved themselves to be excellent innovators, taking things further with the development of 'lighter-than-air platforms' - balloons and related equipment, to you and me. Francis Halle, from the Laboratoire de Botanique Tropicale at Montpellier University took to a balloon in the mid-1980s in order to approach the canopy from above. His work in French Guiana was inspired by the use in Gabon of a tethered helium balloon by Marcel and Annette Hladick. Halle went one further by using a small purpose-built airship-a cigar-shaped balloon with propellers to aid manoeuvrability. "We suddenly had a mobile system that could move around the treetops; there were no other means of doing this," says Mitchell.

D

From this, two balloon-dependent features have developed: the radeau or raft, and the luge or sledge. The raft is a 'floating' platform, employed by French academics Dany Cleyet-Marrel and Laurent Pyot and is essentially an island in the treetops. Made of kevlar mesh netting and edged with inflated neoprene tubes, it rests on top of the canopy, allowing sampling (mostly of plants and insects) to take place at the edges of the platform, and can stay in position for several days. The luge, on the other hand, is an inflated hexagon similar to a traditional balloon basket but with a hole in the bottom covered with Kevlar mesh. Such techniques aren't without their problems, however, "balloons can cover larger areas, especially for collection purposes, but they are extremely expensive [the raft alone cost 122,000 [euro] (86,000 [pounds sterling]) in 2001], not very effective because you can only reach the tops of the trees, and are highly dependent on the weather, " says Dr Wilfried Morawetz, director of systematic botany at the University of Leipzig. “Balloons can usually only be used in the early morning for two to four hours. Last time, we could only fly three times during the whole week." Given these factors, it comes as no surprise that operations involving these balloons numbered just six between 1986 and 2001.

E

The next major breakthrough came from Alan Smith of the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in Panama. Smith had the idea of using a static crane to get into the treetops. Un-tethered balloons may allow widely distributed sites to be sampled, but cranes allow scientists to study an area of at least a hectare from soil to canopy throughout the year, year after year. "Cranes beat any other access mode. They are cheap, reliable and fast. In two minutes I can reach any point in our forest, which is essential for comparative measurements across species," says Professor Christian Korner of the University of Basel. Korner is using a static crane in a unique carbon dioxide-enrichment experiment in Switzerland, in an attempt to discover how forests might respond to the global increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide (see Swiss canopy-crane carbon experiment, right). For reasons of convenience, cranes are generally situated close to cities or a research center. Leipzig University has a crane not far from the town, the location allowing scientists to study the effect of city pollutants on forests. In order to increase the amount of canopy a crane can access, some have been mounted on short rail tracks. In 1995, Dr Wilfried Morawetz was the first to use this technique, installing a crane on 150 meters of track in the Venezuelan rainforest. "In my opinion, cranes should be the core of canopy research in the future," he says.

F

It appears that the rest of the scientific community has now come around to Mitchell's way of thinking. "I think most scientists thought him mad to consider such a complex field station at first," says internationally respected 'canopist' Meg Lowman, the executive director of the Marie Selby Botanical Gardens. "However, we've all come to realize that a combination of methods, a long-term approach to ecological studies and a collaborative approach are the absolute best ways to advance canopy science. A permanent canopy field station would allow that to happen." With a dedicated group of canopy scientists working together and a wide range of tools available for them to get into the treetops, we're now finally on our way towards a true understanding of the least-known terrestrial habitat.

Q14 B Tropical rainforest comprises the richest of ecosystems, rivalled only by coral reefs for its diversity and complex interrelationships. And a great deal of that diversity lives up in the canopy – an estimated 70-90 per cent of life in the rainforest exists in the trees; one in ten of all vascular plants are canopy dwellers, and about 20-25 per cent of all invertebrates are thought to be unique to the canopy Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that rainforests are filled with a huge variety of life, and most of that life actually lives up in the treetops. Because so many unique plants and small creatures exist only there, studying the canopy is very important for science.
Answer Explanation:
The answer is paragraph B, which explains why it is scientifically important and meaningful to study the tree canopy.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because this paragraph provides scientific facts that show the importance ("scientific significance") of exploring the canopy. It explains that a massive portion of living things in the rainforest (between 70 and 90 percent) resides up in the treetops, and many plants and insects are found nowhere else. These facts highlight why committing to canopy study is essential for science.
Q15 C However, the first serious effort to reach and study the canopy didn't begin until 1929. The Oxford University Expedition to British Guiana, led by Major RWG Hingston, still ended up needing the help of locals when it came to building an observation platform Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that real scientific efforts to reach and study the treetops began in 1929 with a group from Oxford University that traveled to British Guiana.
Answer Explanation:
The answer C means that paragraph C describes the earliest scientific study or university trip aimed at reaching the tree canopy.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is C because paragraph C specifically mentions the earliest academic expedition to study the canopy. The text talks about "the first serious effort to reach and study the canopy" starting in 1929 with "The Oxford University Expedition to British Guiana". Here, "Oxford University Expedition" matches "academic research", and "the first serious effort to reach and study" corresponds to "the first academic research attempt mentioned to getting the top canopy".

Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Paragraph A gives a general definition and overview of what a forest canopy is, while also explaining the main difficulty humans face in studying and understanding forests.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is A because this paragraph opens with an overview defining the forest canopy as the connected tops of trees and noting how many species live there. It then directly highlights the challenge researchers face: "The problem with our understanding of forests is that nearly all the information we have has been gleaned from just two meters above the soil..." Key phrases to notice are the definition ("The forest canopy – the term given to...") matching the "overview idea," and the exact phrase "The problem with our understanding of forests" matching "the problem of understanding the forests."
Q17 F However, we've all come to realize that a combination of methods, a long-term approach to ecological studies and a collaborative approach are the absolute best ways to advance canopy science Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that scientists have now understood that studying the forest canopy requires looking at it over a long period of time and having different researchers work together.
Answer Explanation:
The answer F means that paragraph F is the section of the text that talks about realizing the importance of a long-term plan and working together with others.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is F because it mentions that scientists now understand the value of studying forests over a prolonged period and doing so collaboratively. In paragraph F, the text states, "we've all come to realize that a combination of methods, a long-term approach to ecological studies and a collaborative approach are the absolute best ways to advance canopy science." Here, "come to realize" matches "Recognition", "a long-term approach" matches "a long term effect", and "a collaborative approach" directly means "cooperation".
Q18 E Smith had the idea of using a static crane to get into the treetops. "Cranes beat any other access mode. They are cheap, reliable and fast Excerpt/Passage Explanation:

Answer Explanation:
The answer E means that Paragraph E is the section that talks about a new and best method found so far for reaching the tops of trees.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because this paragraph introduces the idea of using cranes to reach the forest canopy. The text states that "Smith had the idea of using a static crane to get into the treetops" and quotes Professor Christian Korner saying, "Cranes beat any other access mode. They are cheap, reliable and fast." Here, using cranes is the innovation, getting "into the treetops" matches "accessing to treetop", and beating "any other access mode" describes it as an ultimate solution compared to other methods.
Q19 locals The Oxford University Expedition to British Guiana, led by Major RWG Hingston, still ended up needing the help of locals when it came to building an observation platform Excerpt/Passage Explanation:

Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is "locals" because Paragraph C discusses an early expedition in 1929 that built an observation platform to reach the canopy. The text notes that this project required the "help of locals" to construct the platform. In the summary, the phrase "the help from the [locals] was imperative" directly matches this statement.
Q20 balloons In terms of canopy access, the French have proved themselves to be excellent innovators, taking things further with the development of 'lighter-than-air platforms' - balloons and related equipment, to you and me Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that French researchers were very clever at finding new ways to reach the tree canopy, especially by developing floating platforms made using balloons and related tools.

The answer "balloons" refers to large, gas-filled bags that float in the air, which scientists used to carry equipment up to the tops of trees.
Reason For Correctness:

Specifically, they created "lighter-than-air platforms," which the text directly describes as "balloons and related equipment". This directly matches the summary sentence mentioning innovations by the French who used this equipment to reach the canopy.
Q21 raft / rafts The raft is a 'floating' platform, employed by French academics Dany Cleyet-Marrel and Laurent Pyot and is essentially an island in the treetops Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that French researchers used a floating platform called a raft, which acts like an island resting at the very top of the trees.
Answer Explanation:
The answer "raft" (or "rafts") refers to a special floating structure or base placed on top of tree branches for scientists to stand on.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is confirmed by paragraph D. The summary describes a "'floating' platform of [input]" that serves "as an island in the treetops." The passage explicitly mentions that "The raft is a 'floating' platform" and describes it as "essentially an island in the treetops." Therefore, the word "raft" directly fits the gap.
Q22 static crane / crane / static cranes / cranes Smith had the idea of using a static crane to get into the treetops Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage explains that in Panama, a researcher had the idea of using a fixed crane machine to reach high up into the tops of trees.
Answer Explanation:
The answer means a tall lifting machine, called a crane or static crane, used by researchers to reach tree crowns.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is supported by Paragraph E. The summary mentions a breakthrough in Panama where scientists used a specific tool to reach the treetops, and notes that this tool would become the core of research. The text states that at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in Panama, someone came up with the idea of "using a static crane to get into the treetops." Later, Dr. Wilfried Morawetz says that "cranes should be the core of canopy research in the future." Therefore, "crane", "cranes", or "static crane" correctly completes the sentence.

His work in French Guiana was inspired by the use in Gabon of a tethered helium balloon by Marcel and Annette Hladick Excerpt/Passage Explanation:

Answer Explanation:
The answer choice D refers to Francis Halle.
Reason For Correctness:

The keyword "inspired" in the passage matches "inspired" in the prompt, and "by the use in Gabon of a tethered helium balloon by Marcel and Annette Hladick" matches "by the method used by other researchers."
Q24 B “Balloons can usually only be used in the early morning for two to four hours. Last time, we could only fly three times during the whole week." Excerpt/Passage Explanation:
The passage shows Dr Wilfried Morawetz explaining that scientists can only fly balloons at specific limited times (two to four hours early in the morning) and with low frequency (only three times in a whole week).
Answer Explanation:
The answer B corresponds to Dr Wilfried Morawetz.
Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is B because Dr Wilfried Morawetz explicitly stated the time and frequency limits of using balloons. In paragraph D, he explains that balloons can typically only be used for two to four hours in the early morning and mentions that during his recent experience, they could only fly three times across an entire week. The phrase 'limited frequency or time' matches his observation about using balloons for only 'two to four hours' and flying just 'three times during the whole week'.

Answer Explanation:
The answer means that Alan Smith (option F) was the researcher who first came up with a way to reach the treetops that was low-cost and dependable.

Another scientist then confirms that cranes are better than any other way of reaching the canopy because they are 'cheap, reliable and fast'. Here, 'had the idea' matches 'initiated', 'reliable' matches 'stable', and 'cranes' represent the 'access mode'.

Reason For Correctness:
The correct answer is E because paragraph E explains that Christian Korner conducts a unique scientific study using a static crane in Switzerland to see how forests react to rising carbon dioxide levels. This directly matches the description of a scientist who conducted a canopy-crane experiment for a specific research goal.

The word "initiated" means to start or be the first to do something, which matches the passage stating he was "the first to use this technique" of mounting a crane on tracks in the Venezuelan rainforest in 1995.
